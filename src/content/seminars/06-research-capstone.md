## The purpose is a contribution to knowledge

A credible graduate capstone should not end with a prettier shield or a certificate promising knowledge of tinctures. It should present a defensible original contribution: a revised interpretation, carefully edited source, documented case study, comparative legal account, historical catalogue or reproducible research tool.

A good question is bounded, capable of a convincing answer with available evidence and open to revision. 'What is French heraldry?' is a broad topic. 'Did the 1696 registration procedure record urban institutions in a defined region differently from the way earlier armorials presented them?' is a researchable question, provided that the relevant sources survive and that the researcher can define a valid sample.

The answer must depend on the sources. If the planned conclusion will not change regardless of what the documents show, the exercise is advocacy rather than research.

## Appropriate research genres

A **critical edition and translation** may transcribe a short source-language document, identify its witnesses, translate disputed terminology and explain the history of the text. A **social-historical study** might reconstruct how non-noble armory appeared in a particular town, examining seals, property records and monuments. An **institutional comparison** could study Flanders, the Netherlands and Germany without treating their different acts as a universal grant.

A **visual/material project** could compare an armorial design across stone, print and manuscript. A **computational project** might create a rights-reviewed data catalogue, showing exactly how historic spellings and identifications were reconciled. A **global comparative study** could examine a postcolonial state's use of European-derived heraldic forms and their interaction with local political and artistic traditions.

What unites these projects is not medium or methodology but discipline: original sources, explicit selection criteria, traceable claims, genuine engagement with alternatives and an accurate statement of uncertainty.

## The proposal as an intellectual contract

Prepare a proposal of 2,000–3,000 words organized as follows:

1. **Question and contribution.** State a clearly bounded question and which existing scholarly interpretation may be advanced, revised or tested.
2. **Literature review.** Identify pertinent research in the source languages, not just familiar English reference websites.
3. **Primary corpus.** Enumerate the manuscripts, records, statutes, seal impressions, monuments or images to be studied, with repository, identifier, dates, access and rights.
4. **Methods.** Explain transcription, translation, visual comparison, historical reasoning, legal interpretation or statistics as appropriate.
5. **Alternatives.** Explain what competing hypotheses predict and what evidence would count against the preferred view.
6. **Limits and ethics.** Address missing archives, nonrandom survival, translation uncertainty, personal data and cultural heritage.
7. **Planned contribution.** Specify whether the deliverable is an article, critical edition, catalogue, dataset or other reusable output.

A methodological statement is not a decorative section filled in after the research has been completed. It records choices that another scholar can inspect, criticize or reproduce.

## Working with rights and translation quality

When publishing a source transcription or translated passage, identify its historical age, edition, copyright status, translator, exact witness and editorial interventions. A scan of an old book may include newer annotations or an independently copyrighted photographic reproduction. A modern article that is freely readable may still be protected against wholesale republishing.

Our project publishes new English research syntheses and links to sources by default. A full critical translation should only be made public when the source is public domain or suitably licensed, or permission is obtained. Translation uncertainties and reviewer status should be shown alongside the text.

## Evaluation matrix

| Dimension | Strong work |
|---|---|
| Research question | A meaningful, bounded, testable historical or institutional proposition |
| Source competence | Original-language evidence, dated witnesses and verified locators |
| Translation | Preserves ambiguity, documents choices and never invents passages |
| Method | Reproducible comparisons and justified sampling |
| Argument | Distinguishes fact, hypothesis and counterargument |
| Ethics | Respects copyright, attribution, privacy and community perspectives |
| Contribution | Something another researcher can actually use |
| Review | Transparent response to criticism and willingness to revise |

No single impressive illustration compensates for bad source methodology. Equally, a modest but careful transcription of a neglected witness may become a valuable contribution.

## Final submission

Produce either a 5,000–10,000-word article with critical apparatus or a digital documentary edition with equivalent analytical depth. Include a concise abstract, source inventory, original-language extracts where permitted, carefully distinguished English translations or summaries, bibliography, methods, uncertainty statement and reproducibility instructions.

**Defence exercise:** Ask a skeptic to challenge your five most important claims. For each one, give the original source, relevant page or folio, and a reason for preferring your interpretation. If you cannot do so, revise the claim or reduce its certainty. That is not defeat; it is the process by which scholarship improves.

A graduate student might produce a genuine contribution by making an obscure sixteenth-century Portuguese manuscript witness accessible in English, comparing a Flemish statute with a Dutch decree, or constructing a well-documented Polish armorial dataset. Fame and novelty are not substitutes for correctness.

## Continuing the research

A capstone is not an end of inquiry. Identify a journal or scholarly society where a specialist could review the work, and maintain a list of outstanding documentary questions. When new evidence appears, incorporate it without concealing the earlier uncertainty.

The same standard applies to the Heraldry Project itself: a growing research collection, versioned editorial notes and an explicit record of unfinished work. Our target is the quality of scholarship, not a ribbon that says 'finished'.
