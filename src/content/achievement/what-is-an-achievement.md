## An object is not a legal conclusion

Suppose we encounter a stone carving of a shield held by two lions, surmounted by a helmet, and accompanied by a ribbon bearing three Latin words. What have we found? A carving, certainly. Perhaps a depiction of someone's coat of arms. Perhaps the sculptor's reconstruction of an earlier device. We have *not*, simply by seeing it, established that the lions were granted as hereditary supporters, that the motto was registered, or even that the carving shows the arms of the person whose name appears nearby. Those conclusions require separate evidence.

This course begins with the **achievement**: the assembled presentation of heraldic bearings and any accompanying devices. The shield carries the principal armorial design. A crest may stand above a helmet; mantling hangs from it; supporters may flank the shield; a motto may appear on a scroll. But these pieces are not a compulsory European checklist. The [Canadian Heraldic Authority's illustrated guide](https://gg.ca/en/heraldry/parts-coat-arms) calls the shield central and describes the surrounding elements as optional. Its useful general explanation is not a universal rule governing medieval France, seventeenth-century Germany, or present-day Scotland.

We need four categories. **Bearing** identifies a design or device recognized as part of someone's heraldry. **Presentation** identifies the way an artist assembles that device on a particular surface. **Evidence** identifies the records and objects by which we know the representation existed. **Entitlement** concerns the legal or customary authority under which someone may bear a particular element. A single object can tell us much about the first three, but it seldom proves the fourth unaided.

## The complete composition and its parts

The shield (*escutcheon*, German *Schild*, French *écu*) is the armorial nucleus. Its blazon is a concise verbal specification that may support many different valid artistic renderings. The [College of Arms explains](https://www.college-of-arms.gov.uk/About/11.htm) that the words of the grant, rather than the exact painting on the parchment, govern its English blazons. This is a powerful idea, but do not assume every historical jurisdiction used the same legal instrument or gave verbal descriptions the same status.

Above the shield lies what German heraldists call the *Oberwappen*, broadly the upper achievement. The helmet, crest (*Helmzier*) and mantling (*Helmdecken*) may be treated as a visually coherent unit. Elsewhere a crown, ecclesiastical hat, or another sign of office may occupy the space over the shield. To call all these objects 'crests' destroys distinctions needed for historical reading.

Outside this axis lie supporters, compartments, mottos, slogans, badges, insignia of orders, coronets, pavilions and other marks. They can be intimately associated with an achievement without all being hereditary in the same sense. A chain of an order is often a statement about a particular holder's honour, not an armorial possession automatically transmitted to every descendant.

## The problem of absence

A medieval seal may show only a shield. That does not establish that its owner had no crest. A printed armorial may include a helmet and crest but omit a motto because the engraver preferred a compact block of imagery. A modern logo derived from a city's arms might omit an official crown and therefore make a different public statement—or merely simplify a reproduction.

Absence in a particular witness should therefore be recorded as **not depicted**, not silently translated into **not possessed**. This distinction matters when comparing two copies of a manuscript, a seal impression and its matrix, or nineteenth-century printed armorials.

The reverse mistake is equally dangerous. A griffin standing beside a shield in a decorative border is **depicted**; that is not itself proof that the griffin is a hereditary supporter. Compare the historical placement of shield-holders in seal design with the specifically granted supporters described by the [College of Arms](https://www.college-of-arms.gov.uk/About/11.htm).

## A disciplined method of identification

To describe an achievement responsibly, work from the center outward. Transcribe the shield independently. Then name each other visible feature without assigning legal status: helmet, crest, foliage, two figures beside the shield, crown, ribbon, collar, background ornament. Only then ask whether contemporary wording identifies any of these as heraldic bearings.

Create a documentary record with columns for the element, image or description, named bearer, physical medium, date, source locator, and the kind of claim the evidence supports. Compare the result with a grant, register entry, historically dated roll, seal matrix, or another contemporary witness. If the original source is unavailable, say so. **Unverified** is a scholarly finding, not an embarrassment.

## Workshop: two witnesses, six claims

Imagine a family's 1620 seal displaying a helmet and two griffin-shaped figures beside a shield. A nineteenth-century armorial shows a visually similar shield but no griffins. A commercial website reproduces the seal and calls the figures 'granted supporters'. Make six short observations: two indisputably supported by the seal, two supported by the printed armorial, and two claims requiring additional proof. Write one sentence explaining why a grant, a seal and an artist's composition are different kinds of witness.
