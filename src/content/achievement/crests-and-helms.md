## A crest was once an object

A *crest* is not another word for the entire coat of arms. In the vocabulary of the [College of Arms](https://www.college-of-arms.gov.uk/Newsletter/018.pdf), it is the three-dimensional object above the helm. That apparently simple statement has consequences. Crests were made to be seen, and some survived as physical constructions. Their contours, attachments, colors and dimensions matter. We should not begin with a modern clip-art lion and imagine it was originally pasted onto a shield.

Material study forces us to distinguish the object from its representation. A crest described as a pair of horns may have been constructed from wood, leather, textile, parchment or other light material. An illuminated depiction can make the horns more elegant, rigid or geometrical than a surviving tournament object. The historian must identify the evidence for material, date and use rather than infer these from a modern blazon.

## The German Helmzier

Germanic armorial art often invites us to read shield and crest together. The paired horn motif commonly called *Büffelhörner* or *Büffelhörnern* is a conspicuous example. Horns may be striped, counterchanged, patterned, or charged to repeat the shield's visual identity. The exact details differ; not every paired object that resembles a horn should automatically be described as a buffalo horn without checking its source.

HEROLD's [Berlin principles of heraldic design](https://herold-verein.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Berliner-Erkl%C3%A4rung-%C3%BCber-heraldische-Gestaltungsgrunds%C3%A4tze.pdf), dated 24 April 2009, recommend that the *Helmzier* recall the shield and be visibly connected to a helmet resting on the shield's rim. The document even proposes approximate proportions for shield, helmet and crest of **3:2:3**. That is a modern association's design recommendation, not legislation retroactively governing every historical coat in German-speaking Europe.

The *Oberwappen* thus raises two different questions. How did historical artists compose it? And which contemporary schools of heraldic art regard a composition as proper? A pictorial answer to the first does not establish a binding rule for the second. A [German regional chapter](/traditions/germany/) examines this problem alongside burgher arms and the *Wappenbuch*.

## Scotland: the crest and the badge

The [Court of the Lord Lyon](https://www.courtofthelordlyon.scot/crests.htm) distinguishes a chief wearing their crest, an armiger wearing their own crest as a badge, and a clansperson displaying **the chief's crest within a strap and buckle bearing the chief's motto or slogan**. The last arrangement communicates allegiance. It does not transfer the chief's personal armorial property to every person of a related surname.

The Scottish conventions involving a plain circlet and eagle feathers are more differentiated than popular 'family crest' merchandising suggests. The Lyon Court describes three feathers for a clan chief, two for certain recognized heads of families, and one for an armiger's own crest badge. These are conventions tied to categories of bearers and require the [Lyon Court's full qualifications](https://www.courtofthelordlyon.scot/crests.htm), not a universal Scottish feather-counting rule. The site's [Scottish chapter](/traditions/scotland/) explains matriculation and the necessity of identifying the actual armiger.

## Spain is not a single crestless territory

The comparatively lesser prominence of crests in many Iberian armorial presentations is a useful observation, but it must not harden into the claim that Spain 'does not have crests'. The winged dragon *cimera* associated with the medieval Crown of Aragon is a decisive counterexample. The [Gran Enciclopèdia Catalana](https://www.enciclopedia.cat/gran-enciclopedia-catalana/cimera) describes the dragon crest as adopted by the Catalan–Aragonese kings from the time of Pere III and notes the preservation of a royal crest associated with King Martí, made of worked parchment and gilded plaster. The later bat motif found in Valencian civic iconography should be investigated as a history of reinterpretation, not projected back onto every depiction of the medieval dragon.

A crown on a Spanish shield is not thereby the same thing as a tournament crest. Spanish *timbre* can designate external insignia above the shield; *cimera* more specifically refers to the crest. Every translated description needs attention to context.

## Helmets, orientation and rank

Helmets also have histories. Tournament helms, barred or open helms, viewing angles and differences of metal may carry recognizable artistic or social associations. But a rule described in an English heraldic manual does not automatically apply to a late-medieval German burgher, a Catalan prince, or an early Italian seal. The [HEROLD principles](https://herold-verein.de/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Berliner-Erkl%C3%A4rung-%C3%BCber-heraldische-Gestaltungsgrunds%C3%A4tze.pdf) explicitly warn that the modern use of a barred helmet to imply noble descent may be pretentious. This is itself an instructive modern judgment about a complex older practice.

Choose one horned German achievement from a dated *Wappenbuch*, one Scottish crest badge, and one representation of Aragon's winged dragon. For each, record the medium, evidence for an actual crest, relationship to the shield, and whether the image communicates the identity of its owner or allegiance to someone else.
