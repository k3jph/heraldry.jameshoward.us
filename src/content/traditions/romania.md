## This is a blazon with the force of a statute

An armorial website can teach that gold is Or and red is Gules, then demonstrate a digital hatching pattern and move on. Romania offers a more serious—and much more interesting—question: what happens when these conventions are *written into national law*?

Romania's [Law 102 of 21 September 1992](https://legislatie.just.ro/Public/DetaliiDocument/2381) defines the state coat of arms and state seal. It does so using formal visual language. The central shield is borne by an eagle, and that eagle carries a smaller shield partitioned into historical territorial components. The law names the charges and tinctures in text, and an annex gives an official graphic model.

Article 2 states explicitly how the coat of arms is to be reproduced without colour. It prescribes the familiar heraldic hatching convention: dots for gold, blank surface for silver, vertical lines for red, horizontal lines for blue, and superimposed vertical and horizontal lines for black. Even the relative thickness of some lines in simplified renderings is addressed.

This is very different from simply saying that artists in a particular country 'usually follow' the *rule of tincture*. An English manual may present hatching as an artistic convention. A Romanian statute directs its use for reproducing a defined state emblem. The same technique can occupy quite different places in law, education and craft across jurisdictions.

## A political geography inside the inner shield

The Romanian national arms make no sense as a generic invented medieval family's shield. Their smaller, quartered shield contains symbols associated with historical territories: Wallachia, Moldavia, Banat and Oltenia, Transylvania and related regions, and the Black Sea lands. The outer eagle carries a cross, sword and mace. The meaning of the achievement is political and historical, not a hereditary identity shared by all people with a Romanian surname.

Studying the design requires identifying the sources of the historical regional symbols. A government law naming Wallachia or Moldavia is excellent evidence of which references the modern state incorporated. It is not automatically proof that the named historical regions used those exact quarterings throughout a thousand years.

A student should separate three layers: an earlier regional emblem, the date and form in which it entered the modern national achievement, and the official post-1992 language explaining it. Changes in the national graphic may be politically significant even where some underlying regional symbolism is presented as continuous.

The internal shield illustrates another problem in translation: *sfertuit* means quartered, but quartering in a national emblem cannot be treated as the private heraldic claim of an heiress's descendants. Both compositions assemble different devices. The rules governing what the assembly **means** depend on the bearer and context.

## A crown returned in 2016

Romania later altered its statutory coat of arms. [Law 146 of 12 July 2016](https://legislatie.just.ro/Public/DetaliiDocumentAfis/180207) amended the 1992 text and specified a **crowned** golden eagle, replacing the earlier form lacking the crown. The law addressed transitional use of official symbols and called for governmental replacement of existing arms and seals by a deadline at the end of **2018**; it also permitted coins and banknotes with older designs to continue in circulation.

This is an unusually useful case of controlled iconographic change. Does the presence of a crown make a modern state emblem 'monarchical'? Such a sweeping statement would be more ideological than evidentiary. A crown can carry a national historical association in a republican symbol without transferring the constitutional powers of an abolished monarch. The historian needs the parliamentary record of the amendment, the political context, and any explanatory debate before confidently identifying the precise intended symbolism.

One date cannot represent the entire chain. The state arms were legislated in 1992, modified in 2016, and transitioned administratively thereafter. A collector may find different designs on printed documents or currency issued at different times. The coexistence of these physical versions need not mean an institution ignored the law; transitional provisions and issue dates must be examined.

## The hatching problem in law and software

Suppose our digital Atelier renders the Romanian eagle's blue field using horizontal lines. Is it an authorized reproduction? Not necessarily. The original state drawing includes much more than a tincture-to-pattern mapping: specific compositions, proportions, charges and graphical standards can matter. A toy SVG with the correct hatch for blue is not therefore the official national coat of arms.

Conversely, a photographic representation in ordinary colour may be legal even when it does not use hatching, since the law expressly permits colour reproduction. The specific legal question is the authorized form *in a given medium*, not whether every rendering uses one mandatory aesthetic.

A serious digital system should retain two sets of metadata: the armorial identity (charges, fields, blazon and heraldic relationships) and the authorized presentation (official model, medium-specific hatching, reproduction rules, historic revisions and source date). This is a richer data model than a single SVG filename.

For graduate software research, Romania is an excellent test case because the law itself provides a formal description and a conversion mapping. One can write automated tests comparing a rendered black-and-white image with legally stated patterns, but human review remains necessary to verify the overall illustration.

## A short Romanian–English concordance

| Romanian | English working equivalent | Analytical note |
|---|---|---|
| *stemă* | coat of arms | Can mean the state emblem or another public coat |
| *stema ţării* | coat of arms of the country | Official national context |
| *sigiliul statului* | state seal | Related but distinct from the armorial achievement |
| *scutul mare* | large outer shield | Distinguished from the inner shield |
| *scutul mic sfertuit* | smaller quartered shield | Political territorial assembly here |
| *acvilă încoronată* | crowned eagle | Statutory form after the 2016 change |
| *semne convenţionale* | conventional signs | Used to encode tinctures monochromatically |
| *culori / cromatică* | colours / chromatic values | Context of reproducing a legal emblem |
