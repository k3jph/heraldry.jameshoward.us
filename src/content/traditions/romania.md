## Article 10 and the approval of local arms

Romania's framework does more than prescribe hatching of the national emblem. **Article 10 of Law No. 102/1992** provides for the arms of counties, municipalities, towns and communes through a specific approval mechanism. A local proposal and design are not themselves national legislation; the formal approval occurs by **government decision** on the proper local-administrative route, with the competent specialist heraldic review described in implementing practice.

The category of bearer matters. The national golden eagle is governed by the statute and its annex, while a county device has its own record and official approval. Even a shared motif such as the aurochs head cannot substitute for tracing the particular act that established the local emblem. The same source may illustrate two very different public authority relationships.

## This is a blazon with the force of a statute

A black-and-white reproduction of Romania’s state arms has to communicate tinctures that the ink cannot show directly. The familiar heraldic solution is to substitute dots and lines for colours. Here, however, the choice of patterns also appears in national legislation. We can investigate how a visual convention becomes a prescribed method for reproducing one official emblem, and what that prescription leaves an artist or software system still to resolve.

Romania's [Law 102 of 21 September 1992](https://legislatie.just.ro/Public/DetaliiDocument/2381) defines the state coat of arms and state seal. It does so using formal visual language. On the large blue shield stands a golden eagle, and the eagle bears a smaller quartered shield on its breast representing historical territories. The law names the charges and tinctures in text, and an annex gives an official graphic model.

Article 2 states explicitly how the coat of arms is to be reproduced without colour. It prescribes the familiar heraldic hatching convention: dots for gold, blank surface for silver, vertical lines for red, horizontal lines for blue, and superimposed vertical and horizontal lines for black. Even the relative thickness of some lines in simplified renderings is addressed.

This is very different from simply saying that artists in a particular country 'usually follow' *heraldic hatching conventions*. An English manual may present hatching as an artistic convention. A Romanian statute directs its use for reproducing a defined state emblem. The same technique can occupy quite different places in law, education and craft across jurisdictions.

## A political geography inside the inner shield

The Romanian national arms make no sense as a generic invented medieval family's shield. Their smaller, quartered shield contains symbols associated with historical territories: Wallachia, Moldavia, Banat and Oltenia, Transylvania and related regions, and the Black Sea lands. The outer eagle carries a cross, sword and mace. The meaning of the achievement is political and historical, not a hereditary identity shared by all people with a Romanian surname.

Studying the design requires identifying the sources of the historical regional symbols. A government law naming Wallachia or Moldavia is excellent evidence of which references the modern state incorporated. It is not automatically proof that the named historical regions used those exact quarterings throughout a thousand years.

A student should separate three layers: an earlier regional emblem, the date and form in which it entered the modern national achievement, and the official post-1992 language explaining it. Changes in the national graphic may be politically significant even where some underlying regional symbolism is presented as continuous.

The internal shield illustrates another problem in translation: *sfertuit* means quartered, but quartering in a national emblem cannot be treated as the private heraldic claim of an heiress's descendants. Both compositions assemble different devices. The rules governing what the assembly **means** depend on the bearer and context.

## A crown returned in 2016

Romania later altered its statutory coat of arms. [Law 146 of 12 July 2016](https://legislatie.just.ro/Public/DetaliiDocumentAfis/180207) amended the 1992 text and specified a **crowned** golden eagle, replacing the earlier form lacking the crown. The law addressed transitional use of official symbols and called for governmental replacement of existing arms and seals by a deadline at the end of **2018**; it also permitted coins and banknotes with older designs to continue in circulation.

This is an unusually useful case of controlled iconographic change. Does the presence of a crown make a modern state emblem 'monarchical'? Such a sweeping statement would be more ideological than evidentiary. A crown can carry a national historical association in a republican symbol without transferring the constitutional powers of an abolished monarch. The historian needs the parliamentary record of the amendment, the political context, and any explanatory debate before confidently identifying the precise intended symbolism.

One date cannot represent the entire chain. The state arms were legislated in 1992, modified in 2016, and transitioned administratively thereafter. A collector may find different designs on printed documents or currency issued at different times. The coexistence of these physical versions need not mean an institution ignored the law; transitional provisions and issue dates must be examined.

## The hatching problem in law and software

Suppose our digital Atelier renders the Romanian eagle's blue field using horizontal lines. Is it an authorized reproduction? Not necessarily. The original state drawing includes much more than a tincture-to-pattern mapping: specific compositions, proportions, charges and graphical standards can matter. A toy SVG with the correct hatch for blue is not therefore the official national coat of arms.

Conversely, a photographic representation in ordinary colour may be legal even when it does not use hatching, since the law expressly permits colour reproduction. The specific legal question is the authorized form *in a given medium*, not whether every rendering uses one mandatory aesthetic.

A serious digital system should retain two sets of metadata: the armorial identity (charges, fields, blazon and heraldic relationships) and the authorized presentation (official model, medium-specific hatching, reproduction rules, historic revisions and source date). This is a richer data model than a single SVG filename.

For graduate software research, Romania is an excellent test case because the law itself provides a formal description and a conversion mapping. One can write automated tests comparing a rendered black-and-white image with legally stated patterns, but human review remains necessary to verify the overall illustration.

## Cuza's objects show a union being represented

The National History Museum's Cuza-era objects give the territorial composition a nineteenth-century material history. Its account distinguishes a transitional form with paired Wallachian and Moldavian shields, a later united shield and a more elaborate princely-court composition of 1864. Silver tableware and porcelain carry these different forms.[^ro-cuza-objects] The reign does not supply a single unchanging image simply because its ruler remained the same person.

In the paired form, the two shields remain visually distinct while an overarching arrangement joins them. A subsequent divided shield brings the signs into one container. That formal change makes a political relationship legible: two identifiable traditions can be coordinated, then represented as parts of a more unified whole. It is a comparison to test against the dated acts and objects, rather than an inference to make from any two adjacent shields.

The princely-court version raises a further question about bearer. Is the design identifying the polity, the ruler or the court's use of the polity's signs? A piece of porcelain used in a princely setting can participate in more than one of those relationships. Its provenance, commissioner and recorded use help decide. The national history of the emblem and the biography of the object should be allowed to intersect without becoming identical.

Silver and porcelain also impose different artistic possibilities. Engraving may rely on outlines and hatching; painted porcelain can retain tinctures and fine ornament. Differences between the objects should be assessed against both the prescribed form and the material. A missing colour on an engraved plate is not proof that the historical coat lacked a tincture.

This group is especially useful for teaching marshalling outside hereditary-family claims. The assembly of territorial signs records a political union. It does not require an heiress whose descendants have acquired the quarters under the English model. The geometry may be familiar while the institutional mechanism is different.

## The Steel Crown is a physical object with a particular story

The museum records the Steel Crown made for Carol I in 1881 from a captured gun associated with the independence war. It also identifies Ferdinand's later use of the crown at the 1922 coronation.[^ro-steel-crown] Here the material itself participates in an account of sovereignty and military achievement.

Do not lose that specificity by treating the crown as merely a generic rank sign. Its physical steel, manufacture and ceremonial use give it an object history. A drawing of a crown in the national arms is a representation in a different medium. The physical object can inform that representation without becoming identical to the pixels, engraving or relief that depict it.

Likewise, steel as an actual material does not create a new heraldic tincture that must be added to every coat showing the crown. The formal armorial model determines how its representation is rendered. Materials, heraldic colours and symbolic interpretations need related but separate descriptions. A steel crown can appear in an artwork whose artist must choose how to convey highlights and structure within an approved palette.

The 2016 restoration of the crowned eagle belongs in this longer history of remembered regalia, but the amending law and its explanatory record remain the evidence for the modern act. A crown associated with kingship can be retained by a republic as a sign of national history. To explain the precise parliamentary intention, consult the bill and debate rather than assuming the physical crown tells the entire story on the law's behalf.

For an exhibition caption, identify manufacture and ceremonial dates separately. “1881 crown used in 1922” does not mean it was made twice, and a photograph taken much later dates only the photograph. These distinctions allow a visitor to follow continuity through a surviving object instead of encountering an undifferentiated label reading “royal crown.”

## Coins make sovereignty visible in everyday exchange

The National Bank's monetary history describes diplomatic objections to the 1868 gold portrait coin and an altered title on a subsequent 1870 issue. The disputed representation concerned the ruler and the scope of his title in the setting of Ottoman suzerainty and neighbouring political interests.[^ro-money-history] Coin inscriptions were doing political work alongside the image.

A coin therefore needs a reading of both faces, its legend, denomination and issue date. The coat of arms can identify the state while a portrait identifies the ruler; the legend can specify the relationship being claimed. Omitting the wording can conceal why two similar portrait types had different diplomatic receptions. Heraldry is one part of the monetary argument rather than the whole of it.

Coin production also creates a different scale of public contact from a ceremonial charter. A small device circulates through ordinary exchange and encounters people who may never see the official original. Repetition can make a political emblem familiar while reducing fine details. A numismatic comparison asks which identifying features survive reduction and which require the legend to complete the message.

The 2016 law's treatment of older coins and notes, discussed earlier, makes the modern continuation equally concrete. A person could legally encounter an uncrowned eagle on older currency after the new official model had taken effect. The issue date and transitional provision explain the coexistence. A photograph of a wallet's contents is not evidence that the state has adopted two rival constitutions.

For research, compare actual issued examples with the official monetary record. A commemorative design, trial strike and circulation coin can serve different purposes even when they carry related arms. Record which category applies before using a specimen to argue what the population ordinarily saw.

## Brașov: a crown can be a pun inside another crown

Brașov's 2024 approval supplies an exceptionally clear civic case. The shield places a gold crown on blue, with a silver rooted trunk of thirteen tips descending from it. A silver mural crown with seven towers surmounts the shield. The official explanation identifies the internal crown as canting on the city's names Corona and Kronstadt, and calls the thirteen-community account of the roots a legend.[^ro-brasov-2024]

The two crowns do different jobs. The crown inside the shield belongs to the city's identifying composition and speaks through its name. The mural crown outside supplies the prescribed public-rank reference. Removing the outer crown does not remove the canting device; replacing the inner crown with a mural one would change the arms. A caption must identify which crown it means.

The rooted trunk also needs an exact description. Thirteen tips are a prescribed feature in this approval. They are not thirteen independent trees or a generic bush beneath a crown. Counting the tips against the annexed drawing provides a modest but meaningful test of accurate reproduction. A stylized version that reduces them to three might retain a loose memory of the emblem while departing from the defined civic model.

The act's use of “legend” is valuable. It can authorize the present image and state a familiar explanation without pretending the story is a contemporary medieval record. A legally prescribed count does not prove the historical truth of the narrative attached to that count. The design and the history of its interpretation can both be studied with the act's qualification intact.

Distinguish the approved 2024 decision from municipal proposal documents whose annex headings leave a decision number blank. A proposal is useful evidence for development and consultation, but it does not carry the same status as the adopted government act. Search results often place the two side by side. The heading, issuer and enactment details decide which one supports a claim of approval.

## Aurochs, eagles and territorial continuities

Romania's composite national coat preserves recognizable regional signs while arranging them for a modern national bearer. The Moldavian aurochs head is one such sign; the Wallachian cross-bearing eagle is another. Their place in today's inner shield needs to be compared with earlier seals and objects rather than treated as a complete medieval national composition.[^ro-national-arms]

An aurochs is not interchangeable with a bison simply because both are large bovines. In a formal identification retain the name supplied by the relevant description and compare the conventional head form. Zoological realism is not the only criterion, but careless substitution can erase a local armorial distinction. The same applies to accompanying stars, crescents or roses in particular historic witnesses.

The geographical name Moldavia also needs chronological care. A historic principality and the present Republic of Moldova are not coextensive administrative bearers. A charge associated with the former can appear in more than one later state tradition. Its presence does not by itself prove a contemporary territorial claim, and its absence from one object's caption does not settle ownership of the historical reference.

Transylvania's field belongs in a similar investigation through its constituent signs and the date of their combined use. Identify each charge and its arrangement from a dated description, then ask how the modern national composition selected and positioned them. Present legislation tells us what the modern state represents; medieval and early modern witnesses are needed to establish the earlier uses.

The result is a history of assembly, not a hunt for a single moment when every part of the present coat appeared at once. Cuza's objects, nineteenth-century coinage, royal regalia and republican legislation provide several stages. Their differences make the history richer than a supposedly timeless national shield.

## Romanian descriptions connect drawing with legal classification

Terms such as *timbrat* concern what surmounts the shield, while *coroană murală* identifies the mural crown. A *stemă vorbitoare* is canting arms: a visual play on a name, as Brașov's approval explains. These phrases are operations and relationships, not isolated decorative labels. A working translation should show where the element goes and what kind of sign it is.

Keep interpretive sections separate from the formal description when a decree supplies both. The description determines the visible composition; the significance section explains the approved account of its parts. The latter may include a legend, an association with a place name or a statement about municipal status. Their legal proximity does not make them identical genres of historical evidence.

For monochrome work, apply the law's tincture conventions only after identifying which areas they encode. A hatch pattern fills an already identified red or blue region. It cannot decide whether an uncertain patch belongs to the field, a bird's wing or a separate charge. Geometry and identification come before colour substitution.

## Graduate investigation: follow public identity through objects

Prepare a sequence comprising a Cuza-era armorial object, an early national coin, the Steel Crown and a post-2016 official eagle model. For each, record the object date, represented bearer, inscription if present and source of the identification. Explain the relationship between physical regalia and their graphic representation without treating them as one object.

Then add Brașov's 2024 decree as a civic comparison. Identify the two crowns, the canting relationship and the legend attached to the roots. Compare the draft annex with the adopted act if both are available, noting changes rather than assuming approval left every proposal detail untouched.

The final essay should explain how armorial composition, inscriptions and material choices communicate public identity at different scales. Include at least one example where a current law supplies a definite form and one where a historical interpretation remains qualified. Romania's evidence supports both precise reproduction and an account of political change; the task is to make those strengths work together.
