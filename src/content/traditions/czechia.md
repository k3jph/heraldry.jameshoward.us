## Municipal arms and a specific parliamentary officer

**Section 34a of Act No. 128/2000 Sb., on municipalities**, specifies the approval mechanism. The **Speaker of the Chamber of Deputies** may grant or change a municipal coat of arms or flag on a municipality's application. The operative [Czech legislation in the e-Sbírka](https://e-sbirka.gov.cz/sb/2000/128) is the appropriate authority, while the parliamentary [REKOS register](https://www.rekos.psp.cz/) records the results. The application, Speaker's act and database record are distinguishable documentary events.

Czechoslovakia's republic abolished noble titles and the inherited legal privileges associated with the previous state in **1918**. A historical armorial patent nevertheless remains an archival source rather than disappearing with the nobiliary status it once reflected. Modern Czech municipal grants have their own statutory basis; they must not be explained as a continuation of the Habsburg personal nobility system. The separate Slovak municipal and register regime is covered in the [Slovakia study](/traditions/slovakia/), not a missing territory awaiting a Czech administrator.

## The ordinary municipal coat is historically interesting

A municipal coat identifies a public body whose elected officers can change while the institution continues. That makes the [Czech Parliament's REKOS register](https://www.rekos.psp.cz/) useful for studying both visual identity and administration. Its records connect coats and flags to named municipalities and, where applicable, dated authorizations. We can follow a design into the procedure that established its official use, then ask how that act relates to older local imagery. The picture becomes an entry into an institutional history.

REKOS documents applications and records information about municipal symbols of the Czech Republic. The parliamentary site explains that the right to use municipal emblems is granted by the Speaker of the Chamber of Deputies, following the relevant committee process. Specialist heraldic and vexillological expertise supports that process.

The distinction is significant because it has no simple English-family-heraldry equivalent. The bearer of a modern municipal coat is a civic authority. It can persist across changes in elected officers, and its administrative record concerns the official identity of a legal public body.

## Distinguish a historical symbol from a fresh grant

A municipality may possess a historically established emblem, a newly designed one, or one modified in the course of local reorganization. A state database can contain records of these different histories, but a search result alone does not establish which category a particular record occupies.

The REKOS site even explains that historic symbols and their verbal descriptions can be added to the catalogue. This creates a familiar historical-data problem: a date of **database entry** might be centuries later than the design's first attested use, and a modern *blason* may be an editorial restatement of an older visual witness. Treating those dates as equivalent would produce false chronologies of Czech armory.

When studying an individual municipality, make a table with at least the first known local witness, the current registration or decision, the verbal blazon, the associated artwork and the date on which each item was produced or published. Source chronology matters more than the size of the illustration.

## The politics of public-symbol design

Modern municipal heraldry can combine references to local landscape, agriculture, architecture, saints, historic lords and national history. An analysis should ask which memory the emblem elevates, whether the imagery was inherited or reinvented, and whether its formal colours and charges connect to a documented predecessor.

It is not enough to say 'the lion signifies bravery'. That is a general symbolic association, often a weak historical explanation. One needs evidence from the municipality's decision, the formal explanatory memorandum, or earlier iconography. A symbol adopted in the 1990s may be presenting modern political identity through a device deliberately made to look ancient.

Comparative scholarship can study how the Czech process differs from Dutch civic grants and Swedish archive-based heraldry. Each produces documentary records but has a distinct decision maker. There is no value in obscuring those legal differences with a generalized label like 'heraldic authority'.

## Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia occupy different fields

The greater Czech arms are a compact lesson in territorial composition. Bohemia's silver, double-tailed lion on red occupies the first and fourth quarters; Moravia's silver-and-red checkered eagle occupies the second blue quarter; Silesia's black eagle occupies the third gold quarter. The latter carries a silver crescent with trefoil ends and a central cross. These details distinguish two eagles that would collapse into a single category in a casual description. The lesser arms retain the Bohemian lion without the other quarters.[^cz-state-symbols]

Read this shield from the bearer's position. The first quarter is at the viewer's upper left, the second at the upper right, the third at the lower left and the fourth at the lower right. The repeated lion does not announce a second Bohemia. The presidential explanation assigns the fourth field to the republic as a whole. A composition can give an existing territorial sign an additional representative function through its placement. Counting pictures and counting represented political units are therefore different operations.

The lion's two tails are part of its identity, not an artist's flourish. Nor is the checkering of the Moravian eagle equivalent to a checkered field behind an ordinary eagle. In one arrangement the charge itself bears the alternating colours; in the other the background does. This is exactly the distinction a verbal description must preserve. A silhouette that omits internal detail may be adequate for identifying an eagle in conversation and inadequate for identifying these arms.

A useful drawing exercise begins with the lesser arms and then constructs the greater shield. Preserve the lion's crown, armament and two tails at the reduced scale. Add the two different eagles without simplifying away the checkering or crescent. When the shield becomes small enough that those features disappear, you have found a reproduction limit. Enlarging the image or selecting an authorized lesser form solves a different problem from redesigning the national arms.

## The Moravian eagle has a contested colour history

Both silver-and-red and gold-and-red checkered Moravian eagles occur in historical discussion. Their coexistence cannot be explained by declaring one illustrator careless. A historical article by **PhDr. Ivan Štarha**, archived on the Czech Ministry of the Interior's website, examines Frederick III's 1462 privilege, later estate usage and a 1915 authorization concerning Moravian self-government. **Štarha argues that the 1462 privilege was invalid from the outset**, rather than merely treating its later effectiveness as a vaguely contested matter. His argument is attributed scholarship hosted by the ministry, not an anonymous ministry decision or an unqualified judicial holding.[^cz-moravian-colours]

The distinction between an estate body and a territory is doing real work here. An institution representing a land can have its own authorized practices without every such practice redefining the land's position in the larger state's arms. Likewise, evidence that members wore particular colours establishes something about their uniform. It does not, without another step, establish the formal tinctures of every eagle representing Moravia.

For a historical image, record the colour actually present, the object's date and the identity it claims to represent. Then investigate which authorization or custom applies. Do not recolour a red-and-gold eagle silver merely because the present Czech state arms require silver. Do not replace the present silver checkering with gold merely because an older privilege is more exciting. Historical variation and present prescription are both intelligible when their dates and bearers remain attached.

The controversy also illustrates why heraldry belongs in political history. Colour changes can encode arguments about regional autonomy, a monarch's authority and institutional continuity. Those arguments are neither visible in a colour swatch alone nor resolved by the rule of tincture. The same visual alteration can be an aesthetic decision in one setting and a constitutional claim in another. The document identifies which setting we are in.

## Brno: a painted privilege can get the colours wrong

Brno supplies an unusually useful test of the assumption that a splendid official painting must be the last word. The city's historical account traces its old divided arms to a seal of 1315 and describes Ferdinand III's augmentation in 1646 following the city's resistance to the Swedish siege. It reports that the artist reversed the white and red bands in the painted privilege despite the written description, and that the city subsequently used the reversed picture. Twentieth-century decisions moved between the older and augmented forms.[^cz-brno-history]

An armorial charter can therefore contain two witnesses that disagree: its verbal prescription and its illustration. Calling the charter authentic does not make that disagreement disappear. A careful edition transcribes the text, reproduces or describes the picture and explains their relationship. If later official use followed the picture, that usage also becomes evidence. The researcher needs a chronology of reception, rather than a single correction applied to the entire record.

The modern Brno statute describes four horizontal areas, silver, red, silver and red from the top, in the proportions 1:2:2:2. It accepts several treatments of outlines and colour replacement, including monochrome reproduction with vertical hatching for red. It expressly treats unblazoned outlines as a matter of drawing rather than a change of heraldic content.[^cz-brno-statute] This is a useful answer to the worry that every black boundary in a digital file must be another charge.

The unequal upper band also matters. Four equal bands would preserve the alternating sequence but lose the prescribed construction. A blazon, a municipal technical standard and a historical painting can constrain different aspects of a rendering. In an ordinary historical sketch, some proportions may vary; in an official application governed by the statute, a construction diagram may impose additional precision. Read the instruction that applies to the task before announcing artistic freedom.

Brno's municipal account labels the explanation of the white bands as two local rivers a legend of uncertain origin. That restraint is worth retaining. A geographical interpretation can become meaningful to later residents without proving why the medieval designer selected the arrangement. The rivers story is evidence of reception if its circulation can be dated. It needs a separate source to become evidence of original intention.

## A regional shield is a negotiated summary

The Moravian-Silesian Region's REKOS entry gives a decision dated 13 November 2002 and separate descriptions for its arms and flag. The arms combine the Silesian and Moravian eagles with an Ostrava horse and rose and a further divided field. The flag description specifies its own arrangement and a 2:3 proportion.[^cz-moravian-silesian-region] This provides an actual modern case beside the older state and municipal material.

A regional device has to make several kinds of identity coexist. Historical territories supply one vocabulary; a prominent city supplies another; the modern administrative bearer supplies the reason for combining them. A new regional shield can consequently include very old charges without being an unchanged medieval coat. Dating the earliest eagle and dating the adoption of the present composition answer different questions.

Compare the arms and flag quarter by quarter. Translate shield directions into hoist and fly positions only after you have established which way the flag is being viewed. Inspect how the divided final quarter becomes a set of stripes and a half-eagle in the flag. A flag can translate armorial content into a rectangular surface while adding explicit proportions. It is not necessarily a shield removed from its outline and stretched until it fits.

REKOS makes this process researchable because the verbal description accompanies the decision record. Preserve both. A screenshot can show the design but omit the decision date, and a search result can expose the date but omit the full text. When citing the record, name the bearer and identify the kind of symbol. A regional flag grant and a municipal arms grant are not interchangeable entries simply because the database stores both.

## Czech terminology preserves operations

The vocabulary is most useful when attached to an action. *Čtvrcený* tells you to quarter a shield. *Polcený* divides it vertically; a *polovina* is a half, whose relation to the whole must be specified. *Zbroj* in the descriptions of beasts concerns their heraldic armament, such as claws and beak, rather than an instruction to put an eagle into a suit of plate armour. The surrounding sentence determines the appropriate English rendering.

Likewise, *orlice* is the heraldic eagle term in these descriptions. Translating it with biological precision does not tell you its displayed attitude, colour or attributes. Those belong to the rest of the description. Build a glossary of whole phrases from the state and regional records, with an accompanying annotated sketch. A collection of detached word equivalents is a start, but the phrase-to-drawing correspondence demonstrates comprehension.

Brno's *šrafování* is hatching: a monochrome convention that encodes tincture. The vertical lines mean red within that convention; they do not describe literal stripes that should be preserved when the arms return to colour. This is a recurring issue in printed armorials. The reader who treats every line as a charge will manufacture a remarkable number of new coats.

For records in older German or Latin, retain the historical place name alongside the modern Czech name and the repository identifier. The multilingual record of these lands reflects changes in administration and readership. Translating a place name helps locate the object today. It does not determine which historical jurisdiction issued its charter.

## Research laboratory

Use the [REKOS searchable registry](https://rekos.psp.cz/vyhledani-symbolu) to locate three municipalities whose symbols were entered or granted at different dates. Capture both the Czech description and its English translation. Distinguish the date of the original historic depiction, any formal decision, and the date the record appears online.

**Graduate assignment:** Build a reproducible comparison of ten municipal symbols, testing whether an apparent preference for castles or agricultural devices is associated with historical urban status. Specify source completeness and what could falsify the proposed pattern. Do not assume the database's current membership is a complete enumeration of all historical Czech coats.

## Graduate investigation: resolve the Brno disagreement

Prepare a critical comparison of the 1646 augmentation as described by the city, the older divided coat and the modern statute. Begin with a three-column transcription table: verbal prescription, painted arrangement, and later documented use. Identify which cells can be filled from the municipal summary and which require the original charter, a seal or a dated municipal decision. Leave the unavailable cells open and list the document needed to fill each one.

Then make two annotated diagrams of the bands: one for the modern 1:2:2:2 construction and one for the reversed historical arrangement as reported by the municipal account. Label each diagram with its evidential status. An illustration reconstructed from a summary is a reconstruction, even if the summary comes from the city. It should not acquire the appearance of a facsimile through a persuasive old-fashioned font.

Finally, write a short account explaining how an error could become established official usage. The strongest answer considers the authority of the charter, the circulation of its picture and later municipal decisions. It also explains why the present statute can prescribe a clear model without making every earlier object conform to it. Finish by comparing that process with the Moravian colour controversy: in one case a reported painter's reversal; in the other, competing claims about an augmentation and the body entitled to use it. Different mechanisms can produce superficially similar colour variation.
