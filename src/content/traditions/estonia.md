## Three lions and two public forms

Estonia’s Government Office distinguishes large and small state arms. Both carry three blue lions on gold, while the larger form adds oak branches.[^ee-state] A small difference in the surrounding composition identifies a real distinction in the public-symbol system. It should not be dismissed as interchangeable decoration.

This chapter also needs to distinguish national identity from the historical knighthood’s family-armorial collection. The objects now preserved and displayed by public institutions can belong to different historical bearers. Their present custody does not make every noble-family plaque an emblem of the republic.

The working model separates design, bearer, physical witness and custodial institution. A state seal and a noble plaque may now be available through the same broad governmental setting while answering entirely different historical questions. Keeping those fields separate lets the collection explain Estonia’s institutional transformations rather than flattening them.

## A seal returns without its original tools

The Government Office’s account follows the state seal established in 1925, the interruption under occupation, a renewed seal in 1993 and recovery of earlier manufacturing tools in 2008.[^ee-seal] The state design, the physical tools and the institution’s use consequently have related but distinct histories.

A later seal can represent renewed use of the state device even while the earlier dies remain missing. Recovering the old tools changes the material history and research possibilities; it does not mean the state emblem had no intervening existence. Conversely, possession of the old dies would not by itself prove uninterrupted official use through every period.

This gives students a concrete way to analyze continuity. Ask whether the continuity concerns the identifying design, the legal institution, the custody of a tool or actual documentary practice. The answer may differ for each. A single sentence declaring that “the seal survived” is inadequate unless it tells us which survival is being asserted.

## Manufacture, impression, and authentication

A die is a tool in the production of a seal or sealing mechanism. An impression is a resulting mark on a particular document or material. The Government Office’s account identifies surviving tools and the seal’s diplomatic uses.[^ee-seal] Catalogue these separately rather than calling every item a seal with the same meaning.

For a worked case, imagine an authentic historic die used to make a modern demonstration impression. The tool can be historical while the impression is modern. That impression does not authenticate a historical diplomatic document merely because it was made with an old tool. Documentary attachment, issuer and date still matter.

The same distinction applies to photographs. A photograph of a die gives evidence of the tool’s current form. A photograph of a sealed document gives evidence of an impression and its attachment, subject to the limits of the image. Neither is a substitute for the other when the question concerns manufacture or administrative practice.

## Plaques are not families

The Government Office’s account identifies 280 historical armorial plaques representing 254 families in the knighthood collection.[^ee-knighthood] Those two counts are different denominators. Multiple objects can represent one family, and the collection’s rules of admission and display shape what appears on its walls.

A study of the collection should therefore count physical plaques, distinct designs, family branches and families separately. It should also record the historical conditions for display. A collection can be extensive while excluding families that did not meet those conditions. Calling it “all Estonian family arms” would turn a defined corporate corpus into an unsupported national total.

Relocation, restoration and reshaping supply further histories. The present display is not a transparent window onto the exact arrangement at every earlier date. Compare inventories, photographs and conservation records before treating its order or material dimensions as unchanged historical evidence.

## Arms and a derived logo have different jobs

Government visual-identity guidance distinguishes the state arms from a derived three-lions logo and assigns them to different media.[^ee-identity] The familiar figures can consequently remain recognizable while the graphic device’s institutional role changes.

This is an excellent comparison for an academy that also teaches blazon. Heraldic identity and a modern logo system can interact without becoming the same thing. A logo may prescribe a fixed layout, typography or approved file. A description of arms may permit different artistic realizations within specified content. The relevant guidance tells us which mechanism governs the actual public use.

An image database should link the two devices while preserving their separate records. If the derived logo replaces every armorial image in the interface, readers lose evidence of the distinction. If it is mislabelled as a new coat of arms, the database invents a heraldic change that the guidance does not establish.

## The Baltic comparison preserves local systems

Compare Estonia with [Latvia](/traditions/latvia/) and [Lithuania](/traditions/lithuania/). Regional proximity and political upheaval make the comparison valuable, but the specific state acts, collections and public-design arrangements remain local. The three countries do not become one institution because a course groups them on a map.

Further work should examine municipal records and individual historical armorial witnesses beyond the state and knighthood cases introduced here. Such expansion requires a new corpus and admission criteria. The present evidence provides a strong beginning in public-symbol administration and material survival without claiming to exhaust every Estonian bearer.

## Three lions can connect institutions without making them identical

Estonia’s state shield and Tallinn’s civic lion shield share a conspicuous family resemblance: three blue walking lions on gold. Tallinn’s municipal account specifies gold crowns on its lions and identifies a city lion seal dated **1277**.[^ee-tallinn-history] These particulars make a stronger comparison than the loose assertion that both designs “come from Denmark.” We can examine a dated witness and distinguish the civic specification from the state model.

The animals’ position matters. A walking lion with its head directed toward the viewer is not simply a smaller rampant lion. Body and head point in different ways. In a row of three animals, their repeated horizontal posture also affects how the shield is filled. Compare this arrangement with a single large rampant lion: one repeats a compact figure vertically, while the other expands a single animal across the field.

The shared pattern provides evidence of a relationship between designs, but identifying that relationship still requires a chronology. A medieval city seal, an imperial provincial achievement and a republican state shield represent different bearers. A motif can pass among them while its political function changes. Record the institution alongside the date so that visual continuity does not become a claim of unchanged sovereignty.

The crowns on Tallinn’s animals provide another useful distinction. They are internal attributes of the charges. A crown used as a crest or external ornament occupies a different place. At small scale, a crown can disappear into the lion’s mane; a researcher should compare the adopted model before deciding that a photographed rendering depicts an uncrowned animal. Loss of detail in a reproduction is not automatically a historical alteration.

## Tallinn’s small arms are a different shield

Tallinn’s symbol system supplies an excellent challenge to an assumption that “small arms” always means the principal shield with its outer ornaments removed. The city’s statute identifies a small shield bearing a silver cross on red, whereas the large shield contains the three crowned blue lions on gold.[^ee-tallinn-statute] The word *small* names a particular civic form. It does not instruct the reader to manufacture a smaller copy of the lion shield.

This is why comparative terminology needs examples. In Estonia’s state system, removing the oak branches distinguishes the smaller presentation from the large one.[^ee-state] In Tallinn’s civic system, the distinction between the named shields changes the internal design. The same translated adjective participates in different local conventions. A table of universal terminology would conceal the very difference that the student needs to learn.

| Bearer | Form to identify | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Republic of Estonia | Large or small state arms | Are the prescribed oak branches present? |
| City of Tallinn | Large civic shield | Are the crowned lions shown on gold? |
| City of Tallinn | Small civic shield | Is the silver cross shown on red? |
| City of Tallinn | Full achievement | Are helmet, crest and mantling included? |

The table is a guide to identification, not a permission chart. The relevant institutional rules determine where each form is used. A business card, civic seal, ceremonial chain and museum caption can have different requirements even when all identify the same city. Start by naming the object and the form rather than choosing whichever shield best fits the available space.

## The full Tallinn achievement restores a third dimension

Tallinn’s full achievement adds a helmet, mantling and a crowned female figure rising as the crest. The statutory witness gives the figure a red robe and crossed arms before the chest.[^ee-tallinn-statute] This fuller composition places the civic shield within the helmet-and-crest vocabulary familiar from other European achievements.

Read the arrangement from the shield upward. The helmet provides the physical and visual support for the crest, while the mantling falls around it. The figure rising from the crest crown is not another supporter standing beside the shield. Her position identifies the heraldic component she occupies. Moving her to the side would change the composition, even if the artist preserved her robe, crown and gesture.

A sculptural version may make this relationship easier to understand than a flat drawing. Imagine the shield as a planar surface and the helmet and crest as objects above it. Then examine how a painter compresses those forms into one image. Proportion, overlap and viewpoint can vary, but the identity of the components remains readable. This is why the [anatomy lesson](/academy/anatomy-of-arms/) treats an achievement as a structured whole rather than as a bag of decorations.

The civic use of a complete achievement also cautions against treating helmets and crests as exclusive evidence of personal nobility. Here the specified bearer is a city. The object can use the same visual grammar for a corporate identity. The institution and documentary context establish what is being represented.

## Two Tallinn flags answer different design questions

Tallinn's **1996 city statute**, consulted in a **2023 consolidated version**, distinguishes the striped city flag from a heraldic flag displaying the crowned lions on yellow. The consolidation date is not the statute's original enactment date. The striped flag alternates three blue and three white bands; the armorial flag uses the lion design.[^ee-tallinn-statute] One municipal identity therefore includes both a banded textile and a banner carrying armorial charges.

To compare them, translate each into operations. The striped flag divides the whole cloth into equal horizontal bands. The heraldic flag treats the cloth as a field carrying three recognizable figures. Reconstructing one from the other would require adding or removing information. Their association within the city’s symbol system is an institutional fact, not a visual equivalence.

The city’s historical account offers a narrative connecting earlier lion-bearing cloth with the later stripe pattern.[^ee-tallinn-history] Treat that narrative as an attributed historical explanation, and look for dated textiles, illustrations or regulations before using it to date a particular flag. A plausible design story can explain why two forms feel related without establishing every stage of the proposed transition.

Flag format also affects the animal drawing. A shield narrows toward its lower point; a rectangular textile does not. The third lion may consequently have more horizontal room on the cloth. That change in available space permits an adapted rendering while preserving count, posture and tinctures. A heraldic flag is a useful test of whether an artist understands the design or has merely stretched an existing shield image.

## Tartu gives us an exact civic reconstruction problem

Tartu’s official description places a silver city wall and towers on red, with a star and chain in the gateway and a crossed key and sword above. The sword passes over the key.[^ee-tartu] The arms offer a detailed drawing problem because several recognizable figures occupy specified positions and overlap in a specified order.

Begin with the architecture, leaving a gate opening large enough to preserve its contents. Place the star and chain within that opening. Then locate the crossed objects in the space between the towers. The key’s bit and the sword’s hilt identify their ends; reversing either can alter the instructed orientation. Finally, preserve the sword’s position over the key at the crossing. These small relationships distinguish a faithful reconstruction from a collage containing the same nouns.

The two crossed objects also demonstrate the value of splitting identification from symbolism. We can identify a key and sword from the description without yet explaining every religious or civic association. A historical interpretation should then locate the relevant institution and witness. Objects often have several possible symbolic associations; the chosen meaning must come from the case under study.

For an exercise, draw a deliberately incorrect version with the key above the sword, the star outside the gate and the chain between the towers. Then write a correction using spatial instructions rather than pointing vaguely at the picture. This develops the skill needed to diagnose heraldic rendering errors in manuscripts, modern graphics and automated builders.

## A recovered die can establish material continuity

The state-seal history already introduced in this chapter distinguishes renewed use of the design from recovery of earlier tools. The State Chancellery’s account identifies the **2008** recovery of the older dies.[^ee-seal] The finding restored access to particular material witnesses; it did not retroactively make every earlier modern seal impression an impression from those recovered tools.

A die and its impression stand in a specific manufacturing relationship. Raised and recessed surfaces reverse; the matrix must account for the final image’s direction and legend. A photograph of the die may therefore need to be compared with a known impression before making judgments about orientation. A correctly cut reversed legend on a tool can look wrong when mistaken for the finished seal.

Material examination can reveal wear, repairs and changes in use. Documentary records can establish custody and concealment. The design can establish an emblematic identity. These forms of evidence reinforce one another, but they remain distinguishable. A researcher who records the die’s measurements and condition has preserved information that a shield-only crop cannot carry.

This case also turns “restoration” into a precise question. Was a design reinstated, a tool recovered, a new tool cut, or an original object conserved? Each can be described as restoration in ordinary prose. A historical account becomes clearer when it says which operation occurred and gives the associated date.

## Graduate workshop: compare bearers, components and material witnesses

Build a comparison using Estonia’s state arms, Tallinn’s two civic shields and full achievement, and Tartu’s described composition. Add one seal or die record from a named institution. Your table should have separate columns for bearer, principal shield, external components, format and date.

Then explain one apparent contradiction. Why do Tallinn’s “large” and “small” arms contain different charges? Why can a flag omit a shield outline while preserving its design? Why does a recovered die not date every impression of that emblem? Choose one question and make the relationship explicit before adding historical interpretation.

A bearer can have several prescribed symbols, and related bearers can share motifs without being the same institution. The material record establishes a particular witness and manufacturing history. A strong answer uses these distinctions to explain the evidence rather than treating variation as an error to remove.</p></details>

## Workshop: four kinds of continuity

A fictional archive describes a restored family plaque, an old state-seal die, a modern diplomatic impression and a derived government logo as four examples of “unchanged heraldry.” For each, identify what actually continued and what changed. Give the date and bearer fields needed to test the description.

<details class="research-answer"><summary>Discuss continuity by layer</summary><p>The plaque concerns a family device and restored material; the die concerns a surviving tool; the diplomatic impression concerns a dated official act; and the logo concerns a derived graphic system. Recognition of familiar forms does not establish uninterrupted material custody, legal use or design identity across all four. Separate records allow the historical continuities to be stated precisely.</p></details>

For graduate work, compare an earlier inventory of the knighthood collection with its present catalogue. Calculate counts using stated denominators and document relocation or restoration separately from changes in attribution. The research should explain how the public presentation of the collection changed, rather than merely reporting how many coats of arms can now be seen.
