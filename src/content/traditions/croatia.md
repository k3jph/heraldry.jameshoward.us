## A public body's heraldry is a matter of administrative law

A county, city or municipality can be a heraldic bearer without being a hereditary family. Croatia's modern legal framework gives that proposition practical consequences. The [2001 Act on Local and Regional Self-Government](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/2001_04_33_569.html) allows these public bodies to use a *grb* (coat of arms) and *zastava* (flag). Its Article 10 addresses approval procedures and the relationship of those symbols to local statutes or specific decisions.

Rather than project the English College of Arms onto Zagreb, we should ask: which municipal organ proposes and adopts the design, which national administrative body approves it, who examines its historical accuracy, and which record survives? These questions turn a familiar picture into a subject of constitutional and local-government history.

A central provision specifies that the coat must be **heraldically correct and described according to heraldic conventions**, and that the official arms consist **exclusively of the shield and its contents**. Thus, for this category of public-body arms, the statute itself draws a distinction between the essential shield and external ornaments. Compare this with the many full achievements containing helms, crests and supporters in other contexts. One cannot dismiss the Croatian rule as an artistic preference when its own statute uses mandatory language.

That distinction also guards against anachronism. A municipality may historically have represented itself with banners, seals and elaborated symbols, while a modern law prescribes the form of an officially approved shield. The existence of an older illustration with a crown or supporters does not, by itself, prove that those elements form part of the modern statutory coat.

## The archive is part of the approval procedure

The [1998 administrative regulation](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/full/1998_07_94_1289.html) gives unusually specific information about historical research before a proposed coat is approved. Among the application materials it requires are a proposed municipal decision, written descriptions of arms and flag, colour artwork and a **written statement by the Croatian State Archives** concerning historically relevant armorial devices and symbols.

This is a powerful pedagogical example. Source criticism is not merely an advanced elective: archival evidence is part of the administrative process envisaged by the regulation. A town's assertion that its coat is 'ancient' needs historical documentation. A new commission may choose symbols with local meaning, but the formal review asks whether the sources actually support the claims made for them.

The regulation describes a *Povjerenstvo*, a multidisciplinary committee whose work combines law, heraldry, archival study, visual art and history. Its assessment concerns the truthfulness of submitted information, appropriateness of charges, conformity with heraldic requirements, accuracy of the description and artistic quality.

Most importantly, that committee produces a **written opinion**. The national ministry conducts the subsequent administrative proceeding and issues a formal decision granting or withholding approval. Opinion and approval are different legal acts. A historian who translates both as 'the coat was granted by heralds' has obscured who held actual governmental competence.

Once approval becomes legally effective, the regulation contemplates a *grbovnica*, a formal diploma that commemorates or documents the armorial decision. It is not another separate public grant to the private citizens of the municipality.

## The law changed: compare 2001 with 2013

A graduate course should not cite the 1998 regulation or original 2001 act as though the administrative system were legally frozen. The [2013 consolidated version](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/full/2013_02_19_323.html) records a consequential **2012 amendment**: the word *prethodno* (previous/prior) was removed from the approval wording, and explicit provisions were added for a ministry-issued administrative decision and judicial challenge through an administrative court.

That revision matters to anyone attempting to reconstruct the legality of a design adopted at a particular moment. A procedure that existed in 1998 is evidence of the rules operating then. A 2013 consolidated text presents the law as amended to that date. Neither necessarily establishes the exact currently operative procedure without further amendment checks.

We should record distinct source dates: the adoption of an individual municipal emblem, the original statute and regulation, the later amendment, the version consulted and the modern digital publication date. The same coat might be represented across all these materials without the law governing it remaining identical.

Croatian civic heraldry thus offers a case study in legal change as well as visual form. The answer to 'who can approve this?' can change across years, and a legally competent institution in one period may cease to have exactly the same function later.

## Heraldry and flags together

Unlike a course concerned solely with shields, the Croatian framework links arms and flags. The 1998 regulation addresses flag colours, placement of a municipal coat upon a flag, standard proportions and the relation of local flags to county and national insignia.

Not all heraldic flags are banners of arms in the strict sense; an approved civic flag may contain a shield upon a differently coloured field. This distinction has practical consequences for graphic design and official protocol. A vector shield compositing tool should not imply that placing a coat of arms in the centre of any flag automatically creates an official municipal flag.

The administrative decision also contemplates controlling use by private or corporate entities. Permission to reproduce civic insignia may be distinct from the right of the public body to adopt its official emblem and from copyright in a photograph of it.

## Source and translation workshops

**Undergraduate exercise:** Read Article 10 of the [2001 law](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/2001_04_33_569.html) in Croatian. Distinguish between the *contents of a shield*, a *flag*, and an external ornament of an achievement. Show why a coat containing supporters is not necessarily an approved public-body coat under the text.

**Document laboratory:** Read Articles 4–12 of the [1998 regulation](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/full/1998_07_94_1289.html) and map every actor: applicant municipality, archive, committee, ministerial decision-maker, and the resulting register/diploma. Annotate the sequence with the exact article numbers, distinguishing evidence gathering, advisory opinion and the administrative act.

**Graduate assignment:** Translate the relevant clauses of Article 10 from both the 2001 law and the [2013 consolidated text](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/full/2013_02_19_323.html). Cite the 2012 amending act where it changed the wording. Write 1,500–2,000 words evaluating whether a town's historic seal, a proposed modern coat and a ministry-approved graphic are legally and historically the same kind of object.
