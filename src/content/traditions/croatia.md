## A public body's heraldry is a matter of administrative law

A county, city or municipality can be a heraldic bearer without being a hereditary family. Croatia's modern legal framework gives that proposition practical consequences. The [2001 Act on Local and Regional Self-Government](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/2001_04_33_569.html) allows these public bodies to use a *grb* (coat of arms) and *zastava* (flag). Its Article 10 addresses approval procedures and the relationship of those symbols to local statutes or specific decisions.

Rather than project the English College of Arms onto Zagreb, we should ask: which municipal organ proposes and adopts the design, which national administrative body approves it, who examines its historical accuracy, and which record survives? These questions turn a familiar picture into a subject of constitutional and local-government history.

A central provision specifies that the coat must be **heraldically correct and described according to heraldic conventions**, and that the official arms consist **exclusively of the shield and its contents**. Thus, for this category of public-body arms, the statute itself draws a distinction between the essential shield and external ornaments. Compare this with the many full achievements containing helms, crests and supporters in other contexts. One cannot dismiss the Croatian rule as an artistic preference when its own statute uses mandatory language.

That distinction also guards against anachronism. A municipality may historically have represented itself with banners, seals and elaborated symbols, while a modern law prescribes the form of an officially approved shield. The existence of an older illustration with a crown or supporters does not, by itself, prove that those elements form part of the modern statutory coat.

## The archive is part of the approval procedure

The [1998 administrative regulation](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/full/1998_07_94_1289.html) gives unusually specific information about historical research before a proposed coat is approved. Among the application materials it requires are a proposed municipal decision, written descriptions of arms and flag, colour artwork and a **written statement by the Croatian State Archives** concerning historically relevant armorial devices and symbols.

The required archival statement gives historical evidence a place in the application itself. If a town describes a proposed charge as ancient, the reviewer needs a witness that can support that date and association. If the charge is newly chosen, the application can explain the present intention instead. Those are different claims, even when they produce similar artwork. Reading the supporting statement shows how the administrative process handles the distinction rather than leaving “ancient” as an attractive word in the caption.

The regulation describes a *Povjerenstvo*, a multidisciplinary committee whose work combines law, heraldry, archival study, visual art and history. Its assessment concerns the truthfulness of submitted information, appropriateness of charges, conformity with heraldic requirements, accuracy of the description and artistic quality.

Most importantly, that committee produces a **written opinion**. The national ministry conducts the subsequent administrative proceeding and issues a formal decision granting or withholding approval. Opinion and approval are different legal acts. A historian who translates both as 'the coat was granted by heralds' has obscured who held actual governmental competence.

Once approval becomes legally effective, the regulation contemplates a *grbovnica*, a formal diploma that commemorates or documents the armorial decision. It is not another separate public grant to the private citizens of the municipality.

## The law changed: compare 2001 with 2013

A graduate course should not cite the 1998 regulation or original 2001 act as though the administrative system were legally frozen. The [2013 consolidated version](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/full/2013_02_19_323.html) records a consequential **2012 amendment**: the word *prethodno* (previous/prior) was removed from the approval wording, and explicit provisions were added for a ministry-issued administrative decision and judicial challenge through an administrative court.

That revision matters to anyone attempting to reconstruct the legality of a design adopted at a particular moment. A procedure that existed in 1998 is evidence of the rules operating then. A 2013 consolidated text presents the law as amended to that date. Neither necessarily establishes the exact currently operative procedure without further amendment checks.

We should record distinct source dates: the adoption of an individual municipal emblem, the original statute and regulation, the later amendment, the version consulted and the modern digital publication date. The same coat might be represented across all these materials without the law governing it remaining identical.

Croatian civic heraldry thus offers a case study in legal change as well as visual form. The answer to 'who can approve this?' can change across years, and a legally competent institution in one period may cease to have exactly the same function later.

## Heraldry and flags together

Unlike a course concerned solely with shields, the Croatian framework links arms and flags. The 1998 regulation addresses flag colours, placement of a municipal coat upon a flag, standard proportions and the relation of local flags to county and national insignia.

Not all heraldic flags are banners of arms in the strict sense; an approved civic flag may contain a shield upon a differently coloured field. This distinction has practical consequences for graphic design and official protocol. A vector shield compositing tool should not imply that placing a coat of arms in the centre of any flag automatically creates an official municipal flag.

The administrative decision also contemplates controlling use by private or corporate entities. Permission to reproduce civic insignia may be distinct from the right of the public body to adopt its official emblem and from copyright in a photograph of it.

## Source and translation workshops

**Undergraduate exercise:** Read Article 10 of the [2001 law](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/2001_04_33_569.html) in Croatian. Distinguish between the *contents of a shield*, a *flag*, and an external ornament of an achievement. Show why a coat containing supporters is not necessarily an approved public-body coat under the text.

**Document laboratory:** Read Articles 4–12 of the [1998 regulation](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/full/1998_07_94_1289.html) and map every actor: applicant municipality, archive, committee, ministerial decision-maker, and the resulting register/diploma. Annotate the sequence with the exact article numbers, distinguishing evidence gathering, advisory opinion and the administrative act.

**Graduate assignment:** Translate the relevant clauses of Article 10 from both the 2001 law and the [2013 consolidated text](https://narodne-novine.nn.hr/clanci/sluzbeni/full/2013_02_19_323.html). Cite the 2012 amending act where it changed the wording. Write 1,500–2,000 words evaluating whether a town's historic seal, a proposed modern coat and a ministry-approved graphic are legally and historically the same kind of object.

## Twenty-five squares need a date and an order

The present national shield has twenty-five red-and-white, or red-and-silver, squares, with red first. The official description specifies that beginning rather than leaving it to the artist.[^hr-state-description] A checkerboard of the same size beginning with white has retained the general motif while changing the prescribed present arrangement. The difference is easy to miss in a small image and easy to reproduce incorrectly from memory.

Begin at the shield's dexter chief, the viewer's upper left, and count five rows of five. With red first, red occupies thirteen squares and white twelve. That result follows from the odd number of rows and columns; it is not an independent instruction to distribute thirteen isolated red charges over a white field. The alternating pattern forms the shield's field throughout.

An earlier object can have a different arrangement without being a failed attempt to draw today's state arms. Record its actual first colour, number of squares and setting before making a claim about continuity or alteration. A historical Croatian checkerboard, a present state rendering and a politically appropriated version require dated evidence to explain their relationships. The first square alone cannot provide the complete biography of an undated object.

The colloquial association with a chessboard is a useful recognition aid but an incomplete description. A conventional chessboard has eight rows and eight columns; the prescribed national shield has five of each. Calling the arms “a chessboard” may help someone identify the motif, but it should not replace the count and tinctures when the task is accurate reproduction. Recognition vocabulary and formal instruction work at different levels.

## Five small shields form an unusual crown

Above the main shield, the national composition places five historical shields: an older Croatian sign, Dubrovnik, Dalmatia, Istria and Slavonia. The official description identifies a star and crescent in the first, bars in the second, three crowned leopard heads in the third, a goat in the fourth and a marten between bars in the fifth.[^hr-state-description] The arrangement is a crown of shields rather than a conventional metal crown borrowed from a rank table.

The word *kruna* therefore needs its context. A reader looking only for pearls, fleurs-de-lis and arches would overlook what this particular crown is made of. Its elements have their own fields and charges, and their order belongs to the complete national design. Extracting one little shield creates a different teaching object from reproducing the entire crown.

The Dalmatian heads illustrate another terminological trap. In heraldic usage a leopard's head can describe the conventional facing head associated with the armorial lion. It does not necessarily ask the artist to draw a spotted zoological leopard. Compare the approved artwork with the term before assigning modern natural-history features to it. The device's recognizability rests on the established heraldic form.

The Slavonian *kuna* is a marten, not a rabbit or a generic small mammal. Its placement between bars matters just as much as the animal. A caption that isolates “a marten” can teach the charge; a description of the full little shield must restore its field, surrounding bars and star. The relationships between charges are part of the coat's grammar.

The historic labels in an official present-day explanation also deserve a careful chronology. They identify how the state interprets the composite crown. An assertion that one device is the “oldest known” Croatian arms needs comparison with dated witnesses if it is to become a wider historical conclusion. We can use the official present arrangement confidently while keeping that historical priority claim attributed.

## Design authorship and digital processing are separate credits

The Croatian Parliament's state-symbol page credits Miroslav Šutej for the design and Božo Kokan for digital processing. It supplies approved digital records and references the legislation governing use.[^hr-state-design] This is a concrete case in which the designer of the emblem and the maker of a digital version are not collapsed into one name.

That distinction is useful for historical as well as modern images. A medieval bearer, the painter of a charter, the photographer of the charter and the illustrator of a modern reconstruction all occupy different roles. A digital drawing can accurately represent a public device while remaining an authored rendering. Identifying its official subject does not make its file anonymous.

For a reproduction, preserve the artwork's credit and check the applicable permissions. For a verbal discussion, the national description supports analysis of the composition without requiring the website to host the official image. The existence of a download button establishes availability; it does not, by itself, specify every commercial, editorial or derivative use one might make of the file.

The approved model is also a useful control for design errors. Count the main squares and inspect the five upper shields against it. Compare the outlines, colours and relative placement before concluding that a popular online version is authoritative. Search-engine visibility is a distribution mechanism, and it is remarkably indifferent to whether the goat has been reversed.

## State and municipal arms use different legal categories

The municipal shield-only rule introduced earlier in this chapter must be kept within its statutory scope. It governs the relevant local and regional public-body arms in the dated legal framework. It does not prohibit the distinctive crown of shields in the national composition. Reading one category's restrictions as a universal Croatian law of artistic form would make the national arms appear to violate a rule that was not written for them.

The same problem arises with historic full achievements. An older city display might include external ornaments, while a modern officially approved local coat consists of the shield and its contents. A researcher should identify which form the object depicts, when it was made and which approval applies. The old object does not become an error merely because a later statute narrows the official municipal form.

The archival statement contemplated in the 1998 approval regulation serves a further purpose: it connects a proposal with evidence of historical local arms. That is a check on the historical argument, alongside heraldic and artistic review. The statement, the committee's opinion and the ministry's approval are separate parts of the procedure. Combining them into a single supposed “grant by the archive” misidentifies who did what.[^hr-regulation-1998]

When the procedure changes, the date of the decision matters. The 2001 act and 2013 consolidated text in this chapter provide dated comparisons; they are not an invitation to assume every provision remains unchanged today. For an actual application, consult the operative rules and competent authority. For historical analysis, retain the version in effect for the decision under examination.

## Zagreb keeps its arms while adding a new graphic mark

In July 2026 Zagreb presented a new visual identity system developed after a public design competition. The city says it retains the historical coat and blue colour while adding an open shield-line mark carrying wording. The published account distinguishes the inherited armorial device from the broader communications system.[^hr-zagreb-identity-2026] This supplies a current case of adaptation rather than an imagined conflict between tradition and modern design.

The two elements can be compared by function. The arms identify the city through an established composition. The new graphic mark organizes wording and can operate across documents, websites, signs and other communications. Sharing a shield reference creates a visual relationship between them. It does not automatically turn every use of the new mark into an emblazonment of the complete coat.

The city interprets the open outline through an idea of openness, connecting it with the gates in its historic arms. That is a documented explanation of the 2026 design. It should not be projected backward as proof that every medieval open gate meant the same contemporary civic promise. A later designer can build a new argument from an old form, and the argument has its own date.

This comparison helps us ask better questions about a website header. Is it displaying the formal arms, a simplified derivative, a wordmark, or a combination? Which element is expected to remain constant, and which is designed to adapt to different formats? A good identity system can assign distinct jobs to several related signs. Treating every variation as a corrupted coat prevents us from seeing that division of labour.

The announcement's URL contains an English path, but its displayed text is Croatian. Language identification should follow the material actually read. Otherwise a source atlas will falsely reassure English-only readers that they can follow the full account without translation. Website routing conventions are administrative conveniences, not evidence about the language of every document they host.

## A Croatian blazon needs more than a colour lookup

Useful working terms include *grb* for arms, *štit* for shield, *polje* for field and *greda* for a horizontal bar in the descriptions examined here. *Srebrno* identifies silver in heraldic colour, which may be rendered white. *Zlatno* likewise identifies gold, often rendered yellow. A translation should preserve the heraldic category while explaining the reproduction convention where necessary.

Direction words require particular attention when a civic instructional page describes an image for a general audience. Does “left” mean the viewer's left or the shield bearer's left? Establish the convention by comparison with the approved drawing, then state it in the English description. If the source uses ordinary visual directions, translating them word for word into a heraldic blazon can reverse the animal.

Descriptions also differ in purpose. A blazon identifies the armorial structure; a graphic standard fixes technical details for a particular official application. A municipal statute can add provisions about approval and permission. Translating all three into a single paragraph loses the distinction between what the coat is, how a file is constructed and who may use it in a given setting.

For unfamiliar animals or historical names, retain the Croatian term beside the English explanation on first use. This makes subsequent consultation possible and exposes uncertainty honestly. A reader can check *kuna* against the original description much more effectively than reverse-engineering an English “beast” after the precise local term has been discarded.

## Graduate investigation: compare a coat with an identity system

Build a dated comparison of the Croatian state arms, one approved municipal coat and Zagreb's 2026 communications mark. Identify the bearer, governing instrument, essential image, external elements and reproduction rules for each. Include the artist or designer where the institutional record supplies a credit. Do not use a national model as evidence of a municipal authorization.

For Zagreb, diagram the relationship between the historical coat and the new open-outline mark. Identify which visible features are retained, which are simplified and which are introduced with wording. Then distinguish the city's explanation of the new design from any medieval interpretation. The comparison should explain continuity through adaptation with dates attached to both.

Finish with an application note for a hypothetical educational exhibition. State which objects can be described from the verified records, which images require a permissions check and which statutory versions need updating before a legal-use claim is made. A complete answer combines formal visual analysis with the administrative history of the symbols. It does not need to pretend that one universal rule governs every shield in Croatia.
