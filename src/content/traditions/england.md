## Start with a roll, not a surname

A medieval roll of arms makes heraldry look deceptively straightforward. Shields appear in rows, names accompany them, and a modern reader can imagine using the result as a directory. The Dering Roll, dated approximately 1270–1280, presents 324 shields. The College of Arms relates it to people owing service to the Constable of Dover Castle.[^dering] That setting gives the arrangement a purpose: it brings identifiable people into a political and military relationship. We should begin with that relationship before treating the shields as ornaments floating free of their bearers.

There are at least three questions here. Which design appears beside a particular name? What did inclusion mean when the roll was compiled? Who could subsequently claim that design? The manuscript supplies direct evidence for the first question and historical evidence for the second. The third needs an account of descent and authority beyond the painted entry. A shield can survive for centuries while the evidence connecting it to a particular living person is absent.

This is why “English heraldry” needs both an artistic history and an institutional history. The artistic history follows forms, tinctures, charges, materials, and styles. The institutional history follows grants, pedigrees, records, inquiries, and disputes. They meet in particular artifacts, but they do different work. A beautifully drawn coat can have a poorly supported attribution; a securely recorded coat can have a mediocre modern drawing. Neither defect is repaired by confusing the two evaluations.

## The College and the Crown

The College of Arms dates its foundation to 1484. Its heralds act under Crown authority, maintain heraldic and genealogical records, and undertake work for individuals and corporations. Its present remit includes England and Wales, Northern Ireland, and parts of the Commonwealth.[^college] Scotland has a separate authority, discussed in its own chapter. Political proximity does not make those systems administratively interchangeable.

For a new grant, the College describes a petition to the Earl Marshal, followed by authorization for the Kings of Arms to proceed. A herald works with the applicant on the proposed design; letters patent complete the grant.[^grant] There are therefore several actors, rather than one person sitting at a drawing board. The applicant supplies circumstances and desired allusions. The officers consider the design within their rules and records. The instrument identifies what has been granted and to whom.

Consider an invented applicant who wants a bridge for engineering, a book for teaching, an oak for a birthplace, a ship for migration, and six other objects for six other achievements. Each allusion might be intelligible alone. Put all of them on a small shield, however, and the identifying device can become an illustrated curriculum vitae. Design selection has to solve the recognition problem as well as the autobiographical problem. An emblem can preserve a meaningful relationship without depicting every fact about its bearer.

The official process also creates durable evidence. A commission to paint a shield produces an image and perhaps a commercial invoice. A grant produces an instrument within a particular authority’s records. Both can be historical objects, but their institutional consequences differ. To classify an object correctly, ask what act produced it and which record documents that act. The elegance of the parchment is evidence of skilled production; the grant’s terms establish the armorial relationship.

## Descent is a chain of people

The College’s account of inherited entitlement requires a connection to an ancestor recorded as entitled to arms. Its illustrated visitation pedigree from Buckinghamshire in 1634 includes Peter Montague, described as being in Virginia.[^descent] This is a useful place to see how an English record can open a transatlantic inquiry. The named person and dated pedigree supply a point from which to work, rather than a promise that every modern Montague shares the same entitlement.

Model the problem as a chain. A is the recorded bearer. B is A’s child, C is B’s child, and D is a modern claimant. Evidence has to establish the relevant links and the applicable mode of transmission. Finding a design under the same surname as D establishes a search lead. Finding an ancestor with that name establishes another lead. The leads become a claim only when the people, relationships, and governing rules have been connected.

A missing generation is especially consequential. Suppose three links are supported by reliable records and a fourth is inferred from a repeated name. That inference carries the entire connection across its weakest point. More copies of the coat of arms do not strengthen the missing parentage. The useful next research step is a birth, marriage, probate, or other record bearing on that relationship. A surname website can provide an illustration without supplying the argument the illustration seems to invite.

The College also describes transmission through a heraldic heiress in defined circumstances.[^law] That term concerns armorial transmission, rather than the size of an estate or a woman’s social prominence. A quartered shield can therefore encode relationships governed by particular rules. The Academy’s [marshalling chapter](/academy/marshalling/) develops the visual grammar; a historical claim still requires the pedigree that makes the grammar applicable.

## Cadency is useful, and context is useful too

Cadency means distinguishing related bearers. The familiar English teaching sequence includes a label, crescent, mullet, and martlet for successive sons. The College’s published FAQ observes that the textbook sequence is little used in modern practice and that context often identifies the particular family member.[^faq] This is a useful warning against reading a diagram of possible distinctions as a census of actual usage.

Imagine two brothers sending letters with the same inherited shield. Their names and addresses can identify the correspondents even when their seals do not carry different small charges. In another setting, where people need to distinguish banners at a distance, a visible difference may do more work. The identifying system includes the surrounding document, location, and occasion. We cannot discover how much the shield had to communicate by studying the shield alone.

Historical variation follows from the same mechanism. A difference might be temporary, persistent, prescribed, adopted, or subsequently abandoned. Its meaning has to be connected to a bearer and period. A small star seen on a medieval seal should first be recorded as a small star in a particular position. Assigning it to the modern “third son” slot requires additional evidence. Familiar vocabulary helps us describe the artifact; familiar charts cannot date themselves backwards into every artifact.

## Visitations, copies, and altered witnesses

The College describes royal commissions to visit English and Welsh counties between 1530 and 1689. Those inquiries concerned the authority under which arms were borne and generated pedigrees and other records.[^law] A visitation record is valuable because it documents an encounter between a claim and an inquiry. To understand an entry, investigate what was submitted, what was recorded, and which surviving witness you are reading.

A published nineteenth-century edition is a further stage. It may transcribe a manuscript accurately, omit material, normalize spelling, or combine information from several witnesses. Its convenience does not make those editorial choices disappear. A researcher should retain the county, visitation date, manuscript or edition identifier, page or folio, and editorial basis. “From the visitation” is a useful beginning for a citation, but a poor stopping place.

The Dering Roll has its own later history of ownership and copying. The College’s acquisition account notes Elizabethan copies in its archives.[^dering] A medieval arrangement and a later copy are distinct witnesses. Their agreement can preserve information; their differences can reveal copying, correction, damage, or intervention. The [visual-language reading](/academy/the-visual-language/) develops a related exercise using separately catalogued Dering witnesses. Follow the shelfmark, not just the famous title.

## Law, corporate arms, and public display

The College identifies the Court of Chivalry as the jurisdiction for misuse of arms and recounts the 1954 dispute between Manchester Corporation and the Manchester Palace of Varieties.[^law] This case makes the corporate bearer visible. Arms can identify a public institution as well as a hereditary individual. A theatre displaying a corporation’s arms creates a question about whose identity is being presented, even if the painting itself is heraldically correct.

This example also shows why permission, attribution, and entitlement are different questions. Naming the corporation accurately answers an attribution question. Obtaining permission to reproduce a particular photograph answers an image-use question. Displaying the arms in a manner that suggests institutional authorization raises a question about the emblem’s use. A research caption and a building façade operate in different circumstances; an argument about one should preserve that setting.

For comparison, follow the [Scottish chapter](/traditions/scotland/) into a different court and register, or the [United States chapter](/traditions/united-states/) into a fragmented set of public and private practices. The productive comparison concerns who decides, what gets recorded, and how a dispute can be handled. Comparing lion styles can come afterwards; the lions will wait.

## Workshop: three records, three conclusions

Work with this invented file: a modern printed shield labelled “Montague,” the College’s caption for a 1634 pedigree mentioning Peter Montague in Virginia, and a set of family records that stops two generations short of him. Write separate conclusions about design, historical presence, and modern descent. Identify the single next investigation most likely to change the inherited-entitlement argument.

<details class="research-answer"><summary>Discuss the missing connection</summary><p>The print supplies a modern attribution to a surname. The institutional caption identifies a historical pedigree and a named person in Virginia. The family records support only the relationships they actually document. The unresolved generations remain necessary to connect the claimant to the recorded bearer. Research directed at those people can strengthen or defeat the connection; buying another illustration cannot.</p></details>

For a second exercise, design an invented shield using only two of the applicant’s ten proposed allusions. Explain why those two carry the argument, identify what is lost by omitting the others, and produce a verbal specification that permits two different artists to draw recognizable versions. Compare this with the College’s distinction between the blazon and the painting.[^grant]

<details class="research-answer"><summary>Discuss identity across two drawings</summary><p>Vary drawing style, proportions within reasonable limits, and the treatment of details while preserving the specified tinctures, charges, and arrangement. The comparison tests whether the design has a stable identity across realizations. If the two versions differ in a feature essential to the specification, the problem is substantive. If they differ only in an unspecified decorative flourish, the divergence can be artistic.</p></details>

## Graduate inquiry: reconstruct one armorial relationship

Select one named bearer documented in an institutional record. Build a chronology of the armorial claim, the source that records it, and at least two subsequent appearances. Ask how the design’s identity persisted across media and whether the later users were connected by descent, office, association, or something else. The project should explain a relationship rather than merely assemble matching pictures.

Use a witness inventory with separate entries for originals, copies, editions, and modern photographs. Where a pedigree is part of the argument, identify each necessary link and its evidence. Where a corporate body is involved, identify the act and date of institutional continuity or change. Finish with the strongest conclusion supported by those records and a specific account of what remains to be established.
