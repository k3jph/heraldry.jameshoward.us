## Start with a roll, not a surname

A medieval roll of arms makes heraldry look deceptively straightforward. Shields appear in rows, names accompany them, and a modern reader can imagine using the result as a directory. The Dering Roll, dated approximately 1270–1280, presents 324 shields. The College of Arms relates it to people owing service to the Constable of Dover Castle.[^dering] That setting gives the arrangement a purpose: it brings identifiable people into a political and military relationship. We should begin with that relationship before treating the shields as ornaments floating free of their bearers.

There are at least three questions here. Which design appears beside a particular name? What did inclusion mean when the roll was compiled? Who could subsequently claim that design? The manuscript supplies direct evidence for the first question and historical evidence for the second. The third needs an account of descent and authority beyond the painted entry. A shield can survive for centuries while the evidence connecting it to a particular living person is absent.

This is why “English heraldry” needs both an artistic history and an institutional history. The artistic history follows forms, tinctures, charges, materials, and styles. The institutional history follows grants, pedigrees, records, inquiries, and disputes. They meet in particular artifacts, but they do different work. A beautifully drawn coat can have a poorly supported attribution; a securely recorded coat can have a mediocre modern drawing. Neither defect is repaired by confusing the two evaluations.

## The College and the Crown

The College of Arms dates its foundation to 1484. Its heralds act under Crown authority, maintain heraldic and genealogical records, and undertake work for individuals and corporations. Its present remit includes England and Wales, Northern Ireland, and parts of the Commonwealth.[^college] Scotland has a separate authority, discussed in its own chapter. Political proximity does not make those systems administratively interchangeable.

For a new grant, the College describes a petition to the Earl Marshal, followed by authorization for the Kings of Arms to proceed. A herald works with the applicant on the proposed design; letters patent complete the grant.[^grant] There are therefore several actors, rather than one person sitting at a drawing board. The applicant supplies circumstances and desired allusions. The officers consider the design within their rules and records. The instrument identifies what has been granted and to whom.

Consider an invented applicant who wants a bridge for engineering, a book for teaching, an oak for a birthplace, a ship for migration, and six other objects for six other achievements. Each allusion might be intelligible alone. Put all of them on a small shield, however, and the identifying device can become an illustrated curriculum vitae. Design selection has to solve the recognition problem as well as the autobiographical problem. An emblem can preserve a meaningful relationship without depicting every fact about its bearer.

The official process also creates durable evidence. A commission to paint a shield produces an image and perhaps a commercial invoice. A grant produces an instrument within a particular authority’s records. Both can be historical objects, but their institutional consequences differ. To classify an object correctly, ask what act produced it and which record documents that act. The elegance of the parchment is evidence of skilled production; the grant’s terms establish the armorial relationship.

## Descent is a chain of people

The College’s account of inherited entitlement requires a connection to an ancestor recorded as entitled to arms. Its illustrated visitation pedigree from Buckinghamshire in 1634 includes Peter Montague, described as being in Virginia.[^descent] This is a useful place to see how an English record can open a transatlantic inquiry. The named person and dated pedigree supply a point from which to work, rather than a promise that every modern Montague shares the same entitlement.

Model the problem as a chain. A is the recorded bearer. B is A’s child, C is B’s child, and D is a modern claimant. Evidence has to establish the relevant links and the applicable mode of transmission. Finding a design under the same surname as D establishes a search lead. Finding an ancestor with that name establishes another lead. The leads become a claim only when the people, relationships, and governing rules have been connected.

A missing generation is especially consequential. Suppose three links are supported by reliable records and a fourth is inferred from a repeated name. That inference carries the entire connection across its weakest point. More copies of the coat of arms do not strengthen the missing parentage. The useful next research step is a birth, marriage, probate, or other record bearing on that relationship. A surname website can provide an illustration without supplying the argument the illustration seems to invite.

The College also describes transmission through a heraldic heiress in defined circumstances.[^law] That term concerns armorial transmission, rather than the size of an estate or a woman’s social prominence. A quartered shield can therefore encode relationships governed by particular rules. The Academy’s [marshalling chapter](/academy/marshalling/) develops the visual grammar; a historical claim still requires the pedigree that makes the grammar applicable.

## Cadency is useful, and context is useful too

Cadency means distinguishing related bearers. The familiar English teaching sequence includes a label, crescent, mullet, and martlet for successive sons. The College’s published FAQ observes that the textbook sequence is little used in modern practice and that context often identifies the particular family member.[^faq] This is a useful warning against reading a diagram of possible distinctions as a census of actual usage.

Imagine two brothers sending letters with the same inherited shield. Their names and addresses can identify the correspondents even when their seals do not carry different small charges. In another setting, where people need to distinguish banners at a distance, a visible difference may do more work. The identifying system includes the surrounding document, location, and occasion. We cannot discover how much the shield had to communicate by studying the shield alone.

Historical variation follows from the same mechanism. A difference might be temporary, persistent, prescribed, adopted, or subsequently abandoned. Its meaning has to be connected to a bearer and period. A small star seen on a medieval seal should first be recorded as a small star in a particular position. Assigning it to the modern “third son” slot requires additional evidence. Familiar vocabulary helps us describe the artifact; familiar charts cannot date themselves backwards into every artifact.

## Visitations, copies, and altered witnesses

The College describes royal commissions to visit English and Welsh counties between 1530 and 1689. Those inquiries concerned the authority under which arms were borne and generated pedigrees and other records.[^law] A visitation record is valuable because it documents an encounter between a claim and an inquiry. To understand an entry, investigate what was submitted, what was recorded, and which surviving witness you are reading.

A published nineteenth-century edition is a further stage. It may transcribe a manuscript accurately, omit material, normalize spelling, or combine information from several witnesses. Its convenience does not make those editorial choices disappear. A researcher should retain the county, visitation date, manuscript or edition identifier, page or folio, and editorial basis. “From the visitation” is a useful beginning for a citation, but a poor stopping place.

The Dering Roll has its own later history of ownership and copying. The College’s acquisition account notes Elizabethan copies in its archives.[^dering] A medieval arrangement and a later copy are distinct witnesses. Their agreement can preserve information; their differences can reveal copying, correction, damage, or intervention. The [visual-language reading](/academy/the-visual-language/) develops a related exercise using separately catalogued Dering witnesses. Follow the shelfmark, not just the famous title.

## Law, corporate arms, and public display

The College identifies the Court of Chivalry as the jurisdiction for misuse of arms and recounts the 1954 dispute between Manchester Corporation and the Manchester Palace of Varieties.[^law] This case makes the corporate bearer visible. Arms can identify a public institution as well as a hereditary individual. A theatre displaying a corporation’s arms creates a question about whose identity is being presented, even if the painting itself is heraldically correct.

This example also shows why permission, attribution, and entitlement are different questions. Naming the corporation accurately answers an attribution question. Obtaining permission to reproduce a particular photograph answers an image-use question. Displaying the arms in a manner that suggests institutional authorization raises a question about the emblem’s use. A research caption and a building façade operate in different circumstances; an argument about one should preserve that setting.

For comparison, follow the [Scottish chapter](/traditions/scotland/) into a different court and register, or the [United States chapter](/traditions/united-states/) into a fragmented set of public and private practices. The productive comparison concerns who decides, what gets recorded, and how a dispute can be handled. Comparing lion styles can come afterwards; the lions will wait.

## Before the College: identifying people in a changing visual culture

The [Bayeux embroidery study](/objects/bayeux-tapestry/) makes a useful starting comparison because its shields belong to a narrative in which bodies, weapons, lettering, and action identify people together. Later armorial practice gives a stable shield design a more specialized identifying role. That change should be investigated through dated witnesses rather than inferred from the mere presence of decoration on equipment. A patterned shield and an inherited coat can look similar while making different claims about their bearers.

The Dering Roll introduced above shows a developed armorial vocabulary operating within a particular relationship of service. Its named shields make the design-bearer association explicit. The roll also translates a device into a medium where the actual shield is absent. Paint on a manuscript can preserve the identifying composition because the composition has an identity beyond one physical support. This is a central development in armorial thinking: the arms can be redrawn, copied, and described while remaining recognizably associated with their bearer.

We can model that development with an invented coat, blue with a silver bridge. The bearer might use the design on a seal, a cloth banner, a monument, or a letter. The seal cannot display blue in the same way as the cloth, and the monument might lose its pigment altogether. A verbal specification can nevertheless preserve which features belong to the design. The identifying relationship survives through a combination of image, language, bearer, and context. It does not require every physical object to reproduce the first painting exactly.

That model also explains why the institutional chronology matters. A visual practice can exist before the organization later responsible for recording or regulating it. The College’s foundation in 1484 is a significant date in the history of heraldic administration, but it cannot serve as the birth date of every English armorial design. The earlier roll is already enough to demonstrate the difference. We need to follow both the development of the identifying practice and the development of the machinery through which it became officially recorded.

## Royal service becomes a continuing institution

The College’s history connects heralds’ royal and ceremonial service with their eventual corporate organization. It records incorporation in 1484, a charter and premises in 1555, and rebuilding following the Great Fire of 1666.[^eng-college-history] These events describe changing institutional arrangements rather than changes to the definition of a lion or chevron. Buildings, corporate continuity, and official responsibilities made it possible to preserve records across generations of individual officers and applicants.

The mechanism is worth making explicit. An applicant deals with living people, but a grant can outlast both the applicant and the officer who handled it. The continuing institution supplies a place where later researchers can identify the instrument, compare its terms, and investigate a descendant’s claim. The practical value lies in continuity of custody and reference. A particularly skilled herald is useful in one lifetime; a durable record system can support inquiries centuries later.

The office also links ceremonial and genealogical work. A ceremony may require the correct person, title, precedence, arms, and insignia to appear in the correct place. Those requirements make accurate identity operational rather than merely antiquarian. A record that is useful to a historian can also be useful to someone arranging an official event. This helps explain why heraldry’s administrative history cannot be separated entirely from public performance: the institution records distinctions that ceremonies then make visible.

We should still distinguish continuity from completeness. Archives can contain gaps, copies, disputed claims, and records made for particular purposes. A continuing custodian gives the researcher an address and an institutional history; it does not make every surviving entry an exhaustive account of the bearer’s life. The useful question is how the record’s purpose shaped the information it preserved. A grant, a pedigree, a funeral record, and a visitation inquiry can share names while documenting different acts.

## English blazon as a specification readers can operate

English heraldic language gives the reader a way to reconstruct a composition from an ordered description. Begin with the field, then the principal ordinary or charges, then the additional features needed to distinguish the design. The order matters because the reader has to build the visual space before placing objects within it. The Academy’s [blazon reading](/academy/reading-a-blazon/) develops this grammar in detail; here we can apply it to the difference between an identifying composition and one artist’s drawing.

Use a constructed coat: *Azure, a chevron between three mullets Argent*. The field is blue. A silver chevron organizes the shield, and three silver mullets occupy the spaces around it in the conventional arrangement appropriate to the description. The sentence does not dictate the exact curve of the shield outline, the texture of the blue, or the artist’s particular treatment of each point. Those variables belong to the realization. The field, ordinary, charges, tinctures, and arrangement supply the features whose preservation makes the realizations versions of one design.

Now change the sentence to place the mullets *on* the chevron. That small verbal change produces a different relationship. The stars occupy the ordinary instead of the surrounding field. It is therefore a substantive alteration rather than a flourish. The exercise demonstrates how prepositions can carry identifying information as efficiently as nouns. A drawing which preserves every object but changes their relationships can be the wrong coat, even where a casual viewer finds the result attractive.

English armorial vocabulary also includes terms inherited through French and Latin usage, while ordinary English words retain specialized meanings. *Chief* can mean the upper part of the shield or a particular ordinary; *proper* specifies a depicted subject’s natural colouring in the relevant description; *difference* concerns a distinguishing alteration. A reader should interpret the word within the construction rather than substitute its everyday meaning. The point of learning the language is to make those relationships recoverable, not to turn the chapter into a spelling competition.

## The shield, crest, badge, and banner carry different statements

A complete achievement can bring together a shield and additional components, but the components need not appear every time the bearer is identified. A small seal may have room for the shield alone. A larger architectural display can include helm, crest, mantling, motto, or other elements where appropriate. The missing components should be evaluated in context. A compact use can be deliberate, while an incomplete or inaccurate reproduction can omit something essential to the particular claim being made.

The distinction between a crest and a badge is especially useful in English comparison. The crest occupies a defined position above the helm in an achievement. A badge is a separate identifying device and can be displayed in other settings. Their subjects might overlap, but their jobs are different. The [anatomy reading](/academy/anatomy-of-arms/) lets us identify the component before interpreting its use. A caption which calls an entire shield-and-helm composition a “crest” hides the structure the viewer needs to understand.

A banner of arms provides another operation: the armorial composition occupies the flag’s field. Placing a complete shield drawing on a plain rectangle produces a different arrangement. Both could display armorial information, but they should be described accurately. In a constructed exercise, transfer the blue field, silver chevron, and three mullets to a rectangular banner without retaining the shield outline. The different container changes available space, while the specified relationships remain the identifying composition.

Public and military insignia add their own institutions and approval procedures. The College’s services account discusses naval badges and the Inspectorate of Regimental Colours separately, including different archival arrangements.[^eng-military-insignia] This is a reminder that “official heraldry” includes specialized work rather than one interchangeable class of object. When a naval badge is the object of inquiry, begin with the naval badge’s record; the presence of a herald in its history does not turn it into a personal hereditary grant.

## Quartering: a genealogy has to become a composition

The earlier account established why descent needs documentary links. We can now see how an eligible relationship becomes a visual arrangement. Start with two invented coats: A, a silver bridge on blue; B, a red rose on gold. In the simplified quartering exercise, A occupies the first and fourth quarters while B occupies the second and third. There are four compartments and two component designs. The repeated bridge does not introduce another ancestor merely by appearing a second time.

The familiar English heraldic-heiress explanation connects a defined genealogical circumstance with the transmission of a paternal coat as a quartering. Work through that circumstance using the College’s account and the Academy’s [marshalling discussion](/academy/marshalling/). A woman’s descent from an armiger and her status within the relevant transmission rule are distinct propositions. A family tree that proves only the favourable ancestral line can leave a necessary condition unexplained, especially where the rule depends on other surviving issue.

A useful fictional file has three parts. The first supplies the arms of the earlier bearer. The second documents the relevant family relationships and the conditions for transmission. The third records the later bearer’s marshalled design. Agreement between them makes the account stronger because the sources answer different questions. A painted shield with two components tells us what someone displayed; the pedigree explains the relationship claimed; the institutional record can establish how that relationship was recognized in the particular case.

The [Stowe Armorial](/objects/stowe-armorial/) takes the compositional problem to an extraordinary scale. Its hundreds of compartments make accumulated ancestry the visual subject. At that size, the display cannot be understood by treating every visible compartment as a new independent person. The researcher needs a key to the components and the relationships explaining their inclusion. The gallery study therefore complements this chapter: it shows how a system capable of combining a few coats can also become an architectural argument about a much larger genealogical past.

## An augmentation changes an existing armorial history

A new grant and an augmentation do different things. An augmentation adds an honourable element to existing arms, so the resulting composition has a before and an after. The College’s Wellington account presents a particularly useful case: a Union Badge augmentation was granted and recorded in February 1813, and a later record placed it differently within the quartered composition used for display.[^eng-wellington-augmentation] The addition’s meaning and placement have to be studied together.

The case changes how we read a succession of images. If one drawing lacks the addition and another includes it, they may record different stages rather than a correct image and a careless copy. If two later drawings place it differently, a documented adjustment may explain the variation. The grant-book references supplied by the College give those alternatives a way to be investigated. Chronology, operative wording, and the intended display context can resolve questions that a resemblance test leaves open.

The new component also introduces a different kind of memory into inherited arms. An ancestral coat identifies a relationship transmitted from an earlier bearer; an honourable addition can commemorate a particular act or recognition. The composition can therefore carry genealogical and commemorative statements at once. A viewer may need the institutional record to know which visible feature belongs to which history. The shield’s apparent unity does not make all its elements contemporary or all its meanings hereditary in the same way.

For an invented design exercise, begin with the bridge coat and add a contrasting chief carrying a small commemorative device. Explain which features remain from the original and which have been added, then identify the record a real researcher would need to justify the addition. The exercise is about compositional consequences rather than granting ourselves an honour. Its value is that the reader can see how a change preserves an identifying core while making a new statement conspicuous.

## Corporate continuity needs an institutional pedigree

A corporation persists through changing members, which makes its armorial continuity different from a person’s descent. A university, council, company, or other body can use arms to identify the institution rather than every individual employed by it. The bearer is therefore an organized body whose constitution, legal continuity, and recorded armorial act matter. A member’s departure does not automatically divide the institution’s arms among former colleagues.

Consider an invented council with a grant, followed decades later by an administrative reorganization. One successor body uses the old design unchanged; another incorporates a component into newly recorded arms; a museum keeps a sign from the former council. The three uses have different relationships to the earlier bearer. The unchanged image alone cannot tell us whether institutional succession was recognized, permission was given, or an old object merely survived. We need the reorganization record and the relevant armorial act.

This is where a phrase such as “the town’s coat of arms” can be convenient and incomplete at the same time. It may refer to the former corporation, the present council, a historic local device, or a broadly adopted civic symbol. Establishing the actual bearer makes the history more interesting because it lets us explain how identity survives political change. The Manchester case introduced earlier shows why the distinction has consequences when a separate organization’s display suggests a relationship with the corporation whose arms it uses.

Corporate design can also make history legible through a few selected motifs. In an invented merger of two educational institutions, combining their complete achievements might produce a crowded composition. Selecting a principal charge from each could preserve a recognizable relationship more economically, but would require a properly established new design where official arms are intended. The design choice and the institutional act answer different questions: what communicates the merger, and who has authority to record the resulting arms.

## Workshop: reconstruct change without flattening the record

Use an invented sequence with five witnesses: an original grant, a later marriage pedigree, a quartered memorial, an augmentation instrument, and a modern banner. Make a chronology of the changes each witness can establish. Then explain which aspects of the final banner depend on inherited relationships and which depend on a subsequent honour. Identify one feature that could vary artistically without changing the recorded design.

## Workshop: three records, three conclusions

Work with this invented file: a modern printed shield labelled “Montague,” the College’s caption for a 1634 pedigree mentioning Peter Montague in Virginia, and a set of family records that stops two generations short of him. Write separate conclusions about design, historical presence, and modern descent. Identify the single next investigation most likely to change the inherited-entitlement argument.

<details class="research-answer"><summary>Discuss the missing connection</summary><p>The print supplies a modern attribution to a surname. The institutional caption identifies a historical pedigree and a named person in Virginia. The family records support only the relationships they actually document. The unresolved generations remain necessary to connect the claimant to the recorded bearer. Research directed at those people can strengthen or defeat the connection; buying another illustration cannot.</p></details>

For a second exercise, design an invented shield using only two of the applicant’s ten proposed allusions. Explain why those two carry the argument, identify what is lost by omitting the others, and produce a verbal specification that permits two different artists to draw recognizable versions. Compare this with the College’s distinction between the blazon and the painting.[^grant]

## Graduate inquiry: reconstruct one armorial relationship

Select one named bearer documented in an institutional record. Build a chronology of the armorial claim, the source that records it, and at least two subsequent appearances. Ask how the design’s identity persisted across media and whether the later users were connected by descent, office, association, or something else. The project should explain a relationship rather than merely assemble matching pictures.

Use a witness inventory with separate entries for originals, copies, editions, and modern photographs. Where a pedigree is part of the argument, identify each necessary link and its evidence. Where a corporate body is involved, identify the act and date of institutional continuity or change. Finish with the strongest conclusion supported by those records and a specific account of what remains to be established.
