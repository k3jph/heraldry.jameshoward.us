## A kingdom of charters, not just illustrations

Hungarian armorial history can be investigated through the [National Archives' *címereslevél* database](https://aol.mnl.gov.hu/adatbazis/cimereslevel-adatbazis/informacio). The Hungarian term *címereslevél* can be rendered 'armorial letter' or 'charter of arms'; the common term *armális* derives from Latin *litterae armales*. Such documents can record not only what the coat of arms looked like, but who granted it, to whom, in what social context and under what procedural conditions. They may bear a seal, inscriptions, a pictorial coat, and signs of later handling. The archives present their surviving holdings as documents for **diplomatics, paleography, sigillography, law, genealogy and visual history**, not merely decoration.

The National Archives describe a historical period of royal or princely armorial grants extending from **1326 to 1918**. Among the earliest named documents are a 1326 crest grant to Miklós (Nicholas), son of Imre, and a 1332 armorial charter to Kolos, son of Kolos, with respective National Archives locators **DF 262464** and **DL 50508**. The database's institutional introduction traces the increasing issuance of such charters under Sigismund and later rulers. It also warns by implication against taking the number of surviving documents as the number originally issued: archival survival is uneven and the corpus is far from complete.

The geographical framework complicates matters further. 'Hungarian heraldry' encompasses documents of the medieval Kingdom of Hungary, later Habsburg administrations and Transylvanian princes, in territories now belonging to several modern states. A modern national border is not a historically accurate proxy for the jurisdiction issuing a 1634 Transylvanian charter. The historian must distinguish the **date and place of issue**, **historical authority**, **present archive**, and **modern nationality of a locality**.

## 1453: John Hunyadi receives an augmentation

A particularly instructive original item survives as **MNL OL, Mohács előtti Gyűjtemény, DL 24762**. The [National Archives' study of Hunyadi's augmentation](https://mnl.gov.hu/mnl/ol/hirek/hunyadi_janos_cimerbovitese) explains that **King Ladislaus V** issued a charter at **Pozsony on 1 February 1453**, augmenting the arms associated with **John Hunyadi**. The original family device included a raven holding a ring in its beak. The augmentation brought a red lion holding a golden crown in a white field into a quartered shield: the raven appears in the first and fourth quarters, the lion in the second and third.

That sounds like a standard armorial description until one notices the exceptional feature the archive emphasizes: **the charter explains the significance of the new device**. In the royal narrative, the lion represents Hunyadi, who sustained wounds in conflict while defending the royal rights during the king's minority. The lion's raised crown signifies the safeguarding and return of crown authority. This is primary material for studying the politics of symbols, especially because the explanation is not a nineteenth-century dictionary of what lions 'always' mean.

A graduate historian should be precise here. The symbolism is evidence of **what the granting charter claims**, not proof that every contemporary viewer interpreted the lion in the same way. The narrative legitimizes Hunyadi's transition from the governorship, supports his status as perpetual count of Beszterce, and presents an approved account of his relation to royal power. The shield communicates a claim in a political event; the text tells us that the political dimension was intentional.

Another National Archives description notes that the family reportedly continued using the earlier raven arms after the augmentation. If confirmed through independent dated use, that is a striking reminder that the *formal grant* of a more complex design and *actual later armorial practice* need not be identical. A corpus should record both as source-specific claims, not automatically treat the newest approved illustration as the only symbol ever used thereafter.

## 1634: The Arany charter and its later illustration

The Hungarian archive has another remarkable lesson, involving the Nagyfalu Arany family. Prince **George I Rákóczi** issued an armorial grant on **10 May 1634** to Arany János and Ferenc. The original has an exact locator: **Magyar Nemzeti Levéltár, Országos Levéltár, collection R 64, 1st item, no. 325**. The [published archive study](https://mnl.gov.hu/mnl/ol/hirek/zart_sisakon_s_pajzson_kezbe_kivont_kardu/nagyfalusi_arany_szalontai_hajdu) explains the document's text, physical condition and subsequent legal use.

The charter's verbal description includes a blue shield with a severed arm resting on a gold crown and holding a bloodied sword. The manuscript originally left space for an illustration that was filled in later. The later painter altered details relative to the description: the illustration added a sword-bearing arm as a crest even though the text does not specify it, and showed an open barred helmet where the wording describes a closed one.

This is not an abstract warning that medieval artists could disagree with authors. It is a **specific identified document** in which the heraldic image and its associated wording differ. Which version is a historian studying when referencing 'the Arany coat'? Is the later painted crest evidence of a changed grant, a family's artistic preference, or an interpretation added by a later hand? The archival account supplies enough information to formulate those questions but not enough to pretend we have answered every one.

The physical document also survives in poor condition, having lost its seal and suffering severe damage to part of the parchment. The presence of such an artifact in an archive is evidence of custody and a historical claim; its condition shapes what its original text can still establish. A modern photograph may omit or conceal some deterioration. The edition must distinguish original lettering, damaged spans and any modern restoration or reconstruction.

## The Arany family's thirty-seven-year legal dispute

The most important part of this episode comes after the charter. The Arany family sought recognition of noble status through a prolonged series of proceedings from **1778 to 1815**. The archive traces multiple jurisdictions, conflicting findings and documentary attempts to establish entitlement.

A crucial distinction emerged: the family possessed the authentic grant, and certain authorities accepted elements of its history, but later claimants struggled to demonstrate an unbroken documentary descent from the named seventeenth-century recipients. Early county findings favored the claim, while later higher authorities and proceedings insisted on closer proof. After decades of appeals, the attempted confirmation did not succeed.

This is better history than merely telling students a coat of arms is not automatically 'owned' by everyone of a surname. It teaches why **a valid original instrument** and **a particular individual's claim to inherit its effects** must be evaluated separately. Even a real, signed and preserved grant is not a genealogical bridge spanning missing generations.

It is tempting to tell the story as a simple moral lesson about overzealous bureaucrats. The archive itself notes questions about the justice of the outcome, but our historical problem is more demanding: which bodies claimed competence, what evidence did they require, how were local and central decisions related, and which missing links mattered? A reader should study the documented proceedings rather than decide the legal answer solely by sympathy for the family or by the survival of the charter.

The Arany materials are particularly poignant because of their association with the poet **Arany János**. That literary connection adds interest; it does not exempt genealogical claims from examination. A serious source study should not assert unproven descent just because the poet shares a family name with the historical recipients.

## Source language and historical jurisdiction

| Original term | Working English | Why the choice matters |
|---|---|---|
| *címer* | coat of arms | A design, not a statement of legal entitlement |
| *címereslevél* | armorial charter | Documentary instrument with date and issuing authority |
| *armális* | armorial letter or charter | Latin loan; genre-specific |
| *címerbővítés* | augmentation of arms | An official modification, not necessarily replacement in actual daily use |
| *nemességvitató per* | contested nobility proceeding | Historical procedural terminology, not identical to a modern civil suit |
| *leszármazás* | descent | Documentary link must be proved in context |
| *zárt sisak* | closed helm | Vital to the distinction between Arany's text and later painting |
| *kihirdetés* | proclamation or formal announcement | May carry procedural significance for a grant |
| *DL / DF / R 64* | archival series identifiers | Keep unmodified when citing records |

Hungarian charter descriptions often have an underlying Latin witness, and a modern archive's Hungarian prose may itself summarize or translate that Latin. A translator must identify which language and document is being translated, especially when the web article embeds a quotation from a modern Hungarian translation of an original Latin grant.

## Undergraduate and graduate laboratories

**Visual exercise:** Draw the two halves of the Hunyadi story as separate shields, first the raven-and-ring device and then the 1453 quartered augmentation. Describe exactly what can be attributed to the 1453 charter. Do not substitute a generic stock raven or lion as though it were the documented manuscript artwork.

**Source criticism:** Create parallel columns for the Arany original Latin text, the later painted rendering, and the National Archives' modern interpretation. Record whether the crest, helm type and charged arm are described or depicted in each layer. Note any missing information rather than filling gaps with a pleasing conjecture.

**Graduate assignment:** Write a 2,500-word study comparing an armorial *grant event* with a disputed *entitlement claim* in Hungary and Transylvania. Use the 1453 Hunyadi and 1634 Arany cases. Include their exact archival identifiers, discuss the material condition of the witnesses, and compare what is proved by a royal/princely instrument with what is required to prove descent. Assess how modern archival essays mediate the original Latin and Hungarian evidence.

## The national shield combines signs with separate histories

The dated English parliamentary version of Hungary's Fundamental Law describes a vertically divided pointed shield. One half has eight alternating red-and-silver horizontal areas. The other has a silver double cross issuing from a small gold crown on the higher middle part of a green triple mount, all on red. The Holy Crown rests above the shield.[^hu-fundamental-law-2020] This is a composite arrangement whose components can also be studied separately in earlier witnesses.

Use the bearer's directions in a working blazon: the bars occupy dexter, at the viewer's left, and the cross composition occupies sinister. An English administrative translation may describe left and right from the viewer's position. That is manageable when the convention is made explicit. An unannounced switch between conventions is how two otherwise competent descriptions come to reverse a coat.

Count the horizontal areas as well as the changes between them. Eight areas require seven divisions; the Hungarian formula *hétszer vágott* describes that repeated dividing operation. Translating it as seven stripes loses one. This is a small arithmetic problem with a substantial consequence for the picture. The comparison belongs with the [Academy's geometry lesson](/academy/geometry-of-the-shield/), where partition and charge counting are treated as separate operations.

There are also two crowns to keep distinct: the small crown beneath the cross within the field and the Holy Crown above the shield. The former belongs to the shield composition; the latter is an external ornament representing particular regalia. Removing the outer crown would not automatically remove the inner one. “The crowned arms” can consequently be an imprecise instruction unless the relevant crown is identified.

The [Slovak double cross](/traditions/slovakia/) uses a blue mount and omits the small crown, alongside other differences in the complete arms. The related motifs provide a history to investigate, not an excuse to redraw either national coat from memory. An accurate comparison labels field, mound, cross form and crowns before discussing symbolic ancestry.

## The Illuminated Chronicle is a medieval interpretation

The Illuminated Chronicle, National Széchényi Library Cod. Lat. 404, depicts both striped shields and double crosses. András Hegedűs's study asks how those forms represent the kingdom and its dynasties, and challenges the simple assumption that every red-and-silver striped shield exclusively identifies the Árpád family. His interpretation allows a distinction between signs of royal power and signs of the kingdom itself.[^hu-illuminated-chronicle-study]

That argument should remain visible as an argument. The manuscript supplies images; the scholar identifies patterns, compares other evidence and proposes an account of their meaning. A reader can evaluate the proposal by asking which figures bear each device, in what narrative setting and alongside which other marks of rank. Merely recognizing a striped shield does not decide among dynastic, territorial and institutional readings.

The narrative dates represented in a chronicle must also be distinguished from the dates of its illustrations. An artist can equip an earlier ruler with signs familiar to the artist's own audience. Such an image is strong evidence for how the past was represented when the manuscript was made. It may require additional evidence before it proves that the earlier ruler actually used the same coat.

This is especially important when arms are shown beside Saint Stephen. His importance to later Hungarian kingship gives later artists a reason to associate him with recognizable national or royal motifs. The association itself is historical evidence, but its object is remembrance. Heraldry can organize a political past retrospectively, giving continuity a visual form that the earlier period did not possess in exactly the same way.

A close-reading notebook should therefore record the depicted event, the manuscript's production context and the proposed function of the arms separately. Add a fourth field for comparative evidence, such as a contemporary seal or charter. That structure makes it possible to learn from an anachronistic depiction without mistaking it for a contemporary portrait of armorial practice.

## The 1031 mantle belongs to a different visual system

The Hungarian National Museum's coronation mantle began as a chasuble commissioned by Stephen and Gisela in 1031 for the church at Székesfehérvár. Byzantine silk and gold embroidery support a complex Christian programme; a dedicatory inscription identifies the donation. The vestment was later converted for coronation use.[^hu-coronation-mantle] It is a major object for understanding royal visual culture before mature hereditary armory.

The date invites a tempting but incorrect shortcut: an old royal object must contain the earliest national coat. The mantle instead requires attention to donors, sacred figures, inscriptions and liturgical form. A royal portrait or cross is not automatically a shield of arms. The question is how the object identifies its patrons and communicates kingship using the conventions available to its makers.

Its later ceremonial use adds another layer. An object originally donated for worship became regalia associated with the continuity of royal inauguration. A historian can consequently ask about both the eleventh-century commission and the later uses that made the textile a coronation mantle. Those uses do not retroactively transform every embroidered figure into a heraldic charge.

The change in form also reminds us that a surviving object may have undergone physical alteration. A museum image shows the textile as it survives, not necessarily its original outline or arrangement. The current object, the reconstruction of the chasuble and the history of coronation use require different captions. Even an exceptionally old and well-documented textile will not answer every question from a single photograph.

Read this case alongside the [Bayeux Tapestry](/objects/bayeux-tapestry/). Both objects can teach the reader about medieval identity and public power without requiring every sign to be mature hereditary heraldry. Beginning the story before a system fully develops is useful; projecting the completed system backward obscures the development we are trying to understand.

## A crown can represent statehood in a republic

The crownless form commonly called the Kossuth arms has its own political history. Ferenc Maruzsa's study follows its importance during the 1956 revolution, its brief continuation as a state symbol afterward and the later debate over the restored national form. The choice of the Holy Crown above the shield expressed a claim to historical statehood within a republic.[^hu-kossuth-study]

The resulting lesson is more precise than “crowns mean monarchy.” A crown can refer to a particular institution, an inherited constitutional idea or a remembered political order. A republic may adopt such a reference without installing a reigning king. Equally, a crownless form can acquire associations with a specific revolution that exceed the literal removal of an ornament.

Compare otherwise similar crowned and crownless images. The internal shield may remain recognizable while the political reading changes through an external feature. That is a useful counterpart to Hunyadi's augmentation, which changes the shield's content to express royal recognition of service. Heraldic meaning can be altered at different levels of an achievement; the location of the alteration helps explain its mechanism.

Historical objects should retain the form appropriate to their date. If a medal or poster uses crownless arms, adding the Holy Crown in a modern reproduction can erase the political message of the original. Conversely, removing the crown from today's prescribed state device because the country is a republic substitutes the viewer's theory for the actual model. Interpretation begins with the surviving or authorized form.

For teaching, separate an inventory of visual changes from an explanation of why they mattered. The inventory identifies crown, shield, colour and accompanying slogans. The explanation requires the object's date, issuer and setting. A crownless shield on an undated souvenir has much weaker evidential value for a revolution than a dated poster issued by a documented organization.

## An armorial letter combines a grant with a material object

The National Archives' armorial database makes it possible to follow the grant beyond a clipped shield image. A charter includes a beneficiary, an issuer and an act, alongside its formal description and painted representation. Its seal and endorsements can help establish how the act was authenticated and subsequently handled. Paleography reads the writing; diplomatics studies the document's form and legal function; heraldry interprets the armorial content.[^hu-armalis-db]

These approaches are complementary. A beautiful coat without the beneficiary's name cannot establish who received the grant. A clear beneficiary without the terms of inheritance may not establish which descendants were included. A detached painting can also conceal whether it was part of the original execution or added later. The Arany example in this chapter demonstrates the last problem particularly well.

The beneficiary should be transcribed in the document's own naming form before it is normalized. Given names, places of origin and kinship expressions may not align neatly with a modern surname field. If several recipients are named, map their stated relationship rather than treating the first named person as the sole founder of every later entitlement. Historical naming is part of the evidence, not clerical clutter around it.

The same care applies to jurisdiction. A grant issued by a Transylvanian prince belongs to that issuer's political and legal setting, even when a later family history folds it into a broader Hungarian story. Place names now located in neighbouring states should be indexed under their historical and present forms. This makes the evidence easier to locate while preserving the circumstances of the act.

Finally, distinguish a grant of arms, recognition of nobility and proof of a later person's status. Their relationships vary by document and circumstance. The Arany litigation shows how possession of a real ancestral instrument can leave descent unproved. The mistake in a surname-coat sale is not necessarily that the displayed charter is fictitious. It is that the seller skips the genealogical argument connecting that charter to the buyer.

## Augmentation explains one act rather than every raven

Hunyadi's 1453 augmentation is especially valuable because the grant itself explains the lion and crown through his public service. This gives the historian an unusually direct statement of intended meaning. The claim is anchored to a specific act by Ladislaus V, rather than inferred from the animal's supposed universal symbolism.[^hu-hunyadi-1453]

Keep that explanatory privilege narrow. A red lion holding a crown in this charter can be read through the charter's account. Another lion elsewhere does not inherit the explanation merely because the animals look alike. The proper unit of interpretation is the charge in its particular grant, bearing and context. Universal lists of “what a lion means” usually discard precisely the information that makes this example worth studying.

The augmentation also requires attention to continuity. The raven and ring remain part of the armorial identity as the new fields are added. An augmentation does not always replace the earlier coat; it can reorganize it into a composition that preserves the bearer while stating a new honour. Draw the older coat, identify the added material and then show how quartering manages their relationship.

For a later object bearing the augmented arms, the date of the object and the date of the grant remain separate. A nineteenth-century print can faithfully represent a fifteenth-century augmentation. It is a later witness to that form, however, and its artistry or annotations may reflect its own period. Citation should make clear whether the reader is viewing the original charter, an archival photograph or a subsequent rendering.

## Graduate investigation: construct a four-witness dossier

Use four witnesses: Hunyadi's grant, the Arany charter and litigation account, a selected image from the Illuminated Chronicle, and the dated parliamentary state-symbol description. For each, identify bearer, date of act, date of surviving object, type of authority and the armorial features actually described or shown. A cell for “not established by this source” is part of the work, because these witnesses answer different questions.

Write two connected arguments. The first should explain how heraldry communicates public continuity through the national shield and the chronicle's retrospective images. The second should explain how a particular person or family establishes an armorial claim through a charter and descent evidence. Use the crown and the Arany litigation as cases where a familiar image is insufficient to settle the wider historical issue.

Finish with a caption for the 1031 mantle that identifies its patronage and later function without calling it an eleventh-century version of the present arms. This final step tests whether the comparative vocabulary has become too broad. A strong researcher can recognize an important identity-bearing object and still explain why it belongs to a different visual system.
