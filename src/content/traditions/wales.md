## Four lions on a small fitting

Museum Wales catalogues a gilt copper-alloy armorial mount associated with Owain Glyndŵr, found at Harlech Castle. It may have belonged to a belt or harness. The record connects the arms with Glyndŵr’s assumption of the title Prince of Wales in 1400 and notes his control of the castle in 1404–1408/9.[^mount] A small fitting therefore opens onto a large political claim. Its portability is part of the interest: authority could be presented on an object that travelled with a person or horse.

There are several scales of identity in Welsh heraldry. A prince can make a personal and dynastic claim; a movement can draw on that claim; a modern institution can adopt a historically resonant design. These relationships can share imagery while differing in bearer, authority, audience, and purpose. If we call all of them “the Welsh arms,” we make the picture simpler by discarding the history we came to study.

Wales deserves a separate chapter because a shared modern granting institution does not exhaust its heraldic history. The College of Arms supplies the current institutional setting for new grants in England and Wales.[^college] The surviving Welsh objects and modern public adoptions supply their own historical questions. An administrative boundary tells us where to ask about a grant. It does not decide which histories merit an explanation.

## Glyndŵr’s seal is a political composition

The museum also holds a replica showing both sides of Glyndŵr’s great seal, which it describes as used in 1404–1406. One face presents a seated ruler, with a sceptre and an elaborate setting. The other presents an armoured rider with a quartered shield of lions and a dragon crest.[^seal] The two faces combine different ways of showing princely authority: enthroned rule and mounted martial presence.

A seal operates through attachment to an act. On an issued document, its impression can help authenticate the issuer’s involvement. The design also presents an account of who that issuer is. In this case, inscription, posture, equipment, and arms form a coordinated political statement. A close reading should therefore retain the whole composition. Cropping the shield out is useful for describing the arms but removes other evidence about how the claim was made.

The museum’s replica gives us mediated access to that composition. It is not the medieval matrix, nor an original impression attached to a medieval document. Its record supplies the historical association and date range for the seal it reproduces. A project examining sealing practice would need surviving documents and original impressions as further witnesses. A project examining modern museum presentation would instead study why and how the replica was made and displayed. The object can support both inquiries, provided we state which one we are conducting.

Compare the mount and the seal without forcing them into one story of use. The seal’s formal composition invokes a ruler; the mount carries arms on a fitting whose original function remains uncertain. Their shared association can strengthen an interpretation of political imagery. It cannot identify the mount’s individual wearer or establish that the fitting authenticated documents. The medium supplies constraints on what an emblem can do.

## Lions, dragons, and the problem of equivalence

The lion shield and dragon crest on the seal already show that several prominent devices can coexist in one achievement. A shield, crest, badge, and flag need not repeat one design. Each occupies a different place in the identification system. A reader who recognizes a dragon should still ask whether it is a crest above a helm, a charge on a shield, or the principal device of a flag.

This distinction is especially useful in a national tradition where the dragon has an extensive public presence. Recognizability can encourage us to merge every use into a single origin story. A better account follows dated appearances and defined acts of adoption. The relevant question is how a familiar figure was used to communicate a particular identity on a particular occasion. Its familiarity explains why the communication can work; it does not determine the bearer.

Consider an invented civic design using a dragon above a bridge. The bridge could identify a town through local history, while the dragon situates the town within a broader Welsh identity. That is a layered argument: particular place inside a larger cultural association. Its success depends on an audience that understands both components. If the bridge is too generic or the dragon overwhelms it, the design may communicate “Wales” more effectively than the intended town. National recognizability and local distinctiveness have to be balanced.

## A royal badge for Welsh legislation

In 2008 the National Assembly announced the Royal Badge of Wales for use on published Assembly Measures. Its account describes discussions with the College of Arms and royal approval, and connects the badge with the arms of the native princes of Wales. The first stated use was the published NHS Redress (Wales) Measure 2008.[^badge] Here we have a documented modern adoption with a defined administrative purpose.

The mechanism is instructive. Published legislation needs to identify the authority under which it appears. A historically grounded emblem can make that identity visible while distinguishing the publication from another jurisdiction’s legislation. The badge’s connection to medieval arms supplies historical reference; the modern approval and use supply its particular administrative role. Both matter, but they are different parts of the explanation.

The Assembly’s statement also places the design within the constitutional circumstances of its time. “Assembly Measure” is a period-specific legislative form. When researching current publications, examine the actual document and its governing context rather than assuming that a 2008 announcement describes every later arrangement. A heraldic chapter can preserve a dated institutional decision without turning it into timeless administrative advice.

This case helps us understand continuity more precisely. A modern body can reuse an older design while changing its institutional meaning. The visual quotation invites a historical association; it does not recreate the medieval prince’s government. We can investigate who selected the quotation, which elements were retained, and what function the new use serves. Continuity becomes a set of demonstrated relationships rather than a claim that nothing changed.

## The Church in Wales has its own bearer

The College of Arms records a grant dated 9 December 1954 to the Representative Body of the Church in Wales. The Province of Wales arms place a gold Celtic cross on a blue cross on a silver field.[^church] This is a distinct ecclesiastical bearer with a defined grant. A banner displaying those arms identifies a church institution; its Welsh setting does not make it a general national flag.

The composition gives us a useful design problem. The larger cross organizes the field, while the smaller cross supplies a further identifying form. The reader must perceive both the overall structure and the central detail. Compare this layered composition with the mount’s quartered lions and the royal badge’s administrative use. Each makes identity visible, but each puts the identity into a different relationship with its audience.

The [Anglican chapter](/traditions/anglican-heraldry/) follows this case into a wider comparison of provinces, synods, sees, and clergy. For now, retain the bearer’s full name. “Welsh church arms” is convenient shorthand once the institution has been identified; it is insufficient as the attribution in a catalogue.

These are working English terms drawn from the consulted records. They do not constitute a history of Welsh-language heraldic vocabulary. That history would need dated Welsh texts, manuscript readings, and a separate translation inquiry. Keeping the source-language label honest helps us see the next research opportunity rather than silently claiming it has already been completed.

## A princely composition can outlive the prince’s government

The Welsh cases introduced above bring political identity into the foreground. A princely seal, an armorial fitting, a later public badge, and a church grant all make a bearer visible, but the bearers occupy different kinds of authority. The useful historical question concerns how a design travels among those settings. A familiar composition can retain enough of its form to invite recognition while the act it accompanies changes substantially. Its continuity has to be explained through that change, rather than asserted because two pictures resemble one another.

The quartered lions give us a formal example. A composition with repeated animals in alternating fields can form one recognizable whole. The visual division into quarters does not automatically mean the design is an inventory of four ancestral coats. The Academy’s [marshalling reading](/academy/marshalling/) distinguishes a quarterly field or established composite from a genealogical assemblage. Apply that distinction before assigning a separate ancestor to every lion in a Welsh display. The historical identification of the whole can matter more than a speculative pedigree inferred from its geometry.

The arrangement also creates a practical recognition system. Repeating the animal across alternating fields preserves a strong rhythm even where the support is small or the drawing is compressed. A fitting and a seal can present that rhythm differently because one may use gilding or other surface treatments while the other depends on relief and impression. The design’s relationships help it remain recognizable across those media. The surviving object still needs its own dating and attribution, since formal resemblance alone cannot decide who commissioned or wore it.

A later public institution can select the same historic reference for a new function. That selection is a contemporary act with an audience and purpose. It might present continuity, territorial identity, cultural memory, or a claim to a particular constitutional place. The medieval bearer’s authority is not literally transferred by copying the motif. The modern adoption should therefore be studied on its own terms, with the older design explaining the reference it makes.

## What Glyndŵr’s two-sided seal lets us investigate

A two-sided seal composition can present different dimensions of authority together. The enthroned face introduced earlier supplies a setting of rule; the mounted face supplies a martial figure whose equipment includes armorial identification. Reading the two faces as an ensemble makes the political statement more developed than a caption identifying the shield alone. The seal presents a person in roles, surrounded by symbols, and associated with the act to which an original impression was attached.

We can distinguish three layers in the interpretation. The first records the depicted features: posture, equipment, armorial forms, and inscription where legible. The second identifies their association through the museum’s catalogue and historical witnesses. The third explains how the ensemble presents authority. A strong analysis moves through the layers in that order. It should be possible for another reader to accept the observations while questioning a proposed political interpretation, rather than having both combined in an uncheckable label.

The modern replica is an especially useful complication because it makes that sequence visible. We can study the replicated composition with the museum’s attribution intact. We cannot use the replica’s material as evidence for the medieval impression’s wax, attachment, or wear. An original document with its seal would supply relationships the detached reproduction does not preserve. The replica mediates a design; its own manufacture and display are additional historical events worthy of separate examination.

A comparison with the [Scottish seals discussed in the Arbroath case](/traditions/scotland/#medieval-seals-and-a-collective-political-act) then asks what sealing did within each particular act. One ensemble authenticates collective participation; another princely composition presents the issuer’s authority. The visual language can overlap while the political arrangements differ. That comparison gives the reader a stronger account of medieval practice than deciding that every impressive seal represented sovereignty in exactly the same way.

## The dragon’s history contains both evidence and explanatory stories

The dragon is a powerful Welsh identifier because audiences encounter it in many settings. Museum Wales’s national-emblems account distinguishes legendary associations from later dynastic and public uses, including its Tudor context and modern flag recognition.[^wal-national-emblems] These kinds of evidence should remain visible in the explanation. A traditional story can tell us how people understood a symbol without establishing the date of its first actual armorial use. A dated administrative record can establish an act without explaining every earlier meaning.

That distinction allows legends to remain interesting. An account linking a ruler to a dragon may present bravery, kingship, opposition, or a remembered relationship to place. Those meanings belong to the story’s historical reception even where the narrated event cannot be verified. The mistake is to ask the story to establish an early surviving shield or a continuous official design when it supplies neither. Study who repeated the account, in what language and medium, and for what audience; the result is a history of meaning rather than an invented artifact history.

Visual conventions also deserve close reading. A dragon used as a crest above a helm, a dragon within a badge, and a dragon occupying a flag’s field can share a subject while differing in design and function. Wing shape, posture, limbs, and surrounding elements may matter to particular specifications. A modern familiar drawing can be useful for comparison but should not be silently inserted into an older seal where the surviving features differ. The national association does not eliminate formal variation.

The lion and dragon therefore need not compete for one slot labelled “the true Welsh symbol.” A shield, crest, badge, and national flag can carry different references within a related visual culture. Their coexistence is part of the tradition to explain. The researcher’s task is to identify which composition was used by which bearer, and how its audience understood the relationship. Asking every object to elect one permanent national mascot is a rather efficient way to lose the history.

## The 1959 flag direction gives the distinction a date

The parliamentary answer of 23 February 1959 makes a particular administrative change explicit. It states that government buildings should fly the Red Dragon on green and white rather than the flag carrying the augmented Royal Badge, while the augmented badge continued for other purposes.[^wal-hansard-flag] This is a valuable record because it distinguishes two forms and their uses within one dated decision. It does not require us to guess that every related dragon composition was interchangeable.

The mechanism is standardization for a defined setting. A government-building flag needs a form that can be recognized and produced consistently. The direction selects which version should occupy that setting. A related badge retains another function. We can therefore investigate how visual clarity, official identification, and existing forms were managed together. The act concerns which sign appears where, rather than a universal banishment of every alternative dragon design.

This also improves the interpretation of photographs. A dated image of a building might show one form before the direction and another afterwards. The administrative record gives a hypothesis about the change, while the images establish what appeared at that location on the recorded dates. If a photograph lacks a date or shows an obscured flag, the direction cannot manufacture the missing observation. Use the documentary act to frame the investigation, then inspect the actual witness.

Compare the case with the 2008 badge adoption for Assembly Measures. Both records define an official use, but they concern different supports and administrative circumstances. The flag identifies through a building’s public display; the badge identifies through a published legislative instrument. A wider history of Welsh public symbolism can connect them while preserving their dates, bearers, and functions. That is more informative than treating the newer act as simply another appearance of an unchanged timeless emblem.

## Welsh pedigrees and the work of named compilers

Family history is another major part of the Welsh armorial record. A pedigree can connect people, places, and arms, but the surviving manuscript may have passed through several stages of compilation and revision. The National Library of Wales record for NLW MS 1502E is a concrete example. It describes a later manuscript copy of Meyrick’s 1846 edition of Lewys Dwnn’s visitations of 1586–1613, with corrections and additions by Hugh Robert Hughes.[^wal-dwnn-witness] The title’s early dates describe the underlying inquiries; they do not turn this later copy into the original visitation witness.

Each stage contributes something different. The inquiry gathers or records claims in its own circumstances. The edition selects and presents an earlier text. The manuscript copy transmits the edition. The annotations add another scholar’s interventions. A modern catalogue locates and describes the resulting object. A convenient citation can retain the important stages without narrating the entire archival history every time, but the researcher must know which stage supplies the assertion being used.

Suppose an invented pedigree in the base text names a parent, while a later annotation supplies a different parent. The discrepancy is information, not clutter to remove for a cleaner family tree. It could reflect additional evidence, a correction, a competing identification, or an annotator’s mistake. Record both readings and their attribution, then investigate the witnesses supporting them. Selecting the version that produces the most impressive quarterings would turn the armorial outcome into a criterion for choosing historical facts.

This is also where language and names matter. Retain the name forms actually present in the witness and supply normalized search forms separately. Similar-looking names can refer to different people; variant spellings can refer to one person. A pedigree’s localities, dates, relationships, and documentary basis help determine which situation applies. The final armorial claim should follow the established identity of the people, rather than using a matching shield to force them into one line.

## Modern reconstruction is part of the tradition too

Museum Wales’s Llywelyn Banner supplies a modern making history with unusually useful detail. The record credits work for Llys Llywelyn under Cefyn Burgess’s guidance and describes four lion panels with a vine-pattern border inspired by Siwan’s carved coffin.[^wal-llywelyn-banner] This is interpretive material culture: a modern textile combines historical references through identified choices. Its value does not depend on pretending it survived from the prince’s lifetime.

A reconstruction has at least two historical addresses. One belongs to the period or subject it interprets. The other belongs to the modern act of making it. An analysis can investigate whether the chosen forms are appropriate to the historical setting, how the sources were combined, and why the resulting object serves the display. A catalogue caption should retain the modern makers and date where available. Those details explain the decisions through which the past became visible to today’s audience.

The border provides a particularly good comparison with the armorial core. A historically inspired decorative surround can frame an armorial composition without necessarily becoming a new prescribed component of the arms. To understand the object, distinguish the armorial arrangement, the textile’s physical construction, and the decorative program. The museum’s account gives sources for those choices; a modern viewer should not reverse the process and infer that every new decorative detail appeared in the original prince’s banner.

The same principles apply to reenactment, illustrated reconstructions, and digital teaching designs. A well-researched modern realization can demonstrate scale, mounting, movement, and visual effect that a fragmentary artifact leaves hard to imagine. Label it accurately and explain the source basis. The reconstruction then becomes a contribution to interpretation with its reasoning exposed, rather than a substitute original whose apparent certainty exceeds the evidence.

## Local identity within a recognizable national vocabulary

A civic or institutional design in Wales can draw on national recognizability while remaining specific to its bearer. The constructed bridge-and-dragon example introduced earlier shows the tension. The dragon can locate the institution within a wider cultural association; the bridge needs to distinguish the particular place or body. A design in which the national subject overwhelms every local feature may succeed at communicating Wales while failing to identify the intended bearer.

One way to test the proposal is to remove the supporting caption. Would several different towns plausibly use the same resulting picture? If so, the local distinction may be too weak. Another test reduces the design to the size of a letterhead or a small sign. A tiny unique detail can be formally distinctive while becoming practically invisible. The best identifying choices need both documentary meaning and enough visual weight to reach the audience at the intended scale.

A modern grant’s explanatory allusions can then be examined as a selection rather than an exhaustive history. A council might choose one geographical feature and one historical reference, leaving many others unrepresented. The omissions do not automatically mean the designer forgot the locality. A compact identifying system must select. The useful question is whether the chosen elements communicate the relationship claimed for them, and how the official act defines the composition that artists subsequently realize.

The Welsh comparison therefore includes several interacting scales: personal and dynastic arms, lineage and pedigree, civic or ecclesiastical bodies, national association, and official public uses. The College’s granting remit supplies one present institutional setting, while Welsh objects and records supply histories that cannot be reduced to that remit. A developed account needs all these scales because the same familiar animal can connect them without making them identical.

## Graduate project: follow one Welsh design across a transition

Choose an emblem with appearances on both sides of a political or institutional change. Document at least three dated witnesses and the act, claim, or use associated with each. Investigate whether the design identifies a person, dynasty, territory, institution, or broader cultural affiliation at each stage. A change in the category of bearer may be more significant than a change in drawing style.

Include one material object and one documentary act. Explain how the object’s manufacture and placement affect its audience, then connect the documentary act to the authority adopting or granting the design. The resulting argument should establish a particular continuity or transformation. Reserve broader claims about Welsh national symbolism for a corpus large enough to support them.
