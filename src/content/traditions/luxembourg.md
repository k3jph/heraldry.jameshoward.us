## A tail worth reading carefully

Luxembourg’s state arms place a red rampant lion on a silver-and-blue field of ten horizontal pieces. The official account specifies gold crown, claws and tongue, with the tail forked and crossed in saltire.[^lu-symbols] Those details distinguish a particular lion device from a generic heraldic animal.

The crossed tail is especially useful for teaching description. A forked tail has two branches; specifying their crossing adds a further relationship. An artist who draws two separated branches has preserved the first instruction while losing the second. A correct reading therefore follows the structure of the description rather than stopping once the shield looks familiar.

This chapter adds Luxembourg as its own institutional setting. Its connections with neighboring Low Countries and German- and French-language armorial practice invite comparison, but the national device’s prescribed forms and legal history require local records.

## Count pieces, not dividing lines

The ten-part field supplies another practical distinction. Ten alternating pieces require nine internal boundaries in an ordinary horizontal arrangement. A description counting divisions may consequently appear numerically different from one counting the resulting pieces while referring to the same structure.

For a worked drawing, begin with ten horizontal pieces before adding the lion. Check that the first tincture and alternating sequence follow the description. Then compare the result with the official model. A horizontal pattern is not enough if the count or starting relationship changes.

This is why bilingual glossaries need more than one-word substitution. *Burelé*, “barruly” and a German account of repeated division can describe related formal structures through different linguistic constructions. Preserve the original term and explain what it instructs the artist to do in the consulted example.

## The three forms are related achievements

The government identifies small, medium and great arms.[^lu-symbols] These forms preserve a relationship to the central shield while changing the surrounding achievement. They are not three unrelated national devices that happen to use the same animal.

A catalogue should store the shared shield design and the three prescribed forms separately, linking each to its appropriate description. Then a particular monument or document can be identified as a realization of one form. Without that structure, a simplified image may be mislabelled as an incomplete great achievement instead of a legitimate smaller form.

The same approach aids version history. A new rendering of the small arms can change artistic style without adding the components required for another level. The institutional description determines the form; file dimensions and decorative richness do not.

## A multilingual booklet is a comparison witness

The government’s September 2003 booklet places French, German and English accounts beside one another and identifies the national-symbol framework and heraldic commission.[^lu-booklet] That arrangement allows readers to compare terminology within one institutional publication instead of combining unrelated dictionaries.

Read the description at component level. Which passage concerns the repeated horizontal field? Which identifies the lion’s posture? Which specifies the tail? Build a concordance that explains the relationship, rather than insisting that every language must reproduce identical syntax.

The booklet is also a dated witness. It contains other historical and demographic information that should not be treated as present-day data. A source can remain useful for a defined armorial description while its surrounding administrative or statistical context has changed. Citation needs the publication date and the particular passage used.

## Historical origin and modern protection are different acts

The official account connects the device with Henry V in the thirteenth century, while the booklet discusses its later national-symbol legal framework.[^lu-symbols][^lu-booklet] One concerns historical development; the other concerns a modern institution’s treatment of the national device. They cannot be collapsed into a single adoption date.

For close historical work, seek the medieval seals or images behind the later origin account. For current use, seek the governing law and applicable guidance. A medieval origin story does not authorize every contemporary use, and a modern protective statute does not establish the precise form of every medieval lion.

This distinction also keeps the state arms separate from the grand-ducal house’s wider dynastic material. Shared elements can occur in related identifying systems while the bearer and surrounding composition differ. The next research stage should investigate those records directly rather than treating every crowned Luxembourg lion as one interchangeable device.

## Workshop: translate the operation

Draw two hypothetical tails, one merely forked and the other forked with the branches crossed. Then explain in plain English what the French phrase *queue fourchue et passée en sautoir* instructs. Add a ten-piece field and count the internal lines separately.

<details class="research-answer"><summary>Discuss the formal relationships</summary><p>The description requires both a division into two tail branches and their crossing. The field’s ten pieces produce nine internal boundaries. A translation should preserve those operations, while an official model provides an additional graphic witness. Neither a generic double tail nor an unspecified striped field captures all the identifying content.</p></details>

For graduate work, compare the booklet’s parallel descriptions with the relevant legal witness and two dated material realizations. Record differences in terminology, prescribed form and artistic treatment separately. The conclusion should explain which differences change the design and which change only its description or presentation.
