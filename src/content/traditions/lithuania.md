## The identity of a coat of arms and the form of its reproduction

A useful heraldic research question is deceptively simple: when does a coat of arms remain the same coat of arms? A formal blazon describes a design without necessarily dictating every brushstroke. But an administrative authority may also approve a model for reproducing an official symbol. Lithuanian legislation supplies a particularly good case for studying how these ideas coexist.

The legislative texts published in the official Lithuanian [e-TAR legal register](https://e-tar.lt/rs/actualedition/TAR.55B9E4E382B3/KccWpcqpst/) discuss the state coat of arms, other official coats and heraldic signs. The Act differentiates classes of symbols and addresses the approval of their *etalonai*—official standards or model representations.

An *etalonas* is not merely an aesthetic suggestion. In context, the word identifies a formal standard approved through a legally prescribed procedure. It carries information about the appearance or reproduction of an official device. It must nevertheless be distinguished from the abstract armorial identity described in a blazon. A successful student should be able to articulate both layers rather than pretending that a colour specification alone answers every question.

## A distinctive approval route

Article 10 of the legislation describes the approval of certain coats of arms of counties, municipalities, ethnographic regions, cities, towns and villages. The official text identifies the **President of the Republic** as approving specified armorial standards on the proposal of the **Lithuanian Heraldry Commission** (*Lietuvos heraldikos komisija*).

This is a different administrative architecture from those we have already encountered. Finland's municipal council has statutory powers to approve its town's arms after receiving the National Archives' opinion. The Czech Republic involves the Speaker of the Chamber of Deputies for the right to use municipal symbols. The Dutch procedure describes a royal decree. Lithuania's presidential approval of specified official standards rests within its own legal system.

The point is not to ask which country's method is closest to England. The point is to compare who initiates a proposal, which body reviews its historical and heraldic content, who approves it, where the authoritative standard is held, and how ordinary public institutions may reproduce it.

## Why the law talks about originals and copies

Article 9 of the Lithuanian act addresses approved standard and project documents and their legal role in establishing the identity of official coats and related heraldic signs. The act also specifies how copies of those approved models are preserved. That creates an empirical research question: if a photograph in a newspaper and a vector image on a city website differ slightly, which is an authorized representation and how would one verify it?

A public armorial database should distinguish the approved official model from a contemporary interpretative redraw, an unofficial educational illustration and an historic witness. A university course should not conceal that distinction because it complicates a search page.

The same problem arises when municipal territories change. A historic image may survive from a time before a modern municipality existed in its current form. The date on which an official standard was approved need not be the date on which its imagery first appeared. Do not merge 'origin of motif' with 'date of government approval' into one field.

## A question about cultural continuity

Lithuanian heraldic history extends well beyond contemporary legislation. A complete regional course will need to study the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, the dynastic and political iconography of its state, city arms, noble and Polish–Lithuanian heraldic affiliations, and the changes introduced by imperial and modern state formation.

The modern legal text is a strong primary source for institutional structure; it is not a substitute for medieval seals, manuscripts or scholarship concerning the Grand Duchy. Historians should avoid the inverse mistake of treating every modern municipal coat as a simple survival from a fifteenth-century emblem. Continuities must be demonstrated through dated witnesses.

In the [Poland–Lithuania chapter](/traditions/poland-lithuania/) we examine the 1413 Union of Horodło and the adoption of Lithuanian noble families into shared heraldic groupings. Here the emphasis is on a contemporary Lithuanian state mechanism. The two topics belong in dialogue but should not be collapsed into a single story of one unified, unchanging legal system.

## Vytis is a mounted knight, not a portrait label

Lithuania’s parliamentary account describes Vytis as an armoured rider on a silver horse in a red field, with a raised sword and a small blue shield bearing a gold double cross.[^lt-state-symbols] Horse, rider, weapon and carried shield form a compound figure. A student who recognizes only “a man on a horse” has found the subject but has not yet read the design.

Begin with the direction and motion of the horse, then follow the rider’s posture. The sword must remain distinguishable above the rider’s head. The small shield has its own field and charge, nested within the larger composition. Its double cross cannot be moved into an empty part of the main red field without changing that relationship. This is a practical example of how a relatively complex charge remains reconstructible through a structured description.

The whole figure also demonstrates the difference between an emblematic rider and an identified portrait. A particular historical ruler may use a mounted image on a seal, while later arms employ a mounted knight as a territorial emblem. The historian must examine the transition rather than assume every later rider depicts the same person. An image can preserve a pose after its identifying function changes.

The parliamentary history places that development in the late fourteenth century and discusses the mounted images on rulers’ seals, including Jogaila’s quartered **1386** seal and the subsequent territorial use of the rider.[^lt-state-history] The account gives us a path into dated witnesses. It does not require us to invent a fully standardized modern Vytis on every earlier seal where a horse appears.

## The double cross has a history inside and outside the shield

The Seimas account identifies the double cross on the knight’s shield in Jogaila’s **1386** seal and traces its development as a dynastic emblem. It notes historical changes in tinctures and in the relative lengths of the two crossbars.[^lt-double-cross] This is a useful warning against projecting the present gold-on-blue rendering onto every earlier representation.

A double cross is constructed from one upright and two horizontal bars. Their length, spacing and relation to the upright determine the particular form. A badly reduced image can merge the bars or make one look like a separate object. Draw the structure first, leaving enough space for both crossbars to remain legible, before adding texture or modelling.

The cross’s position creates another distinction. Within Vytis it is a charge on the rider’s shield. Elsewhere it can appear as an independent emblem. A standalone cross does not automatically reproduce the whole national coat, just as a lion extracted from an achievement does not carry every external ornament with it. Its use has to be identified from the object and institutional context.

For a comparison, make three diagrams: the full mounted figure, the small shield with its cross, and the cross alone. Each extracts a different level of the same visual structure. Label the extraction. This prevents a discussion from treating an independent badge and the full national arms as interchangeable merely because the smaller figure appears within both.

## The Columns of the Gediminids belong in the comparison

The parliamentary account identifies the Columns of the Gediminids as another historical sign, represented in gold on red or without a shield. It associates their use by Vytautas with **1397**.[^lt-state-symbols] The sign offers a different visual grammar from Vytis: an abstract linear construction instead of a mounted figure.

Its conventional name should not tempt the artist to draw a group of naturalistic architectural columns. Study the actual linear form. A named sign can resemble an object without being a pictorial illustration of that object. The space between lines and the connections between uprights are part of its identity, especially when the design is used at small scale.

This makes the Columns a useful bridge to the [tamgas chapter](/traditions/tamgas/), where compact linear signs also demand attention to structure, attribution and use. The comparison concerns a visual operation. Similar simplicity does not establish a common historical origin, identical inheritance rules, or membership in one emblematic tradition. Each sign needs its own documentary history.

The coexistence of the Columns, double cross and mounted knight also shows why a national emblem system is larger than one coat of arms. Different signs can serve as badges, institutional marks or components of a larger design. A comprehensive study should identify those relationships instead of treating every familiar national motif as an alternative official shield.

## Restoration included a transitional purple standard

The parliamentary history distinguishes several steps in the modern restoration. A **1990** standard drew on Juozas Zikaras’s **1925** coin design, and its approved coloured presentation used a purple background and purple rider’s shield. A revised standard by Arvydas Každailis was adopted on **4 September 1991**.[^lt-state-history] The sequence is more precise than a statement that Lithuania simply brought back one unchanged drawing.

The purple phase belongs in our discussion of [tinctures](/academy/tinctures/). It is a dated official presentation, rather than an artist’s accidental substitution or a universal rule about Lithuanian colours. A specimen matching that standard needs to be identified as such. Recolouring it red to satisfy familiarity would erase evidence of the transition.

The adoption of another model also lets us separate continuity of emblem from continuity of drawing. The rider can remain Vytis while the approved rendering changes. Direction, movement, accessories and colour relationships are still significant, but the emblem’s historical identity is not reducible to one digital file. A restoration project can claim continuity with a tradition while making a deliberate choice among its documented forms.

This is precisely why the legal concept of an *etalonas* matters. The standard supplies the approved model for a defined use. A historian can compare successive standards as dated acts without declaring one merely a careless copy of the other. A designer working on current official reproduction has another task: follow the applicable present model rather than selecting the most attractive historical version.

## Kaunas: an aurochs survives several jurisdictions

Kaunas’s native-language municipal account traces an aurochs device through medieval seals and later political settings. It identifies an imperial eagle added to city seals in **1801**, a **1843** guberniya achievement used by the city, and the approval of a restored aurochs standard in **1993**.[^lt-kaunas] The history connects one local emblem with changing institutions rather than treating every stage as the same municipal grant.

The distinction between city and guberniya is particularly important. A provincial or guberniya achievement can be used in a city without having originated as that city’s own arms. The bearer identified by an adoption record determines the design’s administrative scope. A later city use becomes another event to document. An illustration labelled only “Kaunas arms” can conceal this distinction.

The animal’s identification also deserves care. *Tauras* refers to the aurochs in this account. It should not become a goat, stag or cup because a translator chooses a familiar word from another context. The cross placed between the horns belongs to the compound figure. Moving it above the shield would convert an internal identifying detail into an external ornament.

For historical comparison, use dated seals and approved models to track posture, head direction and the cross. A change in presentation may alter the animal’s movement without eliminating its civic identity. A seal made under another jurisdiction may add an imperial component while retaining the local animal. The task is to describe what changed and explain its documented context, rather than judge every variant against the latest drawing.

## Kaunas’s great achievement uses a ship as a supporter

The **2008** Kaunas great standard places the aurochs shield within a ship-based supporting composition, with sails and an upper motto.[^lt-kaunas] It is a valuable reminder that a supporter need not be a human or animal standing at either side. The function is defined by the arrangement around the shield and the adopted achievement.

Read the composition’s levels. The aurochs remains within its own shield. The vessel and sails create a surrounding presentation. The motto occupies another external element. Putting the ship inside the shield would produce a different design, even though all the original motifs remained somewhere in the picture. Heraldic structure depends on relationships, not an inventory of ingredients.

The official English municipal page exposes the cost of a poor technical translation. It renders the animal as a “cup” and the supporting element as a “skydiver.”[^lt-kaunas-en] The native-language page and approved model make the intended subjects clear. Those English words should be treated as translation errors, rather than preserved as exotic heraldic terminology.

This is a useful research habit for the whole sourcebook. When a technical description becomes implausible, return to the original, examine the figure, and check the word’s contextual meaning. A confidently written English page can still misidentify the subject. The remedy is a specific correction supported by evidence, not a sweeping assumption that every unfamiliar word must be wrong.

## Vilnius carries a sacred narrative into civic arms

Vilnius’s municipality identifies its current Saint Christopher arms as a design by Arvydas Každailis approved on **17 April 1991**. The saint carries the Christ child through water, using a staff; the municipal account presents the story behind the image as a legend.[^lt-vilnius] A civic bearer and sacred content coexist in the same specified composition.

The relation between the two figures is the identifying feature. The child is carried on the saint’s shoulders, rather than standing independently beside him. Water establishes the act of crossing. The staff contributes to the saint’s posture. A drawing that keeps two human figures but changes those relationships may cease to communicate the intended subject.

The city’s use should be distinguished from a church’s depiction of the same saint. A devotional painting and civic arms can share iconography while serving different institutions. The armorial setting identifies the city; the saint’s attributes permit recognition of the represented narrative. Neither function has to erase the other.

This provides an effective comparison with Kaunas’s cross-bearing aurochs and Galicia’s chalice. Each civic or territorial emblem uses religiously associated content in a particular composition. There is no need to turn that comparison into a claim that all three bearers have the same ecclesiastical status. Identify the institution first, then explain the adopted motif in its local history.

## A document-focused exercise

Read the [official Lithuanian legal text](https://e-tar.lt/rs/actualedition/TAR.55B9E4E382B3/KccWpcqpst/), focusing on Articles 9 and 10. Write a diagram of the Lithuanian Heraldry Commission's role, the President's decision and the custody of official graphical standards. Keep the Lithuanian terminology visible alongside your English account.

Next, identify one real municipal coat of arms with a presidential approval record and a dated iconographic source. Explain what each document proves and which questions about the medieval history of the emblem still remain unanswered. Do not invent a specific municipality's approval if the register cannot be verified.

**Graduate assignment:** Write an institutional comparison of Lithuania, Latvia and Finland. Your method should separate **registration**, **approval of an official standard**, **expert advice** and **the local legislative authority** that uses a symbol. Include the version dates of all three laws and explain why the distinction is important to archives and digital reproductions.

## Translation workshop

| Lithuanian | Working English | Critical distinction |
|---|---|---|
| *herbas* | coat of arms | May concern official state or local arms |
| *Lietuvos heraldikos komisija* | Lithuanian Heraldry Commission | Specialist state institution |
| *etalonas* | standard / official model | More than an informal drawing |
| *savivaldybė* | municipality | Legal public body |
| *tvirtinti* | approve / confirm | Identify which authority has this power |
| *Respublikos Prezidentas* | President of the Republic | Not a hereditary armorial sovereign |
| *herbinis ženklas* | heraldic sign | Not always a coat of arms itself |

A student translating *etalonas* simply as 'picture' may erase the legal significance of an approved standard. Translating every approval as a grant of hereditary arms would create a much larger error.

## Graduate workshop: separate historical identity from a graphic standard

Compare the 1990 and 1991 Vytis standards with one Kaunas civic model and the current Vilnius composition. Create a table recording bearer, adoption date, principal charge, internal attributes, external elements and the particular graphic witness used. Include a note where an English translation conflicts with the native text.

Then write an argument about continuity. Which features support identification of the emblem across changing models? Which alterations belong to a dated official presentation? Which changes would make the design something else? A strong answer will need both structural analysis and an institutional history.
