## A named adoption at Horodło

The **2 October 1413** act of fraternization was not merely a hypothetical arrangement in which forty-seven anonymous families exchanged devices. The [Orbis Lituaniae documentary account](https://www.ldkistorija.lt/fraternisation-in-horodlo-adopted-coats-of-arms-and-the-real-policy/) identifies, among others, **Jonas Goštautas** as receiving the **Habdank (Abdank)** arms from **Jokub of Rogoźno**, a Polish nobleman. It also names **Kristinas Astikas** receiving the **Trąby**, or Three Horns, arms associated with **Mikołaj Trąba**, archbishop of Gniezno. These cases give the political action identifiable participants and a named heraldic sign.

The adoption brought these Lithuanian nobles into a stated symbolic kinship and political relationship. That does not prove biological descent from the Polish donors; indeed, the distinction is what makes the episode so useful. An invented “Jan of Riverford” is no longer needed to demonstrate why a name under a shared *herb* is not a complete genealogy. The dated adoption and its named participants make the same argument with considerably stronger evidence.

## A heraldic family need not share a modern surname

A Polish armorial can group several noble families under one named *herb*. The name identifies the arms, so an index entry may connect a family to a shared armorial identity rather than assign it an exclusive design. The term *ród herbowy*, often rendered “heraldic clan,” describes this kind of grouping. To use the record, we have to distinguish the named coat, the families associated with it, and any separately documented genealogical relationships. Otherwise a database can turn a shared armorial name into a descent claim the source never established.

This is not an invitation to claim that every bearer of a shared *herb* had a demonstrable common ancestor. The historical relationships between armorial use, genealogical descent, noble affiliation and political organization require study rather than assumed biological unity. 'Clan' is a useful explanatory translation, but it has connotations from other European traditions and should always be qualified.

A *zawołanie*, or battle cry / proclamation, may be associated with a named coat, and a *klejnot* is the heraldic crest or jewel. The recurrence of names for coats of arms rather than simply for people is itself an important feature of the region's documentation. A reader who treats *Łodzia* or *Prawdzic* as necessarily the last name of an individual in every record will misread some historical material.

## A particularly instructive political episode: Horodło, 1413

The [Union of Horodło](https://ruj.uj.edu.pl/entities/publication/297a94ba-7633-40bd-ba0a-38f28b493f17), concluded in **1413**, offers an unusually vivid link between armory and state formation. The Jagiellonian University repository's account of the treaty identifies three documentary acts and the heraldic adoption of **47 Lithuanian families** by Polish noble families into shared coats of arms. This is not easily described by the assumption that a coat is always an exclusive, individual genetic marker.

Heraldic adoption served a political and social role within a changing Polish–Lithuanian relationship. A shared armorial device could become an expression of incorporation, affiliation and privilege. Studying it requires attention to the treaty's diplomatic language, the persons actually named, the documents' witnesses and the later interpretations of the unions. Heraldry here is evidence of political integration, not merely decorative ornament added after the legal event.

The fact that early modern and modern armorial compilers later list families under named coats creates a second problem: can a researcher trace the specific fifteenth-century adoption through later documents, or has a later compilation retrospectively organized a more complicated reality? The most persuasive answer will compare diplomatic records with seals, onomastics and later herbarze rather than simply selecting a convenient surname from an index.

## Herbarze are indispensable, but not magical

The [Związek Szlachty Polskiej's reference list](https://szlachta.org/baza-wiedzy-o-szlachcie/zrodla/herbarze/) identifies a substantial Polish-language literature, including Adam Boniecki's *Herbarz polski*, Kasper Niesiecki's armorial work and later compilations. These works perform enormously useful bibliographic and genealogical functions. Their purpose, authorship, era and source methods should be recorded for each claim.

A nineteenth-century edition may be partly reporting far older records, partly organizing prior antiquarian literature, and partly offering the compiler's historical interpretation. Its description of a coat does not establish the existence of every person alleged to have borne it. Bibliographies often give scholars the beginning of a trail, not the last word.

A controlled database must separate the *herb* as an armorial class from individual noble houses using it, named persons, historical attestations, regional variants, and particular illustrations. One-to-many relationships are fundamental: a data model with one unique coat of arms per surname is historically defective in this context.

## Begin with the herb, then identify the bearer

A named Polish *herb* gives the researcher a different point of entry from the familiar question “What are this surname’s arms?” The armorial name identifies a design and a historical grouping; several family lines may be associated with it. To reconstruct a claim, begin with the named coat, then identify the particular person, family, place and document that connect the claimant with it.[^pl-herbarze]

An invented example makes the information model clear. Suppose three unrelated modern surname strings occur under one armorial heading in a historical compilation. The heading establishes that the compiler associated all three with that coat. It does not supply a complete pedigree joining them, and it does not establish that every present bearer of any of the three surnames descends from the listed people. Those are additional propositions requiring additional evidence.

The armorial design also has to be kept distinct from its representations. A seal, manuscript drawing and printed engraving can record the same named coat with differences in style or particular details. Some differences may be variants; others may be reproduction errors. Record the witness rather than silently normalizing every image into the most familiar modern version. The named heading helps organize the evidence, but it cannot make contradictory details disappear.

This gives a useful sequence for research: armorial name, documented historical bearer, dated witness, and then any proposed genealogical connection. Starting with the last step and purchasing a familiar-looking shield reverses the argument. The result may be a pleasant illustration and a very poor history.

## Leliwa offers a compact visual case

Tarnów’s municipal history identifies Spycimir with **Leliwa**, a gold crescent and six-pointed gold star on blue. It connects his acquisition of local property with the city founded under the **1330** privilege, whose surviving document is held in the Czartoryski Library.[^pl-tarnow-history] Here a named armorial identity enters a concrete civic history through an identified person and act.

The crescent’s orientation and the star’s point count supply the drawing problem. A crescent opening upward leaves a space into which the star can be placed above it. The result is not a generic night sky: the arrangement has a defined field, tinctures and relation between the two charges. Replacing the six-pointed star with a five-pointed one may preserve the subject in ordinary illustration while changing a specified heraldic detail.

The case also discourages lazy religious classification. A crescent can appear in a Christian noble armorial context without making its bearer an adherent of Islam. A star and crescent can be interpreted through several traditions, but their meaning here has to be established from the named coat and historical setting. The same visual nouns do not carry one exclusive affiliation everywhere.

For a comparative drawing, keep crescent and star fixed while changing their relative spacing. Which versions remain legible at seal size? Which make the star appear to touch or merge with the crescent? Style permits adaptation, but the recognizable relationship must survive. This exercise develops the practical judgment needed to compare armorial engravings whose proportions vary.

## Tarnów’s seal connects a person with a corporate identity

Krzysztof Moskal’s study, published by Tarnów’s municipal library, discusses an early city seal showing Spycimir with a sword and Leliwa shield, accompanied by a Madonna-and-Child image and heraldic helmet-and-crest elements.[^pl-tarnow-seal] The seal’s composition includes a person, a civic issuer and several forms of identification. Cropping it to the shield would remove much of the argument the object makes.

The figure’s arms can identify the represented founder without making the whole seal his personal signet. The legend and documentary attachment help establish the seal’s issuer and use. This is precisely the distinction between depicted identity and issuing authority. A civic institution can represent its relationship with a founder through his figure and arms.

The religious image adds another layer. Its presence belongs to the seal’s iconographic program and should be described alongside the armorial elements. It does not eliminate the civic function. An object can authenticate municipal documents while displaying sacred imagery and a founder’s emblem. Historical institutions did not arrange their pictures to fit the categories of a modern spreadsheet.

Moskal also records varied place-based and office-related forms used for Spycimir’s name.[^pl-tarnow-seal] The same person can appear differently across documents. A research database needs to preserve the original strings and explain proposed identifications, rather than pretending the modern standardized name was written identically in every medieval record.

## Jelita makes the origin legend visible

The Wilanów museum’s account associates the Zamoyski family with **Jelita** and its three spear motifs. It distinguishes the Florian Szary origin legend from the documented later family history.[^pl-jelita-zamoyski] The distinction is central to reading the coat. A legend can explain how a family narrated its identity without supplying an independently verified account of the design’s first creation.

Begin with the spears as charges. Count them, identify their directions and crossings from the particular witness, and describe their relationship to the field. Then examine the accompanying story. Does the text claim to explain the number, the arrangement, the armorial name, or all three? The questions make it possible to study how narrative and image reinforce one another.

A dramatic battlefield episode can acquire enormous mnemonic power. That makes it an important part of armorial culture even where its historical status remains uncertain. The historian need not choose between repeating it as literal fact and deleting it as worthless. Identify it as a legend, locate the text that tells it, and compare its date with the earliest surviving armorial witnesses.

The same method applies to later patronage. A Zamoyski object displaying Jelita can establish a represented armorial affiliation. Its maker, donor and use still require object-specific evidence. A family’s famous achievements do not automatically become the occasion for every surviving object bearing its coat.

## Horodło was an act of affiliation with political consequences

Robert Frost’s essay for the Polish History Museum interprets the Horodło adoption within the development of a union of political communities, rather than merely as a private dynastic arrangement.[^pl-frost-horodlo] This explains why shared arms matter to constitutional and social history. They can help articulate who belongs to a privileged political community.

The adoption’s logic is different from a claim that two people inherited the same coat from one demonstrable biological ancestor. A political act can establish an armorial affiliation. To explain it, identify the people named, the documents recording the act, and the privileges or obligations involved. The heraldic relationship belongs within that documentary setting.

The act also should not be extended to everyone living within the affected territories. Named participants and the legal scope of their privileges have to remain visible. A statement about an incorporated noble group is not a statement about equal inclusion of every social or religious community. Comprehensive history must ask who was included, who was excluded, and how later acts changed the arrangement.

For graduate work, compare the text of a specific adoption with a later armorial compilation. Can the later listed family be connected through documents to the named participant? Has the compiler supplied a pedigree, repeated another book, or inferred affiliation from a name? The answer determines what the later entry can establish.

## Medieval naming patterns complicate the family search

Lilia Citko’s **2015** study examines names in the Horodło acts, including place-derived forms, patronymics, nicknames and heraldic names. It places the material in a transition toward two-element naming while noting other structures.[^pl-citko-names] The study provides a concrete reason to resist treating every second word as a fixed hereditary surname.

A place-derived identifier can change when a person’s land or residence changes. An office-related form can identify a role held at one time. A patronymic identifies a parental relationship within a naming system. An armorial name can identify another affiliation. These functions may occupy similar positions in a written name, but they do not supply the same information.

An invented teaching record, “Jan of Riverford,” therefore cannot be normalized automatically into “Jan Riverford” and connected to every later Riverford family. The original preposition and context matter. Another document may identify the same person by an office or patronymic. A defensible identification compares chronology, associates, location and documentary roles rather than relying on a matching word.

Preserve original orthography in the data and add a separate normalized search field. The first retains the witness; the second makes searching practical. Add an identification note where two forms are proposed to represent the same person. This makes the uncertainty explicit and permits later correction without rewriting the original document into a modern name convention.

## Długosz’s text and its printed edition have separate dates

The Wielkopolska Digital Library identifies Zygmunt Celichowski’s **1885** edition of *Insignia seu Clenodia Regis et Regni Poloniae* from the Kórnik codex, with Latin and Polish material and Jan Długosz identified as the historical creator.[^pl-dlugosz-1885] The edition is an important route into an older text. Its printing date is not the creation date of every armorial description it contains.

Read its layers separately. The historical text supplies descriptions and assertions. The manuscript witness preserves a particular transmission of that text. The editor establishes a printed presentation and may add notes or other apparatus. The digital library supplies a modern catalogue and reproduction. Each layer can answer questions, but none should quietly stand in for the others.

If an editorial note corrects a manuscript reading, cite it as an editorial argument. If a modern catalogue supplies the author’s name, distinguish that attribution from a surviving signature in the manuscript. If a printed plate interprets a verbal description, identify the plate as a rendering rather than assuming it reproduces an original medieval painting.

This work is slower than extracting the first recognizable shield, but it produces a source that another researcher can check. The aim is to retain enough of the transmission history to explain why a claim depends on a particular edition or witness.

## Niesiecki’s nineteenth-century edition is a layered compilation

The Mazowiecka Digital Library records the **1839** first volume of Niesiecki’s *Herbarz polski* in Jan Nepomucen Bobrowicz’s Leipzig edition. The title explicitly announces additions from later writers, manuscripts and official evidence.[^pl-niesiecki-1839] The book therefore invites careful attribution: not every statement in the later edition necessarily belongs to the earlier author in the same form.

A researcher should identify volume and page before quoting or paraphrasing an entry. Then examine whether the passage is part of the base text, an added note, or a cited earlier source. The compiler’s reference may lead to another witness whose wording differs. A chain of citations is useful precisely because it permits us to discover where an assertion entered the literature.

The illustrations require the same scrutiny. A coloured plate can be a nineteenth-century interpretation of an older blazon. Its visual completeness does not make it an original medieval witness. Compare the plate with the description and record any difference in count, pose, tincture or external ornament. An attractive image deserves a caption that says what it is.

Modern surname indexing adds a further layer. It can make the compilation searchable, but it can also detach a name from its qualifications or from a shared *herb* heading. Return to the full entry before turning an index hit into a claim. The search result supplies a location in the evidence, rather than the conclusion of the research.

## Graduate workshop

Choose one named *herb* listed in a historical *herbarz*, preferably with more than one family attributed to it. Build a relational diagram rather than a linear family tree. For each alleged bearer record the name string, location, document date, nature of evidence, coat designation, visual variant and bibliographic origin. How many nodes could genuinely be connected by documented descent, and how many are linked only by a shared armorial name?

Now compare a discussion of the 1413 adoption in [Jerzy Grygiel's study](https://ruj.uj.edu.pl/entities/publication/297a94ba-7633-40bd-ba0a-38f28b493f17) with a document-focused study such as [Lilia Citko's analysis of the Horodło acts](https://crossroads.uwb.edu.pl/index.php/sw/article/view/1312). Identify where the historical claim rests on contemporary documents and where it depends on editorial reconstructions.

**Graduate assignment:** Produce a prosopographic dataset of at least ten fifteenth-century names with original orthography, later standardized versions, coat-of-arms associations, and specific source citations. Discuss how a database created from family-name indexing can erase political acts of heraldic adoption.

## Graduate laboratory: build an armorial affiliation graph

Choose one named coat and identify at least three documented people or family lines associated with it in a historical reference. Create separate records for the design, each bearer, each document and each proposed genealogical relationship. Label an adoption relationship differently from descent. The exercise’s purpose is to preserve the structure the sources actually support.

Add one material witness, such as a seal or documented object. Record whether it identifies a person, corporate issuer, donor, or represented affiliation. Then add one origin legend as a narrative record with its own source and date. The legend should connect to the coat’s cultural history without becoming an invented birth certificate for its first bearer.

<details class="research-answer"><summary>What should the graph make visible?</summary><p>A shared armorial identity can connect several lines through documented affiliation while their pedigrees remain separate. A seal can depict a founder’s arms yet function as a city’s authenticating object. A later edition can report older material while adding its own interpretation. Each relationship has a source and a type, so the database does not turn visual similarity or name matching into undocumented descent.</p></details>
