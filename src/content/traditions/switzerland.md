## A cantonal collection is not a national grant office

The State Archives of Bern offers a family-armorial collection organized through families’ places of origin. Its guidance disclaims liability and states that arms cannot be officially registered or legally protected in the setting it describes.[^ch-bern] A record in this collection is consequently not the same institutional act as a grant by a heraldic authority.

Switzerland needs a separate chapter because cantonal collections, communal identity and family display require an account of their own. Filing every Swiss example under “German-speaking heraldry” would also overlook the multilingual setting. The useful comparison concerns institutions and uses, not merely the language in which a shield description can be written.

A collection can preserve excellent historical evidence without adjudicating every present entitlement. The archive’s public role and the armorial record’s evidential value should be stated positively, while keeping them separate from legal certification. That distinction helps researchers use the collection well rather than asking it to do a job it does not claim.

## The place of origin does work

The Bern guide connects the searchable family arms with the canton’s places of origin and directs researchers studying the Burgergemeinde’s families toward the relevant library and publication.[^ch-bern] A surname alone is therefore an inadequate key. Local affiliation and the specific family record matter.

For a worked example, imagine two unrelated families with the same name, originating in different communities. A search returning one shield does not establish that both families used it. The result needs to be matched through locality, genealogical evidence and the catalogue’s own scope. The familiar internet transaction in which a surname produces one universal “family crest” discards precisely those distinctions.

Record the collection, entry and historical bearer before connecting a present person. If the entry rests on a drawing copied from an older document, identify that underlying witness where possible. The cantonal archive becomes a route into evidence, rather than a substitute for every stage of documentary genealogy.

## Patronage makes a shield visible

The Swiss National Museum’s account of the Hohenklingen memorial painting connects armorial display with family commemoration and patronage. It also discusses the early-fourteenth-century Zurich armorial roll and distinguishes related family lines.[^ch-klingen] A shield in a church can identify a patron, preserve a lineage’s account of itself and participate in religious commemoration at the same time.

The composition should be read as an arrangement of people, devices and architectural space. Which shield stands beside which person? Is a crest combined with a shield from a related line? Does the placement emphasize the commissioner or ancestors? Those questions make heraldry an active component of the image’s argument, rather than a marginal decoration useful only to genealogists.

A surviving image can still leave uncertainty about an individual identification. Preserve that uncertainty rather than assigning every figure a name because the family sequence seems plausible. The strongest account distinguishes a documented patron from a proposed identification and explains the evidence supporting each.

## Erasure is a historical use too

Benno Schubiger’s museum-hosted research account follows heraldic removal during the Helvetic upheaval of 1798. At Solothurn’s Jesuit Church, reliefs were removed and family arms painted over; twentieth-century work later uncovered or recreated them.[^ch-1798] The political action is evidence that the devices still carried social meaning for those who wanted them erased.

This supplies a powerful corrective to treating heraldry only as continuity. A device’s destruction can identify a contested hierarchy, patronage relationship or political memory. The blank cartouche and painted-over surface belong in the historical corpus alongside intact shields. A collection that admits only readable arms makes the conflict disappear from its denominator.

Restoration then creates a second problem. A recreated relief can recover the earlier design while also concealing the visible damage caused in 1798. The present monument is therefore evidence of the original commission, revolutionary intervention and later conservation decisions. Its layers need separate dates, even when the restored appearance encourages the visitor to see one timeless whole.

## Public and private arms need different evidence

The Bern guidance concerns its family collection; it does not establish a comprehensive rule for every Swiss public emblem or every possible protection of official symbols. Public, cantonal and communal devices require their own instruments and guidance. A careful comparative reference will expand those corpora separately.

Compare [Austria](/traditions/austria/) before assuming that nearby German-language records imply identical rules. A voluntary family collection, a state public-symbol statute and a prohibition connected with abolition of nobility are different institutional mechanisms. Geographic proximity makes their comparison interesting; it does not settle the result.

For multilingual research, preserve the catalogue’s original title and local terminology. A French-language communal record and a German-language family entry may use different administrative categories. The English synopsis should explain the categories rather than replacing them with whichever British word happens to be shortest.

## The Zurich roll joins shields with crests

The Zurich armorial roll, Swiss National Museum AG 2760, is dated by its manuscript description to about 1330–1345 and associated with the Lake Constance region. The catalogue distinguishes its surviving portions from a lost section known through a later copy, and identifies noble achievements alongside ecclesiastical banners.[^ch-zurich-roll] Its geographical and institutional scope makes it valuable to Swiss heraldry without making it a register of one modern national jurisdiction.

The shield-and-crest relationship gives the roll a particular visual grammar. The upper form contributes to the identity represented with the shield rather than serving as an interchangeable decoration. A comparison that records only field and charges discards information preserved by the compiler. The [Academy’s anatomy lesson](/academy/anatomy-of-arms/) provides the vocabulary; the located manuscript supplies the particular arrangements to compare.

The roll’s format also organizes a sequence. A reader encounters designs beside and after other designs on a long support. That differs from the framed independence of modern catalogue cards. Neighbouring entries, names, and divisions can matter when investigating the compiler’s arrangement. The physical witness encourages relationships that an extracted digital image can hide.

The lost portion supplies another relationship: a later copy can preserve information about an earlier design while remaining a different material witness. The copy may be essential to reconstruction, but its colours, labels, and drawing decisions need to be studied as transmitted evidence. The complete historical corpus is consequently larger than the surviving medieval surface and more complicated than a folder of independent shield pictures.

## Armorial glass makes patronage visible

A glass panel can place a donor’s arms in a building that the donor supported. The Swiss National Museum’s account of sixteenth-century practice explains this relationship and discusses Zug’s gift of a panel to Lachen’s town hall.[^ch-glass-patronage] The displayed shield identifies a contributor, not necessarily the owner of the room or the personal arms of its occupants. Location and bearer need to be connected through the actual patronage relationship.

This changes the question we ask of a window. Instead of beginning with “whose family lived here,” ask who gave the panel, to whom, and for what setting. A public donor can be represented in another community’s building. A personal donor can place an identifying device within a civic or religious environment. Several devices in one room can therefore record a network of support rather than a single institution’s expanding achievement.

The material makes that support conspicuous. Light passes through coloured glass and gives the representation a different presence from a shield painted on an opaque wall. The window participates in the room’s architecture while carrying a portable identifying composition. Its later removal to a collection can preserve the glass while changing the relation between device, building, and audience.

A good historical account follows that movement. The original installation explains one use; later collection and restoration explain the object now available for study. Its catalogue location should not be projected back as the setting in which the armorial statement was first made. The object can remain an excellent witness while its surroundings have changed completely.

## Zurich’s 1500 panel presents public relationships

Vitrosearch identifies Lukas Zeiner’s Zurich panel, Swiss National Museum LM 12804, dated 1500/01, as part of the cycle donated for the assembly chamber at Baden. Its composition joins Zurich’s shield with an imperial shield, lions, and banners.[^ch-zurich-glass-1500] The panel is an ensemble of identifying forms, rather than one shield with every pictured feature compressed into its field.

The difference matters for description. A lion beside a shield can be a guardian or supporter in the ensemble, while a lion inside a shield is a charge. A banner held by a figure has its own surface and identifying content. A shield placed above another shield creates a visible hierarchy of arrangement. The student should name these relationships before attempting to write one blazon for the whole picture.

The formal arrangement can then support a historical interpretation of public identity. The panel presents Zurich in relation to a wider political vocabulary and in a setting shared with other donors. That proposition should be developed from the object’s documented commission and composition, rather than from a generic assertion that every imperial eagle on Swiss glass means exactly the same thing.

The work also offers a useful comparison with the [Austrian imperial tabard](/traditions/austria/). Both organize several identifying devices in one larger display, but one belongs to a civic assembly setting and the other to an imperial herald’s clothing. Shared figures make the comparison possible. Their different bearers, uses, and material arrangements make it informative.

## A family shield can appear inside a public ensemble

The Zurich panel dated 1567, Bernisches Historisches Museum BHM 4357, includes an armorial pyramid, lions, a banner, and a Holzhalb family shield. Its record discusses possible identification of the family member and notes replaced glass.[^ch-zurich-glass-1567] The smaller familial device adds a layer to a composition already expressing public identity. It should not be confused with a quarter of the city’s arms or ignored because the larger shields are more prominent.

This is a practical lesson in the scale of identification. One device can identify the public donor or setting; another can connect a person with the commission. Their coexistence allows the object to record participation in a collective institution. A biography of the proposed individual would need evidence beyond the shared family device, especially where several people could have used related arms.

The catalogue’s uncertainty does not make the panel historically useless. Its public composition, date, and familial reference can be studied while a particular personal identification remains unresolved. A careful account distinguishes those conclusions. Filling the gap with the most plausible name and then repeating it as certain would make the study less precise than the object record it relies upon.

The material replacements introduce a further layer. A restored white area can accurately carry an old design while being later glass. Describe the represented composition and the surviving material separately. This allows the researcher to explain both the armorial identity and the conservation history without calling the whole object either untouched or wholly modern.

## Fribourg’s divided shield tests the value of geometry

The 1550 Fribourg panel in Vitrosearch has an attributed maker, Lienhard Jerli, and separate descriptions of Fribourg’s arms and the imperial arms. Fribourg’s shield is divided horizontally black above silver.[^ch-fribourg-glass] The composition supplies a useful example of identity carried by a simple field division. No animal or elaborate charge is needed for the shield to be a defined armorial object.

A student drawing this field needs orientation and order. Reversing the halves changes the specification even though the palette remains black and white. Dividing vertically creates another operation. Turning the divided field into stripes changes the number and rhythm of the parts. The exercise demonstrates why geometry and tincture relationships are central information rather than a background waiting for the “real” heraldry to arrive.

The larger panel can then associate this simple shield with a more elaborate imperial device. Their unequal complexity does not make one more armorial than the other. They do different work in the composition. A reference that privileges only spectacular achievements risks making the cleanest identifying designs appear trivial, when their visual economy is precisely what gives them strength.

The glass record also separates the heraldic metal from material technique. Silver in a blazon does not require the white field to be manufactured from literal silver. A method such as silver staining concerns the production of colour in glass and has its own technical meaning. Similar words can therefore name a tincture in one sentence and a process in another. Their contexts determine the explanation.

## The Swiss cross is a proportioned figure

The government’s account identifies the 1889 specification of a white cross on red with equal arms one sixth longer than their width.[^ch-cross-proportions] This gives the drawing a relationship that can be constructed, measured, and compared. It is more informative than an instruction to place “a plus sign” somewhere on a red background.

For an exercise, set the width of an arm to six units and its length beyond the central square to seven. Construct four equal arms around that square. The overall cross then spans twenty units in both directions: seven, six, and seven. This is a mathematical consequence of the stated proportions, not a separate historical rule. It lets the student verify the figure before deciding how much surrounding red to leave in a particular rendering.

The outer surface adds a different specification. A shield and a square flag can carry the same cross while offering different boundaries. The dimensions of the cross do not alone determine the dimensions of the whole flag or the outline of an escutcheon. Keep the charge’s internal proportions separate from its position and scale on the support. A correctly proportioned cross can still be misplaced or drawn too close to an edge.

Compare the result with an invented cross having longer lower arms or arms extending to the field’s boundaries. The figures belong to different geometric categories. Their resemblance does not make their descriptions interchangeable. Precise construction is a useful form of close reading because it turns words about shape into an inspectable result.

## Family, canton, commune, and federation are different bearers

Swiss armorial culture involves family identities, local communities, cantons, and the federation, as well as other bodies and historic bearers. The Bern family collection introduced earlier represents one kind of evidence within that setting. A family entry’s organization through place of origin cannot be generalized into a complete rule for the state’s emblem or every communal instrument.

The distinction is operational. A family study follows persons and documented relationships. A cantonal glass donation follows a public institution and an act of patronage. A communal identity may need to be traced through local seals and decisions. The national cross’s history concerns military and federal uses as well as formal specification. The devices can appear together while the underlying bearers remain different.

Switzerland’s multilingual setting adds another reason to preserve the original record’s categories. A translation should explain which kind of bearer and act a term identifies. A generic English label such as “family crest” can remove the difference between a personal device, a communal shield, and a donor’s display. The reader needs the category because it determines which relationship is being investigated.

A substantial regional study can connect these settings without collapsing them. Choose one town and trace family devices, public arms, and patronage displays through bounded sources. Explain how their uses intersected: a family member in office, a public donation, or a commemorative commission. The outcome is a history of people and institutions using heraldry together, rather than an isolated collection of designs.

## Workshop: keep the ensemble intact

Choose one of the three located glass panels and describe its complete arrangement before isolating a shield. Identify which devices belong to the field, which sit outside it, and which occupy separate supports such as banners. Then explain the historical relationship connecting at least two of the represented identities. The exercise should preserve the object’s composition instead of treating its surrounding figures as disposable decoration.

For the design component, construct the Swiss cross from the six-to-seven relationship and the Fribourg field from its ordered division. Render each at two sizes without changing its identity. Explain what information the geometry preserves and what belongs to the larger support. Together, the two tasks join material history with an exact reading of heraldic form.

## Workshop: what did restoration restore?

A fictional tourist caption calls a relief recreated in 1982 “an untouched seventeenth-century coat of arms.” Rewrite it using separate fields for original commission, removal, reconstruction and present material. Explain what a modern photograph can establish and what requires conservation documentation.

For graduate work, compare a family-armorial entry, a patronage monument and an erased or restored device from one canton. Ask how the same identifying design changes evidential function across the three. The final study should account for access, collection policy and survival, because the objects available to researchers are already shaped by the history they hope to explain.
