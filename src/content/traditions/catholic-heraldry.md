## Begin with a named pope’s shield

The Holy See Press Office’s explanation of Leo XIV’s arms, published on 14 May 2025, identifies a Marian fleur-de-lis and an Augustinian flaming heart pierced by an arrow with a book. The achievement also includes a mitre and crossed Petrine keys.[^leo] Several kinds of identity are being presented together: a person’s chosen design, a religious affiliation, and the office he holds.

That arrangement supplies the chapter’s organizing model. The shield can carry personal or institutional identity. External insignia can indicate an ecclesiastical office or dignity. A surrounding motto can express a chosen theological relationship. These elements may work together while changing on different timelines. If we treat the entire achievement as one indivisible inherited picture, we lose the mechanism through which an ecclesiastical career can be made visible.

Catholic heraldry also crosses territorial traditions. A bishop may use a locally recognizable armorial style while presenting insignia connected with a wider ecclesiastical system. The relevant evidence may include a national grant, a diocesan record, a Church instruction, and a dated public explanation. The point of comparison is how these layers interact, rather than which country owns the nearest example of a green hat.

## The personal design and the office can change independently

Leo XIV’s stated symbolism connects the lily with Mary and the heart and book with the Augustinian tradition. The motto is *In illo uno unum*.[^leo] The public explanation therefore attributes a particular reading to named elements. A researcher should preserve that attribution before proposing further interpretations from biography or reception.

The external keys make a different relationship visible. They identify papal office in the achievement. A personal shield can persist or develop through an ecclesiastical career while the surrounding insignia change with office. To reconstruct that development, compare dated versions and their official descriptions. A current papal image cannot by itself establish the form used by the same person as a priest or bishop.

The example also teaches source comparison. The Press Office wording describes the book differently from an early Vatican News account, which calls it closed.[^leo][^news] Record the different descriptions and consult the official image and original-language account before producing a definitive transcription. An institutional website can contain several authored accounts; “the Vatican says” may be too broad to identify the witness on which a disputed detail rests.

The analytical point remains secure despite that small difficulty. The book participates in an explicitly attributed Augustinian account, and the achievement’s external insignia participate in an office account. The uncertain detail should be investigated at its actual scale, rather than allowing it to swallow the entire design or silently choosing the wording we prefer.

## A hat is a structured sign of dignity

The College of Arms’ Catholic schedule, approved in 1967, distinguishes hats by color and tassel arrangements. It describes a red hat with fifteen tassels on each side for cardinals, green with ten for archbishops, and green with six for bishops, among other categories.[^schedule] The combination of color, number, and accompanying insignia carries the distinction.

The practical lesson is to count by the stated unit. Fifteen on each side is thirty in total. A drawing may organize the tassels into descending rows. If a catalogue records only the total, it should still preserve the arrangement needed to compare the schedule. A damaged or cropped photograph may hide some tassels; the photograph’s visible count is an observation, not necessarily the intended rank indication.

The hat is also an external component. Changing it does not automatically change the charges on the shield. A person’s promotion can therefore be presented by altering the surrounding insignia while retaining a recognizable personal design. The record needs both a stable identifier for the shield and a date for the particular achievement. Otherwise, several career-stage images can be mistaken for unrelated coats of arms.

Real classification requires the office, period, and applicable rules. Color alone cannot do all the work. A green area in an illustration might be a hat, mantling, or part of a charge; an informal drawing might be inaccurate. Locate the component first, then compare the complete arrangement with the dated source. The structure is what makes the visual evidence useful.

## 1967 and 1969 are different normative witnesses

The published 1967 schedule includes mitres and croziers in several configurations.[^schedule] Two years later, the Vatican Secretariat of State’s instruction of 31 March 1969 addresses cardinals’ and bishops’ armorial bearings. Paragraph 28 calls for a simple, clear heraldic design and directs removal of representations of the pastoral staff and mitre from the bearings addressed there.[^instruction] The dated sequence matters.

A reference work should not combine both witnesses into one present-tense chart. The older schedule preserves a framework at a particular time; the later instruction provides evidence of a normative change for its stated subjects. Historical achievements may retain features appropriate to their period. Present copying of those features needs a current explanation rather than an assumption that an old chart settles the matter.

Scope matters as much as date. Paragraph 28 addresses the subjects it names; it is not a ban on depicting every mitre in every church emblem. A mitre can appear as a charge, in a corporate design, or in a distinct papal achievement. Each case requires its own governing evidence. The reader should carry the instruction’s actual subject forward, rather than enlarging it into a universal rule through shorthand.

This comparison also gives us an operable translation problem. The relevant Latin terms identify the armorial bearing, pastoral staff, and headpiece within the clause. A translation should retain the action directed and its subjects. The chapter offers an English synopsis of the passage, not a new official translation of the instruction. Close legal or philological work should preserve the original text and apparatus.

## Benedict XVI makes a documented change

The Holy See’s explanation of Benedict XVI’s arms states that he replaced the tiara with a mitre in his personal papal achievement and included the pallium. It explains the mitre’s three gold bands and the pallium’s pastoral relationship.[^benedict] This is a particular documented choice within papal presentation.

The comparison with Leo XIV makes continuity visible without assuming every external element must remain fixed. We can identify shared papal components and then compare the dated arrangements. The different personal shields carry different histories; the surroundings express office through related forms. The appropriate unit is the achievement used by a named bearer at a dated stage.

A change in a pope’s personal achievement also does not automatically replace Vatican City State’s arms. Personal, ecclesiastical, and State identities can share devices while remaining separately defined. The distinction becomes especially important when an image is reproduced without a caption. Keys and a headpiece invite further identification; they do not decide the bearer on their own.

## The State’s arms have their own legal text

Article 23 of the Vatican City State’s 2023 Fundamental Law distinguishes the flag, arms, and seal and refers to annexed models. Article 24 replaces the 2000 Fundamental Law and gives commencement on 7 June 2023.[^state] A State emblem is therefore defined through its own documentary act, separate from an explanation of a pope’s chosen personal arms.

The annexes matter because a legal specification can rely on a graphic model. A text-only catalogue should retain the annex reference rather than assuming the verbal summary includes every relevant feature. Comparing the 2000 and 2023 witnesses requires the actual versions and models. A still-accessible explanatory page referring to the older law is a historical witness, not sufficient evidence that the older law remains current.

The seal introduces a further distinction. A central emblem and encircling words can identify an authenticating instrument rather than a general armorial display. Preserve the legend and object type when cataloguing it. The same familiar devices can participate in a personal achievement, State arms, flag, and seal; the document and context identify which relationship is present.

## Workshop: a mitre in three positions

Compare three constructed cases: a bishop’s historical achievement with a mitre above the shield, a church corporation whose shield includes a mitre as a charge, and a papal achievement described in an official 2025 account. Explain why the 1969 instruction cannot be applied to all three merely by searching each image for a mitre.

<details class="research-answer"><summary>Discuss subject, date, and structural position</summary><p>The historical bishop’s case needs its date and the rules applicable then. The corporate case places the mitre inside a design with a different bearer and function. The papal case has its own dated official description. The 1969 passage must be read with its named subjects and action intact. Visual resemblance does not erase those different institutional and structural relationships.</p></details>

For another exercise, compare two dated achievements for an invented bishop whose personal shield stays the same while the external insignia change. Describe the constant design, identify the changed components, and state what documentary evidence would establish that the change corresponds to a new office rather than an artist’s choice.

## Graduate project: one career, several achievements

Select an ecclesiastical bearer with at least three dated armorial witnesses. Reconstruct the personal shield’s history and the office-related changes in its presentation. Use appointment records, official explanations, and applicable instructions alongside the images. Preserve uncertainties in dates or descriptions rather than normalizing them into one ideal achievement.

A second route examines one corporate or State emblem across a legal revision. Retain the relevant text and annexed artwork, compare the actual components, and distinguish a changed law from a changed graphic realization. Either project should show how an ecclesiastical identity is assembled from relationships that can develop independently while remaining intelligible together.
