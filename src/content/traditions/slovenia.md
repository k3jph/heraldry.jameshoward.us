## Start with what the municipal statute describes

A municipal statute may describe a landscape, lettering, or a particular graphic arrangement alongside a shield. Our introductory vocabulary can identify some of those elements without capturing the entire specification. The Slovenian statutes examined here let us test that limit against actual civic designs. Start with what each text defines, then identify which further ordinance or approved image would resolve the details. A limitation in the teaching model gives us a reason to consult the source more closely.

In **Ajdovščina**, a [2012 municipal statute](https://pisrs.si/pregledPredpisa?sop=2012-01-1843) describes the municipality's arms, flag and seal in Article 7. It explicitly calls the coat a *grb*, and offers visual instructions involving a shield, triangular upper features, battlement-like shapes, repeated wavy lines and the placement of the municipality's name.

In **Mežica**, a [2025 statute](https://pisrs.si/pregledPredpisa?sop=2025-01-2085) describes an emblem whose imagery includes Mount Peca, a ring of nearby hills, mining hammers and the stylized legendary King Matjaž. The same provision explains the municipal flag and official seal. The statute was adopted on 18 June 2025, published on 4 July and came into effect on 19 July, according to the official Slovenian legal portal.

Each municipality has made a statement of corporate identity through local law. Yet their descriptions involve shapes, settings and graphic devices that are not all straightforwardly representable in our introductory British-influenced shield generator. Instead of marking the municipalities as 'wrong', the course should ask what the statute defines, which visual material is normative, how the historical symbols are explained and whether a more conventional blazon exists in a separate local ordinance.

## Ajdovščina: the shield, the flag and the seal

The Ajdovščina text provides an opportunity to study the relationship among three official artifacts. It describes the **grb**, then places that emblem in a flag design, then identifies the coat as the central content of seals used by municipal departments. The statute is the key primary legal document for the description it contains; it is not necessarily the last or most detailed administrative account of authorized reproduction.

Article 7(4) explicitly refers to a separate **odlok**, an ordinance that determines the form, contents and use of the arms and flag in greater detail. That distinction is essential. A reader who stops at the higher-level municipal statute may not have the final design specification. One should record the authority of the statute, identify the further ordinance, compare their dates and check whether the current legal portal lists amendments.

The coat's shape itself invites a semantic question. It is described in terms of a *španski ščit*, literally a Spanish shield shape, with a semicircular base. The top includes a geometric form with battlement-like elements. Does the phrase describe the general silhouette of the coat, a charge within it, or the artistic framing of a symbol? A translator who automatically maps every geometric noun onto a familiar English ordinary may fail to reconstruct what the text actually specifies.

A proper digital art exercise should begin by translating the Slovenian source sentence, sketching only the visible structural elements unambiguously described, then comparing the sketch with an authoritative approved image. Disagreements should be written down rather than hidden by artistic improvisation.

## Mežica: geography, mining and folklore in a coat

The Mežica coat is more explicitly interpretive. Its [municipal statute, Article 6](https://pisrs.si/pregledPredpisa?sop=2025-01-2085), describes a light-blue field, a grey lower field with a large pale-green area for the municipal landscape and **Mount Peca**, five smaller dark-green areas representing surrounding hills, a stylized figure of **King Matjaž** and crossed mining hammers.

These are not universal heraldic symbols with one fixed meaning. The source itself states the interpretation intended by the municipality. The mountain and surrounding hills encode topography; the hammers refer to local mining history; King Matjaž reflects regional legendary culture. The presence of such explanations makes the statute valuable for historical interpretation of an actual administrative graphic, but they remain a contemporary institutional account of symbolism.

A heraldic critic might object to the use of many colours, a scenic composition, and lettering within a shield. Such criticism could be a legitimate discussion of traditional design principles. It is not the same as proof that the municipality has no legally defined emblem. One must distinguish **an aesthetic appraisal**, **historical continuity**, and **the text of current local law**. They answer different questions.

Similarly, the presence of a legend in public symbolism does not establish the literal historicity of the legend. It tells us what the city invokes in its public identity. This creates an opportunity for interdisciplinary study of folklore and civic visual culture.

## The legal and historical research gap

Slovenian municipal practice grew through several political settings, including Habsburg rule, the Kingdom of Yugoslavia, socialist Yugoslavia and independent Slovenia. A history of any present-day municipal emblem should not assume an uninterrupted medieval local identity merely because today's statute names an ancient town or a legendary figure.

A strong archival investigation would search predecessor municipalities, seals, urban privileges, nineteenth-century heraldic collections, socialist-era graphic standards and independent-era adoption documents. The question 'When was the coat adopted?' must specify whether it means first attested use, formal modern legislative approval or the date the current visual model was published.

The official Slovenian legal portal is valuable because it provides precise dates and texts for local statutory acts, but legal databases do not necessarily give a ready-made history of their symbols. That history must be built through evidence beyond the current statute.

## Triglav is a prescribed figure in a modern state coat

Slovenia's national arms place a white representation of Triglav on blue, with two blue wavy lines below and three gold six-pointed stars above, arranged as a point-down triangle. Red borders the shield's sides. The government supplies geometric and colour rules as part of the model.[^sl-national-symbols] This gives us a composition that can be read heraldically and also requires a particular modern graphic construction.

Start with the relationships. The stars are three, not a scatter of unspecified celestial charges. Their arrangement has two above and one below. The mountain figure sits between stars and waves, and the red edging does not simply form an ordinary complete bordure of constant width. A familiar English phrase can be a useful working description without matching every feature of the prescribed artwork.

The waves illustrate why a charge's shape and interpreted meaning should be recorded separately. The official explanation associates them with sea and rivers. The image gives a repeatable pair of lines; the text assigns their national significance. A reader should not infer that one line represents a named river and the other a named sea unless a source actually makes that allocation.

The official account identifies sculptor Marko Pogačnik as designer and reports his interpretation of the coat as a cosmogram.[^sl-symbols-designer] That term belongs to the documented design programme. It is not a universal heraldic category requiring every national coat to possess the same spiritual scheme. A modern coat can be shaped by its designer's distinctive reasoning while remaining a public symbol governed by formal standards.

Compare the national figure with a photograph of Triglav. The charge need not reproduce a view from one precisely identified summit or valley. Its purpose is to make a recognizable, reproducible sign within a shield. The photograph supplies topographic evidence; the prescribed model supplies the armorial figure. A successful comparison explains what the simplification preserves rather than accusing the symbol of failing as a landscape photograph.

## A three-dimensional rendering has its own instructions

The government's national-symbol page also describes a three-dimensional treatment with an upper concave and lower convex section and a transition related to the Triglav figure. It requires the graphic lines to remain governed by the geometric rule.[^sl-national-symbols] This is unusually concrete guidance for a carved, moulded or cast public emblem.

A relief cannot rely on colour in the same way as a printed page. Depth, edge and light may carry distinctions that paint supplies in a flat version. The official instructions acknowledge the medium while preserving the model's structure. Material adaptation does not mean the sculptor can invent a fourth star because the upper field appears empty from one angle.

When studying a relief, photograph it in a way that records the form rather than only its dramatic shadows. Note whether colour is present and whether a highlight has made a blue wave appear white. A single photograph under strong side lighting can exaggerate depth or hide an edge. Comparison with the prescribed flat image helps identify the feature; the object itself remains necessary to understand the three-dimensional execution.

This is a useful extension of the Academy's distinction between arms and emblazonment. The national device can persist across print, screen and relief, while a technical rule controls how a specific medium realizes it. The term “artistic freedom” is most useful after the prescribed features have been identified. Without that first step, it can become a polite name for drawing something else.

## Ljubljana's dragon rests on a very specific tower

Ljubljana's detailed municipal description places a green triple mount at the base of a red shield, with silver walls and tower, a raised gold portcullis and a green dragon above. The dragon has raised bat-like wings and a curled, arrow-ended tail. The description even locates its claws on particular merlons; the gold edge is treated as decorative.[^sl-ljubljana]

This precision gives the city coat more structure than “a dragon on a castle.” The creature's posture relates to the architecture, and the architecture relates to the ground. If the dragon is enlarged until it floats above the tower, the rendering has changed that relationship. If the walls disappear behind a logo-sized dragon silhouette, the result may function as a separate city symbol without reproducing the complete arms.

The city's discussion recognizes the dragon as a symbol in its own right, associated with strength, courage and greatness. Its appearance on the Dragon Bridge is another public use. Distinguish that emblematic dragon from the full shield when captioning an image. A dragon sculpture can participate in the city's identity without becoming a three-dimensional coat of arms by virtue of being green.

The gold edging provides a good technical test. The city expressly distinguishes decoration from the coat's essential content. Therefore, not every visible line around an official drawing should be promoted to a heraldic bordure. To decide whether an edge belongs in a blazon, consult the description and approved model. Graphic containment and heraldic content can occupy the same visual boundary while having different status.

For a drawing exercise, reconstruct the described walls and tower before adding the dragon. Count merlons and position the gate. Then draw the dragon in relation to the supporting structure. The procedure exposes which details are structural and which can vary in the artist's line. It also rewards a closer reading of the Slovenian description than a ready-made English label would encourage.

## The dragon's history is not the date of a founding legend

Ljubljana Castle's historical leaflet distinguishes thirteenth-century seals featuring walls and a defensive tower from the dragon's later appearance. It notes a fifteenth-century dragon resembling a crocodile and cites Valvasor's 1689 description of a green creature on a white tower.[^sl-dragon-history] The civic sign changed; the dragon was not necessarily present whenever the fortified-city motif was present.

That sequence gives the historian something measurable: dated witnesses to additions and changes. It also leaves room for a separate history of legends explaining the dragon. A story about an ancient hero can become part of local identity without proving that the charge appeared on a medieval seal at the story's supposed event date. Legends and seals answer different chronological questions.

The crocodile resemblance is instructive rather than embarrassing. Historic artists could render fantastic creatures through forms familiar from their visual vocabulary. A heraldic dragon does not require identical anatomical treatment in every century. The comparison should identify continuity in position and function alongside changes in body, wings and tail. Modern standardization often narrows a range of older representations.

An exhibition could therefore place the early fortified seal, the later dragon-bearing image and the present approved coat in a chronological sequence. The caption should say what each witness actually supplies. A modern reconstruction of an early seal needs its reconstruction credit; Valvasor's written description needs its text date. The sequence becomes more persuasive when it does not conceal the differences between its sources.

## Kranj's eagle is connected to a dated seal model

Kranj's 2022 ordinance prescribes a red displayed eagle on silver and identifies a seal matrix of 1530 as the historical basis for its figure, proportions, shield and colour combination. It also specifies permitted colour, monochrome and relief treatments.[^sl-kranj-2022] The dated ordinance is a modern act referring to an older witness.

The eagle should not be merged with every other regional eagle. A red eagle on silver differs from a blue eagle on silver or a black eagle on gold before any historical interpretation is attempted. Record the field and charge tinctures, the head's orientation and any attributes. The charge family connects the cases for comparison; the complete coat distinguishes the bearers.

The relationship to a seal matrix also needs a careful material reading. A matrix is the tool from which an impression is made, and its engraved orientation may be reversed relative to the resulting seal. If studying a photograph of the matrix, establish whether the publication shows the matrix, an impression or a reversed image. The ordinance's reference to a historical model is a starting point for that investigation.

A monochrome historical witness cannot independently supply colour in every circumstance. When a later regulation identifies both a seal model and a colour combination, ask what additional evidence supports the tinctures. A painted armorial, surviving civic textile or earlier written description may contribute. The red-and-silver present specification is clear; a history of how it was established requires its own documentary chain.

This case belongs beside Ajdovščina and Mežica, rather than replacing them. The three municipalities show different kinds of locally prescribed visual content: an eagle linked to a seal, architectural and watery geometry, and a landscape with mining and folklore. Their common public status does not force their imagery into a single medieval formula.

## The archive records the prescribing public bodies

The Archives of the Republic of Slovenia describes an electronic record of public symbols, arms, flags and seals organized by the legal bodies prescribing them. Entries include registration details and the date and number of the relevant instrument, with accompanying documentation.[^sl-public-symbols-register] This is an administrative route into the history of public identity.

Its scope should remain explicit. A record of symbols used by state bodies, courts, administrative units and municipalities does not establish that every private family coat in Slovenia has been granted or entered there. The archive's role is to preserve and organize the specified public records. Calling it a universal heraldic authority would expand the claim beyond the description.

For a municipal case, connect the statute or ordinance to the archival record through the public body's name and the instrument number. An approved drawing can then be tied to the act prescribing it. If the municipality later revises its standards, retain both versions with dates. A current web image cannot explain the revision on its own, and an old statute cannot establish a subsequent graphic update without another record.

The archive also makes seals part of the inquiry. A round stamp with the arms and an institutional legend is a particular administrative format, rather than just a cropped version of a flag. Compare the legend, issuing body and official purpose. The document to which the seal is attached can establish who acted and in what capacity, which the emblem alone may not identify sufficiently.

## Graduate comparison lab

Read Ajdovščina's [Article 7](https://pisrs.si/pregledPredpisa?sop=2012-01-1843) and Mežica's [Article 6](https://pisrs.si/pregledPredpisa?sop=2025-01-2085) in Slovenian. Prepare parallel English summaries that preserve the original terminology for coats, flags, seals and ordinances. Create two visual specification tables. Avoid using the introductory Atelier to silently omit elements it cannot model.

**Graduate assignment:** Compare the two statutes in 1,500 words. Ask whether their designs function primarily as traditional heraldic compositions or as modern local graphic emblems. Describe what each statute **actually authorizes**, what further normative ordinances must be located and what historic artifacts would be necessary to assess claims of continuity. Include a method for asking local residents and archives about the cultural meanings of Mount Peca, King Matjaž and the Ajdovščina motifs.

## Graduate investigation: test three descriptions against drawings

Prepare annotated working drawings of Slovenia's national arms, Ljubljana's municipal coat and Kranj's eagle from the cited descriptions. Mark every feature determined by the text and every feature that required consultation of an approved image. For Ljubljana, distinguish the decorative gold edge from essential shield content. For the national arms, identify the specified star arrangement and partial edging rather than substituting a generic bordure.

Then compare one historical witness for Ljubljana or Kranj with the modern model. Keep the witness date, image date and prescribing instrument date in separate fields. Explain what changed, what remained recognizable and which historical meanings the available evidence actually supports. A later civic explanation may be important to reception even where it cannot prove medieval intention.

Finish by locating the relevant public-body record or identifying what must be requested from the archive. Your dossier should join formal analysis to administrative evidence. The strongest result will explain how a coat can preserve historical associations while being defined through contemporary law and graphic standards. That combination is a central part of the Slovenian tradition represented by these cases.
