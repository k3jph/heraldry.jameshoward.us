## A book that can actually be located

The Portuguese *Livro do Armeiro-Mor*, compiled and illuminated in **1509** during the reign of Manuel I, gives us a dated manuscript through which to investigate Portuguese heraldry. The national archive, the [Torre do Tombo](https://antt.dglab.gov.pt/), identifies the historical manuscript with the royal papers, *Casa Real, Cartório da Nobreza, livro 19*. Once we have a specific catalogue identification, a heraldic image ceases to be an anonymous web decoration and becomes a historical object capable of citation.

The manuscript is also a reminder that armorial books were created for particular projects. An illustrated compilation is a deliberate selection, produced in a courtly and political environment. Its page order, inclusion of particular bearers, treatment of titles and incorporation of non-Portuguese material may tell us as much about the compiler's ambitions as any single shield.

The Torre do Tombo's archival descriptions place the work in the early sixteenth century, not in a vague 'medieval European heraldry' without dates. The named artist João do Cró, also encountered as João du Cros, has to be distinguished from a later editorial publisher who produced a facsimile or scholarly explanation. A manuscript's creation, archival custody and modern reproduction are different events.

## The shield and the rest of the page

A heraldic manuscript does not consist solely of an abstract blazon. It has page layout, ornament, scripts, labels, visual hierarchy and perhaps corrections. Studying the arms without the folio can remove historical context. We should ask how many emblems share a page, what captions identify their bearers, which devices include crests, and how external ornaments help construct rank.

In many online galleries the original page has been cropped to a shield. That crop can be useful for comparison, but its metadata should say so. It may also remove the very written inscription by which the page identifies the arms. A student examining a complete folio can separate visual evidence of composition from the verbal evidence of the inscription.

This is a good setting for a graduate practicum in material culture: how would the interpretation change if a device survives on parchment, in a seal matrix, in a printed armorial, or as a stone carving? What information survives in each medium, and what must be supplied by outside documents?

## Vasco da Gama: the value of a precise locator

The Torre do Tombo supplies a particularly valuable documented example in its [Vasco da Gama armorial exhibit](https://antt.dglab.gov.pt/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2025/02/vasco-da-gama-conde-almirante.pdf). The archive identifies a depiction of the arms of the admiral in the *Livro do Armeiro-Mor*, *Cartório da Nobreza*, book 19, **folio 87**. The folio reference gives us a research address.

What it does not automatically provide is permission to reuse every modern photographic reproduction. We will eventually need a rights-clear reproduction, preferably supplied or explicitly licensed by the archive, before embedding a full-resolution image in the permanent museum collection. Nor should a single image be asked to prove an entire lineage or every legal consequence associated with its bearer.

There is a larger historical point. Portugal's heraldic development became entwined with maritime expansion, overseas claims and contacts with other political systems. The fact that a record names someone an 'admiral of India' is a prompt for historical interpretation. The armorial page is evidence about the culture of Manueline Portugal; it is not an endorsement of the empire through which that identity emerged.

## Translating an original folio

Portuguese heraldic vocabulary shares many Romance roots with Spanish and French but has its own usage. The *brasão de armas* is an armorial bearing; *escudo* is a shield; *esquartelado* refers to quartering, while *partido* and *cortado* refer to field divisions. The word *livro* points to a book or manuscript, but its significance in the title of an institutional armorial should not be erased by turning every occurrence into a generic 'register'.

A label appearing beneath a shield may be medieval or early modern orthography. Modernizing a historical personal name without documenting the change can hide an evidentiary problem. A scholarly transcription preserves the original string, supplies a normalized reading separately, and explains uncertain letters rather than making them disappear.

| *Cartório da Nobreza* | nobility papers/registry |
| *almirante da Índia* | admiral of India (historical office/title in context) |

## Caption control at folio 87

The [transcription desk](/transcription-desk/?witness=armeiro-mor-87) now makes this locator usable as a short edition exercise. The archive’s presentation places the illustration on **PDF page 23** and cites **book 19, folio 87**. These are two different addressing systems. Preserve the archival location in the witness inventory and use the PDF number as a navigation aid for that particular file.

The modern caption supplies a quoted identification of Vasco da Gama and his office. This project has verified the caption and locator; it has not turned the caption into an independently reviewed transcription of the manuscript hand. That distinction gives the student a concrete task: inspect the original lettering, establish what can be read at the supplied resolution, and compare the reading with the modern archival designation.

Keep the personal designation, office term, and geographical phrase visible in your notes. A translation should explain the role of *almirante* in the historical wording rather than treating a short label as a full account of the office’s powers. The page can support a study of how identity was represented; a claim about appointment, jurisdiction, or political authority needs appropriate documentary evidence beyond the shield and its label.

The desk separates diplomatic transcription, normalized reading, and working English translation. If you supply ordinary word spaces, modern accents, or an expansion, record the intervention in an apparatus entry. If the available image does not support a reading, mark that limit. If the PDF cannot be opened, a study of the modern caption remains possible, but should say explicitly that the manuscript itself was not inspected.

## Quinas are nested designs, not five blue spots

Portuguese heraldry gives us a particularly useful lesson in nested composition. A *quina* is a small blue shield carrying silver roundels. Five such shieldlets appear in the national shield; each contains a design of its own. The resulting picture has several levels: the principal shield, its group of smaller shields, the roundels inside them, and the surrounding border. A reader who counts only the blue shapes has recognized one layer of a much more precise arrangement.

Work from the outside inward. First identify the field and border, then place the shieldlets, then read their orientation and internal charges. An artist who turns a side shieldlet toward the centre has made a visible choice. So has one who changes the number of roundels. The fact that a small image makes those choices difficult to distinguish does not erase them from the design’s history.

Sintra supplies a revealing comparison. The municipality’s account specifies old-style quinas on its tower: five blue pointed shieldlets arranged in cross, the lateral ones turned inward, each carrying **eleven** silver roundels.[^pt-sintra-arms] The national device should therefore never become a shortcut for redrawing every historical or civic occurrence of quinas. Identify the particular description. A local design can intentionally refer to an earlier form.

This is also a useful exercise in counting relationships. Five shieldlets with eleven roundels apiece produce fifty-five roundels in that component, but the blazon’s meaningful instruction remains eleven on each shieldlet. A single aggregate count loses the nested arrangement. Heraldry frequently works this way: three roses on each of four quarters is a different description from twelve roses distributed across one undivided field.

## Évora preserves another historical royal composition

The Museu Nacional Frei Manuel do Cenáculo records a fourteenth-century Évora civic carving, **ME 1744**, with an armed rider, human heads and royal arms above. The royal component has lateral shieldlets turned toward the centre and a border bearing **twelve castles**.[^pt-evora-stone] It is a named witness to a historical composition. Its value would be diminished by retouching it to resemble the modern national shield.

The object also requires us to read two levels of identity. The upper royal component and the rider below belong to a larger civic representation. Isolating the royal arms can help study their details, but it removes their placement within the city’s design. A complete description should state both the internal royal arrangement and its relationship to the surrounding composition.

The violent imagery deserves direct treatment. A rider with a raised sword and severed heads is not merely a generic symbol of courage. Describe the figures before explaining the historical tradition attached to them. Claims about conquest, named adversaries or an individual hero need evidence beyond the catalogue’s physical description. The carving can show how violence was represented in civic identity, even when the surviving record leaves particular identities unresolved.

For comparative work, place Évora’s royal component beside Sintra’s historical quinas and a documented modern national model. Record counts, orientations and setting. The differences form a research question: when and why did a particular institution preserve an older version? They should not be treated as drawing errors simply because modern viewers recognize another arrangement more readily.

## The armillary sphere survives a change of regime

The Portuguese Presidency’s account of the republican flag settlement traces the selection process of **1910–1911**. The adopted green-red flag retains the national shield and places it on the Manueline armillary sphere.[^pt-national-symbols] The visual result combines a changed political arrangement with older identifying components. The new republic did not need to discard every earlier motif to establish a new state presentation.

An armillary sphere is a representation of a spherical framework, with intersecting bands rather than an opaque globe. That distinction matters in heraldic drawing. Replacing it with a solid ball preserves an approximate silhouette while losing the device. A detailed model may show overlapping rings and their order; a small graphic can simplify those features while retaining the recognizable framework. Evaluate the simplification by asking which identifying relationships survive.

The sphere also occupies a different structural position from a charge inside the shield. On the national flag it sits behind the shield at the meeting of the two coloured areas. To reproduce the design, the artist must handle an overlap: sphere first, shield above it. A description that simply lists “shield, sphere, green, red” lacks the information needed to reconstruct the composition.

The Presidency’s account also preserves the sequence of proposal, government approval and subsequent formal adoption.[^pt-national-symbols] This provides a useful chronological caution. An emblem can be proposed before it is adopted, and can appear in public during a political transition before its definitive legal description is issued. A date attached to a drawing may belong to any of those stages. Identify the stage rather than treating every date as the moment at which the whole design suddenly came into existence.

## Lisbon’s ship carries several historical programs

Lisbon’s municipal archive presents a **1961** drawing by João Paulo de Abreu e Lima and traces the ship’s changing presentation. Its account describes a republican use of a bireme that looked toward Roman Olisipo, followed by restoration of Vincentian ship-and-raven references in **1927**. It identifies the official composition published in **1940**.[^pt-lisbon-arms] A vessel that looks like a timeless municipal motif can consequently record very different historical ambitions.

Read the boat as a designed object within the arms. Hull, propulsion, sail and occupants can change how a viewer identifies it. A rowing vessel and a sailing vessel can both refer to Lisbon’s maritime setting, yet they need not refer to the same historical narrative. Keep these visual distinctions in the description. “A ship” is a useful first recognition and an inadequate final catalogue entry when the particular form is the point of the revision.

The ravens add another relationship. In the archive’s account, they connect with the legend of the transport of Saint Vincent’s remains.[^pt-lisbon-arms] The legend helps explain the civic iconography, but it should remain identified as a legend. The documented adoption of a device and the historicity of the story represented by it are separate questions. A city can deliberately represent a foundational narrative without a modern historian having to certify every episode as a literal event.

This case also exposes the limits of generic symbolism tables. The archive offers a particular historical interpretation of the boat and birds. Replacing it with a list stating “black means honesty” and “ships mean adventure” removes the very institutional and chronological content that makes Lisbon worth studying. Begin with the city’s evidence and explain the form in that setting.

## Mural crowns classify the bearer within a local framework

Portugal’s **Lei 53/91** regulates local-government heraldry and certain public-administrative legal entities. It identifies arms, flags and seals, provides a process involving expert consultation, and distinguishes categories of mural crown. The text prescribes a gold five-towered crown for Lisbon, silver five-towered crowns for municipalities seated in a city, and silver four-towered crowns for municipalities seated in a *vila*.[^pt-civic-law] These are rules within a specified administrative framework, rather than a universal European rank chart.

Sintra’s municipal description supplies a concrete four-towered silver crown and a scroll naming *Vila de Sintra*.[^pt-sintra-arms] The external ornament tells us something about the designated civic presentation. It does not mean that a resident can transfer the crown to personal arms or that every settlement with four visible towers has the same status in another country.

Compare the crown with the tower inside Sintra’s shield. One is an external classification ornament; the other is a charge carrying local historical content. Their shared architectural form does not make them interchangeable. Moving the internal tower above the shield would change both the composition and the statement. A good diagram labels the two functions separately even when both are drawn with battlements.

The statutory framework also names simplicity and distinctiveness as design principles.[^pt-civic-law] These principles make sense together. A simple shield that duplicates a neighbour’s arms has not solved the problem of identification. A unique design containing an inventory of every local landmark may be equally unsuccessful at small scale. Design work has to find a compact combination that remains recognizable and belongs to the bearer’s documented identity.

## Military heraldry uses a fuller achievement and specific offices

The Portuguese Air Force’s Lumiar page describes commander’s arms with a silver pelican feeding three young on red and a Pegasus crest. Its explanation connects the pelican with the base’s support functions.[^pt-lumiar] This is a specific institutional use of an old motif. It supplies a better account than treating every pelican as a symbol with one mandatory meaning in every setting.

The page also demonstrates the value of maintaining the distinction between shield and crest. The pelican occupies the shield; Pegasus supplies the crest above the helmet. An illustration cropped to the shield no longer shows the latter. A reader who calls the pelican “the crest” has therefore not merely chosen an informal synonym: they have made it harder to discuss two actual parts of the same achievement.

The designated bearer matters as well. A heading identifying a commander’s arms points to an office-related presentation. It should not be rewritten as a hereditary family grant to whichever officer happened to command the base when the page was published. The figure may hold an office with a prescribed emblematic system while also possessing unrelated personal arms. Institutional role and personal genealogy require separate descriptions.

A military case can consequently be read on three axes: the visible design, the bearer or office specified by the text, and the institutional explanation of the motifs. All three contribute to the study. The explanation does not replace the blazon, and the blazon alone does not reveal the particular support duties that motivated the choice of a pelican.

## Lagos turns historical references into a new service design

A **2018** issue of the Navy’s *Revista da Armada* publishes the arms of the Lagos local maritime-police command. Its blazon combines an armillary sphere, anchor-bearing six-pointed stars, and a silver chief containing a three-pendant label charged with fleurs-de-lis. The accompanying explanation connects selected elements with Prince Henry and Lagos.[^pt-lagos-police] The design demonstrates how a modern service can use inherited local references within its own composition.

The nested charges are worth drawing slowly. First place the chief as a distinct upper area. Then place the label within it. Finally add the fleurs-de-lis to the label’s pendants. A fleur-de-lis beside the label would not fulfil the same instruction. The arrangement resembles the quinas exercise in its logic: the description specifies not only what exists but what carries what.

The stars and anchors form another nested relation. A star charged with an anchor remains a compound figure, rather than two independent charges separated across the field. At small size, the inner anchor may become difficult to read. That is a reproduction problem to solve through scale and line weight, not a reason to reclassify the charge as a plain star without telling the reader.

The published explanation gives this particular label a historical reference.[^pt-lagos-police] Compare that with the label used for cadency in other traditions. The shape is recognizable across the examples; its institutional meaning depends on the design. A student who has learned that a label can difference an heir’s arms has learned one important use, not an exclusive definition of every label in heraldry.

## Archival workshop

Use the [national archive's manuscript descriptions](https://antt.dglab.gov.pt/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2025/02/vasco-da-gama-doc.pdf) and its [folio-specific Vasco da Gama publication](https://antt.dglab.gov.pt/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2025/02/vasco-da-gama-conde-almirante.pdf). Create a catalogue record separating the artist/compiler, patron, probable creation date, archive, collection, book, folio, later commentary, and image-rights status.

**Graduate assignment:** Locate a second dated Portuguese armorial witness. Formulate a method for comparing two artistic witnesses of the same arms without treating artistic differences as automatic legal differences. Discuss how court sponsorship shapes what is collected and which bearers are omitted.

## Graduate workshop: reconstruct a Portuguese design in layers

Choose Sintra, the Évora stone, or the Lagos command as your case. Make a diagram with one layer for the principal field, one for major charges or divisions, one for charges carried by other charges, and one for external ornaments. Add a source beside every decision that cannot be established from the object or official description.

Then write a short comparison explaining one historical difference. Sintra’s eleven roundels on each old-style shieldlet, Évora’s twelve castles, and a military label’s particular local reference all provide defensible topics. The purpose is to explain a documented variation, not to force every Portuguese design into a single national template.
