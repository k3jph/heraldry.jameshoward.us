## A book that can actually be located

The Portuguese *Livro do Armeiro-Mor*, compiled and illuminated in **1509** during the reign of Manuel I, gives us a dated manuscript through which to investigate Portuguese heraldry. The national archive, the [Torre do Tombo](https://antt.dglab.gov.pt/), identifies the historical manuscript with the royal papers, *Casa Real, Cartório da Nobreza, livro 19*. Once we have a specific catalogue identification, a heraldic image ceases to be an anonymous web decoration and becomes a historical object capable of citation.

The manuscript is also a reminder that armorial books were created for particular projects. An illustrated compilation is a deliberate selection, produced in a courtly and political environment. Its page order, inclusion of particular bearers, treatment of titles and incorporation of non-Portuguese material may tell us as much about the compiler's ambitions as any single shield.

The Torre do Tombo's archival descriptions place the work in the early sixteenth century, not in a vague 'medieval European heraldry' without dates. The named artist João do Cró, also encountered as João du Cros, has to be distinguished from a later editorial publisher who produced a facsimile or scholarly explanation. A manuscript's creation, archival custody and modern reproduction are different events.

## The shield and the rest of the page

A heraldic manuscript does not consist solely of an abstract blazon. It has page layout, ornament, scripts, labels, visual hierarchy and perhaps corrections. Studying the arms without the folio can remove historical context. We should ask how many emblems share a page, what captions identify their bearers, which devices include crests, and how external ornaments help construct rank.

In many online galleries the original page has been cropped to a shield. That crop can be useful for comparison, but its metadata should say so. It may also remove the very written inscription by which the page identifies the arms. A student examining a complete folio can separate visual evidence of composition from the verbal evidence of the inscription.

This is a good setting for a graduate practicum in material culture: how would the interpretation change if a device survives on parchment, in a seal matrix, in a printed armorial, or as a stone carving? What information survives in each medium, and what must be supplied by outside documents?

## Vasco da Gama: the value of a precise locator

The Torre do Tombo supplies a particularly valuable documented example in its [Vasco da Gama armorial exhibit](https://antt.dglab.gov.pt/wp-content/uploads/sites/17/2025/02/vasco-da-gama-conde-almirante.pdf). The archive identifies a depiction of the arms of the admiral in the *Livro do Armeiro-Mor*, *Cartório da Nobreza*, book 19, **folio 87**. The folio reference gives us a research address.

What it does not automatically provide is permission to reuse every modern photographic reproduction. We will eventually need a rights-clear reproduction, preferably supplied or explicitly licensed by the archive, before embedding a full-resolution image in the permanent museum collection. Nor should a single image be asked to prove an entire lineage or every legal consequence associated with its bearer.

There is a larger historical point. Portugal's heraldic development became entwined with maritime expansion, overseas claims and contacts with other political systems. The fact that a record names someone an 'admiral of India' is a prompt for historical interpretation. The armorial page is evidence about the culture of Manueline Portugal; it is not an endorsement of the empire through which that identity emerged.

## Translating an original folio

Portuguese heraldic vocabulary shares many Romance roots with Spanish and French but has its own usage. The *brasão de armas* is an armorial bearing; *escudo* is a shield; *esquartelado* refers to quartering, while *partido* and *cortado* refer to field divisions. The word *livro* points to a book or manuscript, but its significance in the title of an institutional armorial should not be erased by turning every occurrence into a generic 'register'.

A label appearing beneath a shield may be medieval or early modern orthography. Modernizing a historical personal name without documenting the change can hide an evidentiary problem. A scholarly transcription preserves the original string, supplies a normalized reading separately, and explains uncertain letters rather than making them disappear.

| Portuguese | Careful English rendering |
|---|---|
| *Livro do Armeiro-Mor* | historical title, normally retained in Portuguese |
| *brasão de armas* | coat of arms / armorial bearings |
| *f.* / *fólio* | folio |
| *Casa Real* | royal household |
| *Cartório da Nobreza* | nobility papers/registry |
| *almirante da Índia* | admiral of India (historical office/title in context) |

Formulate a method for comparing two artistic witnesses of the same arms without treating artistic differences as automatic legal differences. ## Caption control at folio 87

The [transcription desk](/transcription-desk/?witness=armeiro-mor-87) now makes this locator usable as a short edition exercise. The archive’s presentation places the illustration on **PDF page 23** and cites **book 19, folio 87**. These are two different addressing systems. Preserve the archival location in the witness inventory and use the PDF number as a navigation aid for that particular file.

The modern caption supplies a quoted identification of Vasco da Gama and his office. This project has verified the caption and locator; it has not turned the caption into an independently reviewed transcription of the manuscript hand. That distinction gives the student a concrete task: inspect the original lettering, establish what can be read at the supplied resolution, and compare the reading with the modern archival designation.

Keep the personal designation, office term, and geographical phrase visible in your notes. A translation should explain the role of *almirante* in the historical wording rather than treating a short label as a full account of the office’s powers. The page can support a study of how identity was represented; a claim about appointment, jurisdiction, or political authority needs appropriate documentary evidence beyond the shield and its label.

The desk separates diplomatic transcription, normalized reading, and working English translation. If you supply ordinary word spaces, modern accents, or an expansion, record the intervention in an apparatus entry. If the available image does not support a reading, mark that limit. If the PDF cannot be opened, a study of the modern caption remains possible, but should say explicitly that the manuscript itself was not inspected.

Compare the roles of manuscript writing, modern description, material change, and digital navigation.
