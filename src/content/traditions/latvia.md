## Which bearers does the statute include?

The opening of Latvia's [*Ģerboņu likums*](https://likumi.lv/ta/id/123815-gerbonu-likums), or Coats of Arms Law, requires us to identify the bearer before following the registration process. Its scope expressly includes **public bodies and private persons**, whose categories and applicable rules must remain distinguishable. A register entry can therefore concern more than one kind of armorial identity. Reading the definition first prevents us from treating every shield in the system as evidence of the same public or private right.

The opening definitions of *ģerbonis* reach a state or municipality, another public body, an administrative institution, a legal person in private law, and a natural person. Those categories are not identical, and some special rules concern public bodies or state symbols. But the inclusion of private entities in the statutory scope is enough to undermine the claim that personal heraldry is everywhere a wholly unregulated matter outside Britain.

This is not an invitation to translate the Latvian system as a College of Arms transplanted to Riga. The source identifies a **Ministry of Culture**, a **State Heraldry Commission** and a formal *Ģerboņu reģistrs* (register of coats of arms). Each has different administrative functions. The appropriate historical approach is to describe the Latvian actors and their powers before deciding how the system compares with a foreign granting authority.

## Registration is a process with stages

Article 3 concerns applications for registration of a coat of arms, including a description of the proposed arms and information about the person or organization requesting recognition. Article 4 describes review by the State Heraldry Commission and the Ministry's decision-making process. Article 5 sets out the assessment of the proposed description and image against heraldic requirements and artistic considerations. Article 6 concerns the register and the publication of an official registration notice.

This division of labor is worth drawing as a flowchart. The applicant prepares a description and, where appropriate, a visual design. The Ministry refers the proposed material for specialist consideration. The Commission assesses compliance and may offer recommendations. A government official makes a registration decision. The registry preserves the authorized details, and the official notice provides a public record.

The exact administrative requirements and deadlines must be checked against the **consolidated version in force at the time of a particular application**. The law's online text records amendments, and legal research should never quote today's wording as if it governed a registration performed twenty years earlier. Legislation is versioned evidence.

## A register with a sensitive-data problem

Article 6 describes registry metadata concerning the bearer, including private persons. A researcher might be delighted to see names, addresses and personal identification fields in the statute's description of stored information. But a public humanities website has no reason to copy personal identity numbers or private residential details into a teaching database merely because a law names them as administrative data.

**A research corpus is not an excuse to publish sensitive personal information.** Our digital armorial should retain public document IDs, names of public institutions, design descriptions, dates, official links and necessary source metadata. It must avoid unnecessary identifiable details of living individuals. The existence of a legal government register is not proof that all its fields should be republished by a separate educational site.

That distinction becomes particularly significant when collecting images or attempting to correlate heraldic designs with genealogical databases. A model can create a privacy risk even when each individual record was originally collected for a legitimate public purpose.

## Legal protection and educational reproduction

The law addresses exclusive rights and legal protection, including the need for a sufficiently distinctive registered design. It also sets out principles for use. Importantly, Article 8 speaks separately about using coats of arms as illustrations or reference material in encyclopedias, teaching aids and other scholarly and informational publications. That is a valuable source for our own academic use cases.

It would nevertheless be poor practice to interpret this one statutory permission as a universal image licence. Another country's photographic reproduction, museum image, graphic-design copyright, trademark-like limitation or official insignia restriction may still require separate inquiry. **The legal status of the coat and the copyright status of the image representing it are different questions.**

For example, a graduate project might compare a newly registered municipal shield with one in a historical building photograph. Even if the registry permits explanatory illustration, the photograph may have been taken by a contemporary professional and licensed on separate terms. The catalog should preserve provenance and rights for both the armorial design and the photograph.

## The national arms combine a new state with regional devices

Latvia’s great state arms were approved in **1921** and restored in **1993**, according to the Presidency’s account.[^lv-national-en] Their composition combines a rising sun with the lion and griffin associated with historical regions, while stars and external figures complete the larger achievement.[^lv-national] The design is a useful example of a modern state building an emblem from components with different histories.

Read the shield’s divisions before identifying the animals. The sun occupies the upper compartment; the lower area is divided between the red lion on silver and silver griffin on red. This is not ordinary quartering into four equal areas. An artist who begins with a quartered template will have to undo that assumption to place the three components correctly. The field’s geometry supplies the framework within which the charges become intelligible.

The sun also appears as a partial figure. Its relation to the edge of the compartment gives it the appearance of rising. A full sun floating in the same area would preserve the subject while changing the instructed presentation. This is an example of meaning carried by placement: the figure does not need an accompanying sentence inside the shield to enact the operation.

The regional animals contribute another kind of identity. Their inclusion does not turn the national shield into a register of personal families. A motif can refer to a historic territory when it appears in a state composition, while a related motif in another shield may identify a different bearer. The design and its institutional explanation must be read together.

## A griffin is an organized composite creature

The Latvian state griffin makes the anatomy of a heraldic monster particularly useful. Identify the head, wings, forequarters and hindquarters before simplifying the silhouette. The recognizable creature emerges from their combination. A lion with decorative wings does not necessarily preserve the griffin’s intended structure, just as an eagle drawn above a lion does not become one griffin merely because both animals are present.

In the Latvian description, the griffin carries a sword in its right foreclaw, and the lion in the other lower compartment is reversed.[^lv-national] The two animals therefore require attention to attribute and direction. The sword is a charge carried by the creature. The creature’s orientation is part of its own pose. Keep those instructions separate so that turning one does not accidentally turn every object in the compartment.

For a drawing exercise, begin with a plain griffin and then add the sword. Does the foreclaw still read as grasping it? Is the blade distinguishable from a wing feather or an ornamental line? Next reduce the drawing to the size of a small document seal. The exercise shows why heraldic artists often exaggerate the separation of limbs and accessories: the design has to survive reduction while retaining its identifying relationships.

The external lion and griffin require another distinction. They support the shield rather than occupying its field. A description of the shield alone does not automatically include them. Conversely, a complete great achievement that omits them has lost a prescribed part of the presentation. This is why “the arms” should be qualified when a discussion moves between shield and achievement.

## Parallel official pages can expose a translation discrepancy

The Presidency’s English and Latvian pages provide a useful caution for close reading. The English text describes the griffin’s tongue as blue; the Latvian description gives it gold.[^lv-national-en][^lv-national] The disagreement concerns a small detail, but it is exactly the kind of detail that a heraldic specification can make identifying.

Record the discrepancy rather than silently harmonizing the pages. The English page helps orient a reader who does not know Latvian. The original-language text and applicable official model are needed to settle a production question. Institutional publication establishes provenance, but it does not make two inconsistent statements mutually compatible.

This is a more general translation lesson. A translated noun can be correct while a colour adjective is wrong. A sentence can preserve every component while attaching one modifier to the wrong component. Close reading therefore checks grammatical relationships, not just a glossary of equivalent words. For a compound creature carrying an object, the reader has to know whether “silver” modifies the animal, its sword, or both.

The useful response is proportionate. We do not need to dismiss the entire English page because of one discrepancy, nor to ignore it because the publisher is authoritative. We need to identify which claim is affected, locate the stronger controlling witness, and retain a note explaining the choice. That makes the synthesis reviewable by another reader.

## Several state forms are a hierarchy of presentation

The Latvian Presidency distinguishes the great arms, the small arms and an augmented small form with oak branches.[^lv-national] These are defined presentations within one state system. Their differences should be studied from the adopted forms rather than inferred from a universal rule that every country must have exactly three grades.

Compare the information retained at each level. The principal shield preserves the sun and regional animals. The greater presentation adds figures and other external elements. The intermediate form can preserve a surrounding vegetal presentation without including the complete supporter arrangement. Reduction thus operates on components, rather than merely shrinking the same picture until it becomes illegible.

This distinction matters in public documents. An institution’s authority to use a particular form is an administrative question; the artist’s ability to fit it into a page is a graphic question. The two questions interact but should not be confused. A designer may need a prescribed smaller form for a constrained space, rather than an improvised crop of the great achievement.

For a comparative assignment, place Latvia’s forms beside Estonia’s large and small state arms and Luxembourg’s state presentations. Identify exactly which external components change. Then compare Tallinn, where the named small shield has different internal charges. The exercise demonstrates why local definitions are indispensable even when English terminology appears familiar.

## Riga’s gate and keys preserve a different civic composition

Riga’s city account dates approval of its current arms to **1925** and renewal to **1988**. The shield combines a gate and towers, a lion’s head under the raised grille, crossed keys above, and additional upper ornaments; the great form adds lion supporters.[^lv-riga] It is a civic composition with a much more architectural structure than the state shield.

The gate is not a background illustration to be omitted once the keys have been recognized. Its opening contains the lion’s head, so the composition depends on nesting. A small reproduction must leave enough room for the head to remain visibly inside the gate. Otherwise the viewer may see a detached animal face beneath two unrelated towers.

The crossed keys provide another reconstruction problem. Their shafts, wards and angle have to remain legible, and their crossing belongs to a different part of the shield from the gate opening. Place the major architectural masses first, then fit the upper charges into the remaining field. This ordering keeps the arms from becoming a stack of independently drawn clip-art objects.

Riga also illustrates a limit of explanatory webpages. Its English page calls the flag a square while giving rectangular proportions elsewhere.[^lv-riga] A designer should resolve that contradiction against the formal standard before manufacturing a flag. The heraldic historian can still use the page for orientation while recording that this particular production claim remains inconsistent. A contradiction in one sentence does not erase the named adoption history, but it does change how confidently we use that sentence.

## The commission’s work is more than approving pictures

The Presidency describes the State Heraldry Commission’s responsibilities in research, maintenance of information and examination of heraldic and artistic solutions.[^lv-commission] This institutional role helps explain why the registration process is not equivalent to uploading an image to a public gallery. An emblem has to be examined as a design belonging to an identified bearer within a regulated system.

Consider an invented applicant proposing a silver griffin on red because it looks impressive. The first question is not whether the drawing is attractive. It is whether the proposed arms can be distinguished from existing protected identities, including state or regional components. A technically competent drawing can still fail the identification problem. A distinctive design must also be capable of consistent description and reproduction.

The division of labour matters. The artist develops the composition; the applicant identifies the intended bearer and reasons for the design; the commission examines the heraldic solution; the administrative process produces a registration decision where the law provides for one. Those functions can interact without collapsing into a single claim that an artist “granted” the arms by drawing them.

For a researcher, the documentary sequence supplies several possible dates. A preliminary design can precede the commission’s assessment, which can precede the administrative decision and publication. Identify the relevant stage in captions. “Designed in,” “approved in” and “registered in” are useful distinctions precisely because they prevent one date from doing three different jobs.

## Private arms need an identified bearer and a transmission argument

Latvia’s statutory inclusion of natural persons and private legal persons broadens the field beyond public bodies.[^lv-heraldry-law] It does not eliminate the need to identify the particular bearer. A registered association’s arms, a natural person’s arms and a family-related design can involve different questions about use, succession and representation.

An invented teaching example makes the issue clear. Suppose a registered legal association uses a shield on its publications and members’ badges. A badge can show membership without making every member a hereditary bearer of the association’s coat. A member who leaves the association has another relationship to the emblem than a successor to the legal entity. The registration record and governing documents, rather than visual similarity alone, establish the relevant identity.

For a personal or family case, distinguish the registered design from the genealogical argument connecting a later claimant to its bearer. A catalogue entry can establish that a design was recorded for someone. It cannot independently establish an undocumented parent-child relationship. A familiar surname supplies a search term, not the missing documentary link.

The public-facing project should therefore emphasize designs, named institutional records and the evidence needed for a claim, while leaving sensitive personal identifiers out of teaching examples. That approach improves the scholarship as well as the presentation: a list of private numbers would contribute nothing to understanding the blazon or the transmission argument.

## Source-reading laboratory

Open the Latvian [Coats of Arms Law](https://likumi.lv/ta/id/123815-gerbonu-likums). In Article 1, identify the defined categories of bearer. Make a process map from Articles 3–6 showing which public actor performs each decision, what document it creates and where that document is registered.

Now compare the statutory framework with the [Flemish private-arms decree](https://codex.vlaanderen.be/Portals/Codex/documenten/1006070.html). Both concern private persons, but they use different structures and legal categories. Do not label one 'more official' without a defined comparison criterion.

**Graduate assignment:** Produce a 1,500-word paper on how Latvia defines the legal identity of a coat of arms and the relationship among registration, distinctiveness and protection. Include a short original-language clause, a carefully attributed English translation, a statement of the version of the law you used, and an explicit privacy policy for any proposed data extraction.

## Graduate workshop: audit a Latvian armorial explanation

Write a short audit using the national shield, Riga’s civic composition and one law-defined registration category. Identify the bearer and form before describing each design. Then select one translation or proportion discrepancy and explain which evidence would settle it.

Add an invented private-association example, explicitly labelled as hypothetical, and state what membership would establish. The exercise should distinguish a right to display an affiliation badge from a claim to bear hereditary arms. It should also distinguish a drawing, commission assessment and registration act.
