## The bearer need not be a person

Heraldic designs identify collective bodies as well as individuals. Municipalities, universities, associations, and other institutions can use arms in the appropriate framework. Their displays raise questions of continuity, representation, public authority, and institutional memory. Studying them takes us well beyond the idea that heraldry is simply a catalogue of aristocratic families.

An institution's arms may appear on a building, a flag, a diploma, stationery, or a website. The design travels among media with different functions. A diploma authenticates or commemorates an academic act; a flag identifies; a carving may mark patronage or ownership; a web icon must remain legible at a very small size. The same motif can participate in all four without making the objects interchangeable.

Begin by identifying the bearer and the object. “University arms on a twentieth-century diploma” is more informative than “old shield.” It gives you two histories to investigate: the history of the design and the history of its particular use.

## Arms, seals, flags, and logos

An **armorial design** can be part of a **seal**. A **banner of arms** can spread it across a flag. A modern **logo** can reproduce, simplify, or depart from it. These relationships do not make the terms synonyms. The function and documentary status of each should be established separately.[^media]

Suppose a city has a recorded coat of arms and adopts a new graphic mark for a tourism campaign. The mark might use the arms' tower without reproducing the whole coat. Unless the relevant documents say otherwise, the marketing decision does not establish that the arms were abolished or regranted. Identify what changed: campaign identity, graphic treatment, official emblem, or institutional bearer.

Similarly, an uncolored seal impression cannot by itself establish a shield's tinctures. A flag's rectangular field may require a different layout from the shield. A cropped website icon may omit supporters. Each is evidence of a particular display. Compare them with the formal record before diagnosing a change in the arms themselves.

## Documentary encounter: the Great Seal of the United States

The National Archives dates the adoption of the Great Seal's design to **20 June 1782** and explains that Charles Thomson submitted a description and symbolism statement after several design efforts. Its account distinguishes the seal's authenticating use from appearances of the design elsewhere.[^seal] This is a documented case in which an emblem has both a formal specification and an attributed explanation.

The State Department's Foreign Affairs Manual identifies the obverse as the coat of arms of the United States and describes its official use and custody.[^state] Read those statements as institutional evidence about a public emblem. Do not transfer the rules for that emblem to private personal arms simply because both contain a shield.

A useful exercise is to compare three things: the adopted description, a particular surviving drawing, and a later impression. What is continuous? What differs in artistic treatment? Which document controls the official specification? The answers should identify their sources rather than assuming that the most familiar modern picture is the original design.

The case also shows how to use symbolism responsibly. A contemporary explanation can support an attributed account of a color or figure's intended meaning. It does not establish that the same tincture or figure means the same thing in every unrelated coat of arms. Symbolism is documented here, not universalized.

## Institutional continuity: Universities Canada

The Canadian Public Register identifies **Universities Canada** in a confirmation dated **15 May 2015**, at **volume VI, page 503**. Its shield blazon describes four pairs of green maple seeds on silver, while its symbolism commentary connects the design to earlier organizational emblems and intellectual growth.[^universities]

The act type matters. A confirmation is not automatically the first invention of the emblem. The commentary explicitly situates the design within institutional continuity. A catalogue should preserve the confirmation date, the bearer name, and whatever evidence establishes earlier use. Combining all of those into one “created” date would confuse the history.

The record also helps separate description from interpretation. The blazon tells an artist what to depict. The symbolism explains why the institution associates the design with particular ideas. A historian can study both while keeping them distinct. If another institution uses green seeds, it does not inherit this record's explanation by visual resemblance.

## Architecture preserves uses, alterations, and losses

Arms on buildings can identify patrons, offices, dynasties, institutions, or later commemorative additions. A carved shield may survive a change in ownership or government. Its survival is evidence of material continuity, not necessarily of uninterrupted legal authority or institutional identity.

To study an architectural display, record where it is, how it is attached to the building, whether it is integral or inserted, what inscriptions accompany it, and what the conservation record says. A doorway's date does not automatically date a later inserted panel. A repainted stone may preserve an older shape with newer tinctures. These questions require the building and object history, not just the shield's visual vocabulary.

Missing features are evidence too, but their cause must be investigated. A shield may be blank through unfinished work, deliberate removal, weathering, or restoration. Do not announce a political purge from a smooth stone alone. A documentary account, technical study, or comparison with earlier photographs can support an interpretation that the present surface cannot settle.

## Documentary encounter: stained glass

The Metropolitan Museum of Art catalogues **Heraldic Shield**, object **12.137.2**, as British stained glass dated **1300–1320**. Its collection record supplies the medium, dimensions, credit line, and a Public Domain image designation.[^glass] This is a materially different witness from a painted parchment or metal seal.

Glass has leading, transmission of light, possible repairs, and a history of installation or relocation. Those properties affect how an image is seen and preserved. A transcription of the design should not erase the medium. A material study should not infer a bearer without documentary evidence merely because a charge resembles known arms.

For this introductory encounter, use the museum's title and date range as attributed catalogue information. We have not claimed a fresh identification of the bearer or a complete reconstruction of the original architectural setting. The research question is how an armorial composition functions in this medium and which additional records could explain its use.

## Symbols make public claims

Public emblems are choices about representation. A university may choose a charge associated with learning. A city may represent local industry, landscape, or historical authority. A postcolonial state may adopt, adapt, or replace inherited armorial language. Explaining those choices requires dated sources and the relevant communities' own accounts.

Do not assume a European heraldic category is the best first explanation of every non-European symbol. An emblem can incorporate an Indigenous figure whose meaning and authority require a different body of knowledge. The comparison should ask how the communities involved describe the symbol, what permission or authority matters, and how the public record represents the choice.

Likewise, a symbolism statement is a source to analyze, not necessarily the whole story. It can reveal official intention while leaving public reception, disagreement, or later reinterpretation open. A study may compare those layers, but it should not invent the disagreement simply because controversy would make a more exciting paragraph.

## Worked problem: a logo changed; what follows?

**Constructed scenario:** a university's old website used its complete achievement. Its new website uses a simplified book-and-star mark. A diploma still carries a seal containing the shield. A press release calls the web change a “new visual identity.” Has the university abandoned its arms?

The evidence supports a change in the web presentation and branding language. It does not yet establish a change to the recorded arms or seal. The continuing diploma use suggests another display persists, but its date and status should be checked. Seek the brand guidance, institutional decision, and relevant armorial record. Do not force a yes-or-no answer before the documents distinguish the changes.

## Workshop: follow one emblem across three media

Choose a documented civic or institutional coat and find three dated uses. Record the object, bearer, medium, source, and whether the design was altered or merely adapted. Write a short argument about continuity and change. Include one case where the source does not let you decide.

Assessment should reward the distinction between a formal act and a visual adaptation, proper attribution of symbolism, and preservation of the objects' dates. It should not reward finding the greatest number of vaguely similar pictures. Three securely identified witnesses can support more scholarship than thirty untraceable images.
