## Start by separating the components

A full heraldic display can look like a crowded stage. There is a shield, a helmet, something sitting on the helmet, cloth curling around it, creatures on either side, a motto, and possibly several additional insignia. The temptation is to treat all of it as one indivisible logo. That makes it much harder to read. Start with the parts, then ask what their arrangement tells you.

The **achievement** is the assembled display. The **escutcheon** is the shield. Its surface bears the field and charges. Above it may be a helm, wreath or torse, and crest. Mantling surrounds the helm. Supporters may stand beside the shield on a compartment. A motto may appear on a scroll. Other insignia can have particular institutional meanings. This vocabulary describes a familiar arrangement; it does not say that every armiger must display every component.[^parts]

Anatomy comes before interpretation because an object has to be identified before its role can be explained. A bird on the shield is a charge. A bird above the helm may be the crest. A bird standing beside the shield may be a supporter. The zoology is identical. The heraldic job is different. If you record all three as “bird emblem,” your catalogue has thrown away information.

## The shield and its contents

The shield is the place to begin a blazon of the arms. Identify the field, then its major divisions or charges, then the relationships among them. A small shield placed on a larger shield is an **inescutcheon**; it does not mean the display has two unrelated central shields. A border can be a constituent feature of the arms rather than a decorative picture frame. The distinction depends on the description and context.

Do not assume the outer contour is a dating instrument. A shield shape may imitate an earlier style. An artist can deliberately choose a historical shape for a contemporary commission. A diagram in a modern textbook may simplify an object that was not simple in its original form. To date the work, use the object record, inscriptions, material evidence, provenance, and scholarship together.

This becomes especially clear when arms are placed on something that was never a combat shield. A stained-glass panel has its own dimensions and structural constraints. A carved stone has its own edges. A plate has a circular surface. The design travels; the physical support changes. Record the support separately from the armorial composition.

## The crest is a particular component

The **crest** is conventionally located above the helm, often on a wreath. Calling an entire coat of arms a crest is common everyday usage, but it prevents precise discussion. If someone asks an artist to reproduce “the crest,” the artist needs to know whether the request concerns the component above the helmet or the complete display.

The wreath, or **torse**, is shown as twisted bands of cloth. **Mantling** is the drapery associated with the helmet, often elaborately stylized. Their artistic treatment can be exuberant without making them charges on the shield. A long curling piece of red and white mantling does not turn the field into a red-and-white divided field. It remains outside the shield.

Fox-Davies treats these elements separately, and his chapter on the components of an achievement provides a useful historical vocabulary map.[^manual] His conventions must still be located in time and place. Reading his description of a British helm in 1909 is not enough to decide how a modern Canadian artist should depict a helmet, or what a particular continental arrangement signified three centuries earlier.

The practical lesson is simple: first name the component; then locate the rule that applies to it. Resist the reverse procedure of spotting a helmet, remembering a chart from somewhere, and announcing a rank.

## Supporters, compartments, and the temptation to upgrade

Supporters occupy the sides of the shield. The **compartment** is the base on which they stand. It may represent terrain, plants, waves, or a constructed support. The Canadian institutional guide describes eligibility for supporters in its own system.[^supporters] That is evidence of Canadian practice, not a global permission slip to add two lions because there is room in your design file.

This is one reason an achievement is more than ornamental packaging. External features can make assertions that matter within a tradition. An illustrator should ask whether a feature belongs to the recorded design, whether it is an allowed form of display, and whether it is merely a later embellishment. “It looked empty without them” is an explanation of the illustrator's decision, not evidence of entitlement.

For a teaching exercise, you can draw components separately and label them as hypothetical. For a reproduction of historical arms, additions must be justified. For a proposed personal design, begin with the applicable jurisdiction rather than a shopping list of impressive ornaments. The objective is a coherent display, not winning a competition for the largest number of accessories.

## Motto, badge, banner, and seal

A **motto** is a phrase associated with a display. Its position and treatment vary. A **badge** is a distinct emblem, even when it reuses a charge from the arms. A **banner of arms** carries the armorial design across a flag's surface. A **seal** is a device or impression used in authentication; its image may contain arms, a figure, lettering, or a combination. These are not synonyms.[^flags]

The difference is easiest to see through a constructed example. Suppose a shield bears three keys. A badge is a single key inside a ring. A banner repeats the shield's composition across a rectangle. A seal places the shield inside a circular inscription. All four may serve the same institution, but copying one into the role of another changes the object you are making. If you catalogue the seal as a banner, you have not merely chosen an unusual label. You have misidentified its function.

Do not assume every emblem derived from a charge is an officially granted badge. Designers routinely create simplified marks. To identify one as a recorded heraldic badge, locate the relevant documentation. The visual resemblance may be obvious while the documentary status remains uncertain.

## Documentary encounter: an actual shield is also an object

The Metropolitan Museum of Art's **Shield for the Field or Tournament (Targe)**, object **25.26.1**, is catalogued as German and dated approximately **1450**. Its materials include wood, leather, linen, gesso, pigments, and silver. The museum identifies the painted arms with either the Gottsmann or the related Türriegel family of Franconia, preserving uncertainty rather than selecting one bearer.[^targe]

That uncertainty is a teaching opportunity. A physical shield has a front, a back, layers, repairs, dimensions, and provenance. A modern SVG representing only its arms cannot preserve all of those properties. The museum reports that earlier decoration was protected under later paint. A photograph shows a surface; a conservation account explains a history of surfaces. Those are complementary kinds of evidence.

When studying this object, do not silently transform “either of two related families” into a unique identification. Do not date every depicted feature separately to 1450 simply because that is the catalogue's date for the object. Record the museum's attribution and date as such, and say which questions require closer material or documentary study.

## Worked problem: label before you interpret

**Constructed teaching example:** a shield bears a silver tree on green. A silver bird stands above a helmet. Two red animals stand beside the shield on rocky ground. A scroll below carries a phrase. The drawing has no grant, register entry, or attribution.

You can identify the tree as a charge, the bird as a putative crest, the side figures as putative supporters, the rocky ground as a compartment, and the phrase as a motto. You cannot establish that a real person is entitled to this assembled achievement. You also cannot identify the animals' species if the drawing lacks diagnostic features. Use “unidentified quadrupeds” rather than inventing wolves because the phrase mentions courage.

Now imagine that the silver bird also appears alone on the institution's stationery. It might be a badge, a crest used separately, or a modern derivative mark. The correct next step is documentary comparison. The fact that the isolated bird looks familiar does not settle its formal role.

## Workshop and answer discussion

Select an institutional image of a complete achievement. Prepare a two-column inventory: **component observed** and **basis for identification**. Add a third column if the institution supplies a symbolism commentary. Keep the commentary's meaning separate from your anatomical description. You may link to a protected image without reproducing it.

<details>
<summary>How should the inventory be evaluated?</summary>

Give credit for correct spatial relationships, precise vocabulary, and explicit uncertainty. A crest should be identified by its position and source description rather than by its being the largest animal. A badge should not be invented from a cropped image. A good inventory acknowledges omitted components and does not interpret omission as loss of rights. Its conclusion explains which parts are described by the source and which are the student's visual identifications.

You have not necessarily changed the arms; you have changed the display and the information available to a viewer. That distinction will matter throughout the course.
