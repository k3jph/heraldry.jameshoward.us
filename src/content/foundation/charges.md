## A charge is a figure with a role in the design

Heraldic charges include animals, plants, tools, buildings, celestial forms, geometric figures, and invented creatures. The range is large because an identifying image can draw on many kinds of visual material. The task is not to memorize every charge before reading a shield. It is to learn how a charge's type, number, tincture, orientation, and relationship to other elements are described.[^range]

Suppose a description gives us a lion, a star, and a tower. We can now make an inventory, but we cannot yet draw the shield. We need the number of each figure, the lion’s posture, the tinctures, and the positions of the figures relative to one another. Those relationships distinguish this composition from another containing the same three kinds of charge. For each figure, record its type, count, depiction, and location, then check which details the source actually specifies.

Once we can describe the charge and its place in the design, we can investigate why it was selected. That requires another connection: evidence about the choice made for this bearer. A grant may supply a symbolism statement, which we can transcribe and attribute to the record. Identifying the same animal elsewhere supplies a visual comparison, but the second bearer’s intention remains a separate question. The description gives us something to investigate; the explanation needs its own source.

## Animals have attitudes

**Attitude** is the conventional position of an animal. A lion **rampant** is shown upright in a characteristic raised posture. **Passant** indicates walking, conventionally with a forepaw raised. **Statant** indicates standing. **Sejant** indicates sitting; **couchant** indicates lying down with the head raised. **Dormant** indicates sleeping.[^attitudes]

The vocabulary is not interchangeable across every species. A posture that is conventional for a lion may be described differently for a fish or a bird. When studying a historical animal, use a suitable manual and compare its diagnostic parts. Do not turn every raised quadruped into a lion merely because rampant lions are familiar.

Head direction can be specified separately from body attitude. **Guardant** turns the head toward the viewer; **regardant** turns it backward. A lion passant guardant is therefore not simply “a walking lion.” The front-facing head is part of the description. Small additions to the words can make large differences to the image.

In damaged objects, posture may be easier to establish than species, or species easier than posture. Record the uncertainty at the relevant level. “Quadruped rampant, species uncertain” is a useful description when the mane, tail, or head has been lost. It does not require you to abandon all description because one feature is unresolved.

## Wings, tails, and attributes

Birds introduce their own vocabulary for wing positions and flight. An eagle **displayed** spreads its wings in a conventional frontal arrangement. A bird **volant** is represented in flight. A bird with folded wings is not equivalent to either simply because it belongs to the same species.[^birds]

Animals can also carry objects, wear collars or crowns, or have specified tinctures for particular parts. Terms such as **armed** and **langued** can describe claws, horns, or a tongue according to the animal and usage. These attributes are useful discriminators. A tiny red tongue may be visually secondary, but if specified it belongs in the reproduction.

At the same time, do not promote every decorative highlight into an attribute. An artist may shade a gold beak with brown. That does not necessarily mean the beak is formally specified as brown. Compare the description before turning modeling into a tincture change. The same caution applies to reflected light, photographic color balance, and conservation retouching.

## The martlet deserves a close look

In conventional English usage, a **martlet** is a stylized footless bird. The absence of feet is a diagnostic feature, not permission to draw any bird badly. Fox-Davies discusses it among birds and distinguishes the heraldic figure from ordinary zoological identification.[^martlet] The course uses a dexter-facing martlet, which looks to the viewer's left in a frontal drawing.

Do not assume that **martlet** and French **merlette** are interchangeable in every detail. Regional vocabulary and artistic conventions require comparison. Record the original term and inspect the relevant depiction. A bilingual dictionary may offer an equivalent suitable for lookup while a philological study still needs to investigate differences in use.

It is equally unwise to infer cadency whenever you encounter a martlet. A martlet may be a principal charge, a badge element, or a difference in a particular system. Its role is determined by the composition and evidence. Unit 8 will examine the English cadency sequence; that sequence does not convert every martlet in world heraldry into someone's fourth son.

## Number, arrangement, and visual grouping

Three charges can be arranged two and one, in a horizontal row, in a vertical column, or along an ordinary. These are different configurations. A repeated charge may be distributed across the field as a pattern rather than counted as a fixed small group. Terms such as **in fess**, **in pale**, and **in bend** describe arrangement; they do not necessarily introduce the corresponding ordinary.[^arrangement]

**Constructed example:** *Argent, three keys Azure in fess* asks for three blue keys arranged horizontally on white. *Argent, on a fess Azure three keys Or* asks for gold keys on a blue band. The vocabulary overlaps, but the layer structure differs. The first has keys and field; the second has field, ordinary, and keys.

For a catalogue, separate **charge count** from **distribution**. If the figure is repeated as a semy, an image may show a count determined by its crop and the artist's spacing. That observed count should not silently replace the patterned description. You can count what the image shows while acknowledging that the armorial specification is not a fixed count.

## Objects need diagnostic descriptions too

Tools and buildings are not inherently simpler than animals. A key has an orientation, a ward, and sometimes an interaction with another key. A tower can have openings and additional specified features. A book may be open or closed, and an inscription may be integral rather than an artist's filler. Name what the source gives you and identify what the image actually preserves.[^objects]

A useful design test is to remove internal detail and inspect the silhouette. Can a viewer still tell a hammer from a key, or a tower from a rectangular block? This is an original practical exercise. It does not explain why every historical artist drew a charge in a particular way. It helps you identify which features a successful teaching reproduction must retain.

Avoid technological anachronism when interpreting an old object. A shape that resembles a modern electrical symbol may be a conventional medieval figure. Conversely, a modern grant can include a newly introduced object. Date the witness and consult its description before deciding that a familiar-looking form has a timeless meaning.

## Documentary encounter: symbolism with an identified source

The Canadian Public Register's record for **Grant MacEwan University**, dated **20 May 2010**, is a confirmation at **volume V, page 516**. It describes a crest with a sparrow on a walking staff and separately explains the association of the bird with the university's namesake.[^macewan] This is the kind of evidence that supports an attributed symbolic explanation.

The explanatory connection belongs to this university’s record. We can follow it from the named bearer to the crest and then to the register’s commentary. Encountering a sparrow in another coat supplies only the shared figure; it supplies none of the other links. The same applies to the walking staff. Attribute the explanation to the register so that a reader can see why it applies here and what would be needed to make a comparable claim elsewhere.

The register's text and image also have different reproduction considerations. This chapter links to the record rather than reproducing the contemporary artwork. Consult the register's rights guidance before copying an image. Access for study and permission for reuse are distinct questions.

## Describe a charge as a bundle of features

An identifying description needs several variables: figure type, count, attitude, body direction, head direction, attributes, tinctures, and arrangement. Some can be omitted when a convention supplies them; others must be explicit. The point is not to make every blazon longer. It is to know which information the words carry and which information the reader is expected to supply. A compact description succeeds when its intended audience can recover the same structure.

Suppose three drawings show a red lion. One walks with a forepaw raised; one rises on a hind leg; one lies down. Their figure type and tincture agree, but their attitudes differ. Hold the attitude constant and turn the head toward the viewer in another version. That changes head direction without necessarily changing the body's action. Add a collar in a third. Each operation alters a separate feature. A catalogue that records only lion and red loses distinctions that may identify the design.

This model makes uncertainty precise. A damaged beast may preserve its raised body and long tail while losing its head. You can identify a rampant quadruped and describe the tail without deciding the species. A small bird may preserve folded wings but no feet. You can record that visible posture while acknowledging that damage, cropping, or convention could explain the missing feet. Each field should carry the confidence its evidence warrants.

Use one inventory row per distinct charge group. Preserve the original term beside a normalized name. If two records use different words for apparently similar figures, their difference becomes a research question rather than disappearing into the database. A reference resource should help recover what the source said, not merely what its editor hoped it meant.

## Attitude and direction are independent instructions

The elementary English lion vocabulary distinguishes rampant, passant, statant, sejant, couchant, and dormant. Head direction can be added with guardant or regardant. These combinations describe an articulated body rather than a generic animal sticker. A lion passant guardant is walking with its head turned toward the observer; a passant lion looking backward has another head instruction. The combination matters even when the images share almost the same body outline.

A useful drawing method begins with a stick skeleton. Mark the spine, hindquarters, raised forelimb, head, and tail. Establish attitude and direction before adding a mane or claws. A beautiful face cannot repair the wrong posture. Conversely, a simplified lion can remain recognizable if its diagnostic structure survives. The exercise teaches which parts perform identification and which supply artistic character.

Do not universalize a posture term across species. A deer introduces antlers and hooves; a horse has another structure; a fish uses another orientation vocabulary. **Haurient** describes an upright fish-like position, while **naiant** describes swimming horizontally in elementary English usage.[^fish] A displayed eagle's wings are not a quadruped's rampant attitude transferred to a feathered body. Vocabulary follows the form and conventional treatment of the figure.

Pairs add another relationship. Figures facing one another or turned back to back can be described through such terms as **combatant**, **respectant**, and **addorsed**, according to the figure and source convention.[^pair-vocabulary] Each member still has an attitude and attributes. If one has a crown and the other does not, the arrangement's symmetry cannot authorize a second crown. Describe the preserved figures independently before reconstructing a damaged pair. A mirror-symmetrical restoration may be plausible without being supported.

## A lion and a leopard can be a language problem

Modern zoological names are not always the governing taxonomy of a historical blazon. In French heraldic vocabulary, **lion** and **léopard** can distinguish conventional bodily attitudes and head treatment within a lion-like figure rather than two modern biological species. That distinction is part of French heraldic vocabulary. The linked IRHT guide is a general heraldic orientation, **not** a cited illustration of the lion-versus-léopard contrast.[^french-animals] A literal translation that produces a spotted modern cat can change the historical image.

Keep the source term, identify its conventional form in the relevant language, and compare the depiction. An English note can explain the lion-like morphology and the defined posture. That is more informative than silently replacing the noun or insisting the historical writer was zoologically confused. The writer may have been using a technical category with another purpose.

The complication generalizes. A conventional dolphin, griffin, or martlet need not reproduce a natural specimen. A plant can be stylized; an object name can refer to earlier technology. Successful identification respects the source's classificatory system before importing a modern scientific one. Biological knowledge can help describe a real species without overriding a documented conventional figure.

For a multilingual concordance, compare a defined expression rather than a noun in isolation. Record posture, head direction, attributes, and image context. A dictionary equivalent helps find records; technical equivalence needs those additional features. The aim is to translate the information that controls the drawing.

## Wings make several different statements

Bird figures need separate attention to wing position, body direction, and flight. A displayed eagle spreads its wings in a conventional frontal form, while the head has its own direction. A bird volant is represented in flight. Wings elevated, lowered, folded, or addorsed create other relationships. A profile bird with wings raised is not the same structure as a frontal eagle with outstretched wings.

The MacEwan crest introduced earlier specifies a sparrow's wing arrangement on a walking staff.[^macewan] The staff has its own orientation. These are connected figures with separate instructions. A generic flying sparrow above a vertical staff would lose the perch relationship and the staff's direction even though every named object appeared somewhere in the picture.

Germany's notice of 20 January 1950 supplies an actual eagle specification: it defines head, wings, plumage, and colored parts, distinguishes an unframed eagle, and reserves artistic realization for particular purposes while referring to authoritative models.[^german-eagle] Small morphological instructions coexist with artistic freedom. This is a defined official design, not a universal eagle template.

Compare head count, head direction, wings, tail, and attributes before assigning political significance. A double-headed eagle supplies a visible distinction; its meaning depends on bearer and source. A single-headed eagle's direction may be specified, but a photograph can be reversed. Establish the represented form and the image's production history before building the historical argument.

## The martlet is a small bird with large editorial consequences

The site's martlet makes a useful laboratory subject because it looks simple. A footless stylized bird still needs a recognizable body, head, wings, and tail. Removing feet alone does not turn an arbitrary bird into a competent martlet. At small scale, silhouette and negative spaces may carry more information than tiny feather lines. Simplification can preserve the figure; changing its structural or directional features requires a reason.

The Anglo-Norman Dictionary's entry **merlet** separates English martlet and French merlette descriptions and records dated heraldic usages.[^and-martlet] This makes the language issue concrete. We have historical spellings and genre-specific uses rather than an assumption that all similar small birds share one timeless definition. Follow the dictionary's citations to editions and, where possible, manuscripts. It is a scholarly guide to usage, not a photograph of every bird it names.

A contemporary Canadian example introduces another form. John Cameron Cairns's 1995 grant names **Canadian martlets**; its commentary describes wings and tail forming a maple-leaf shape. The French blazon uses **martinets canadiens**, while the commentary also uses **merlettes**.[^canadian-martlet] A one-word translation table would conceal the difference between formal description and explanatory prose within this bilingual record.

We can therefore ask three distinct questions: what an elementary English martlet looks like, what a historical French source meant by merlette, and what this Canadian grant specifies by its modified figure. Their answers can be connected without being identical. A renderer with one generic martlet cannot claim to reproduce all three faithfully. A small bird is perfectly capable of exposing a large data-model problem.

Role remains another variable. A martlet can be a principal charge, badge element, or documented difference. Cory Douglas Martinson's register commentary gives explicit personal and name-related explanations for its martlets.[^martinson] That is evidence for an attributed interpretation. Another shield's bird does not carry those explanations with it. Shape identifies a figure; its place in a historical argument needs a source.

## Heads, limbs, and halves need their own morphology

A head can appear in profile or frontally, with different treatments at its cut edge. **Couped** describes a clean cutoff; **erased** a conventional jagged termination. **Caboshed** presents a head facing the viewer without the usual neck extension. A **demi** figure represents a defined portion of an animal or person.[^parts-vocabulary] These are meaningful features, not merely the artist's decision to save space.

An erased head does not establish that a creature was actually decapitated in the story of the arms. The term describes the charge's form. A demi-lion above a crest support is not necessarily a damaged full lion; the source can specify a half figure. Historical narrative must be established separately rather than extracted from the violence implicit in an edge treatment.

On a broken object, an irregular lower edge might belong to an erased charge or the physical fragment. Look for the surrounding field, preserved contour, source description, and other depictions. An isolated head cannot automatically supply the complete design. Record whether the visible edge belongs to the figure or the break, and state uncertainty where the photograph cannot decide.

Hands and arms pose another orientation problem. Identify palm or back, thumb, fingers, and relation to the composition. A hand's side is not safely inferred from its position on the viewer's left. A later drawing that reverses it may change the specified figure while remaining superficially convincing. Component anatomy is evidence; general resemblance is only a starting point.

## Attributes can identify the charge as strongly as the animal

A collar, crown, chain, weapon, tool, or plant can distinguish uses of the same species. Attributes have their own positions and sometimes their own tinctures. A crowned lion is not merely a lion drawn regally; a lion holding a key has a defined relationship to another object. A faithful reconstruction retains the attribute even when it simplifies artistic detail.

At small scale, a collar may disappear while the species remains identifiable. That does not make the collar irrelevant to the arms. If the reproduction cannot display it, state the limitation or choose a larger format. A documentary illustration should not turn a production difficulty into a changed blazon. A deliberately simplified brand mark can be useful while remaining a derivative whose relationship to the arms needs explanation.

The Royal Canadian Geographical Society's College of Fellows badge combines narwhals with the Society's central compass-and-maple-leaf device.[^narwhals] The record identifies both composition and institutional use. A narwhal-only inventory loses the relation to the compass; a compass-only inventory loses the surrounding animals. The badge's identity is assembled from their relationships.

Its commentary connects the northern animal with Arctic inquiry in this particular emblem. Attribute that explanation. It does not make every narwhal signify this Society or the same activity. Even where an association is widespread, an argument about a chooser's intention requires evidence for that chooser. Otherwise a symbolism section becomes a supply of interchangeable virtues.

## Plants combine stylization with specific identification

Flowers, leaves, fruits, branches, trees, and sheaves can be conventionally stylized or specifically described. A heraldic rose is a conventional figure; a named natural flower proper raises another representational question. A branch may have a fixed leaf or fruit count. A tree can grow from a mount or have exposed roots according to the description. Vegetation can carry structural information as exact as an ordinary's direction.

Consider a fictional oak branch with three leaves and two acorns. If those counts are explicit, adding more changes information. If a source simply requests an oak branch, the artist may have more freedom while still representing the requested plant competently. A generic green spray can be attractive and botanically inadequate. Heraldic stylization and specific identification should cooperate.

Alfreda Jean Attrill's register entry provides a useful actual case: trilliums form a repeating field treatment, and the commentary attributes a name allusion to them.[^attrill] Without the commentary, the plant would support a possible pun, not a report of documented intention. The example also shows that a flower need not carry only territorial symbolism. Its role depends on the local record.

The Canadian Royal Crown supplies a modern composite using maple leaves, a snowflake, and a wavy band among its features.[^canadian-crown] Natural forms become parts of a defined object, which can itself appear in another display. A faithful drawing needs the crown's specification before treating it as a generic royal crown. Nested identity applies to objects as well as marshalled shields.

## Inanimate objects have a technology and a date

A key is identifiable through bow, shaft, and ward. A wheel has hub and rim, with spokes where required. A tower has a silhouette and openings. A book can be open, closed, bound in a specified tincture, or charged with writing. Those features distinguish a requested object from a vaguely similar shape. Historical technologies also differ from their present counterparts; the most familiar modern version is not automatically the right one.

Three upright keys can form a horizontal group. Their individual orientation is palewise; their arrangement is in fess. Horizontal keys can form a vertical group instead. Cross two keys and they acquire another relation; specify which lies above and the construction becomes more exact. Count and type cannot recover all these instructions.

A conventional millrind is not a circuit symbol because its shape looks familiar on a screen. A modern grant may deliberately introduce a contemporary instrument. Neither conclusion follows from resemblance alone. Locate the source term, date, and relevant technology before assigning a historical role. The language and object history can settle an ambiguity that a modern visual analogy creates.

**Proper** likewise needs a defined referent. A wooden tool, metal key, or book can involve several materials and colors. The source image or commentary may help establish the intended realization. Proper invokes the represented object's recognized appearance in the relevant convention; it does not authorize any attractive palette. Unit 3 explains why natural coloration is itself a source-specific question.

## Monsters are specified combinations, not failed zoology

A griffin combines recognizable animal parts into a conventional figure. A unicorn has diagnostic features distinguishing it from an ordinary horse. Other hybrid charges combine bodies, heads, wings, tails, or human features. The combination is the identity. Replacing a griffin with an eagle because its upper half looks avian simplifies away the charge rather than merely reducing detail.

Identify the parts and their joints: head, forequarters, hindquarters, wings, tail, and attributes. Then consult the source's conventional name. This produces a reconstructible form even when the vocabulary is unfamiliar. The goal is understanding the menagerie, not winning a competition to recite it. A student who can explain why a hybrid is assembled a particular way is better prepared than one who knows its name but draws the wrong rear half.

Do not automatically explain a hybrid as accidental marshalling. Dimidiation can create joined forms, but an intentionally specified monster is another mechanism. Begin by asking whether the source names one charge or portions of separate coats. An unusual silhouette cannot provide its own compositional history. Unit 9 develops how a joined image can become evidence of a relationship without becoming independent proof of it.

A contemporary grant can define a new compound charge. That is one reason a finite illustration library cannot exhaust heraldry. Institutions and language can specify forms outside the software's inventory. Retain an unfamiliar name and definition instead of substituting the nearest available animal. Tool coverage describes an implementation, not the limits of armorial possibility.

## Human figures require context as well as anatomy

A person can be a saint, officeholder, personification, named historical figure, or conventional body part. Clothing, attributes, gestures, and inscriptions can identify the figure. A key can support a saint identification within a defined religious context, but the object alone cannot settle it. The source's description and the image's diagnostic features should work together.

Historical terms may encode assumptions about race, gender, occupation, and status. Preserve source wording in a transcription, then explain its language editorially rather than silently endorse its categories. A present description can identify visible features while distinguishing them from a source's social classification. This follows the same separation between witness and interpretation used for names, colors, and animals.

Institutional commentary can document what an authority says a person represents without resolving every historical question about the representation. Ask who selected the image and whose account survives. A relevant community may supply evidence absent from the grant commentary. Ground that inquiry in the actual case rather than manufacture a controversy. An authoritative caption begins an interpretive trail; it need not be its final boundary.

New teaching designs should use explicitly fictional figures or appropriately documented references. State what was constructed. The task is to teach form and reasoning, not turn another community's sign into decorative raw material without understanding its authority. The public-life chapter develops how institutional designs make representational claims.

## Fixed count and repeating treatment answer different questions

A group of three charges has a fixed count. A field semy has a repeating treatment whose visible count responds to support and spacing unless the description gives additional constraints. An edge cuts a pattern unit; a fold hides one. Counting visible instances does not automatically establish the design's required total. Keep the observation and specification separate.

Arrangement remains another constraint. Three figures two and one, in pale, in fess, or in bend can share count and type while making different designs. A group around an ordinary respects that ordinary's space. Fitting the composition into a narrow marshalled compartment changes proportions without necessarily changing the meaningful arrangement. Equal numerical coordinates do not guarantee equal visual space.

Damage complicates count. Two complete stars and a fragment might support a reconstruction of three, but the inference depends on the fragment and the object's extent. A blazon can strengthen it. An empty symmetric area alone cannot supply a missing charge. Distinguish complete observations, fragmentary observations, and reconstructed elements rather than present all three as equally visible.

The distinctions affect research statistics too. Counting each rose in a semy as an occurrence makes patterned designs dominate the result. Counting each shield once asks another question. Neither method is inherently wrong; their denominators differ. State whether the unit is a depicted charge, group, design, or bearer. A number becomes an argument after its unit is defined.

The results describe these readers and drawings. They can expose ambiguous silhouettes and improve a teaching asset. ## Research project: one figure across languages

Choose a narrow charge family and assemble a dated multilingual corpus with bearers, original descriptions, and image locations. Compare features the vocabulary distinguishes. A martlet study can record feet, beak, wings, and tail; a lion study can record attitude and head direction. Avoid collecting everything sharing an English name and treating that name as the conclusion.

Build a concordance with source term, working equivalent, morphology, and evidence. Include an example challenging the initial mapping. Explain whether it reflects regional convention, historical change, ambiguous language, damage, or another figure. If the evidence cannot decide, retain the alternatives and identify what would help. The useful resource teaches how to handle an exception instead of removing it from the table.

Conclude by explaining what travels between languages and what a translation must supply explicitly. A dozen controlled examples can sustain a substantial argument. The accomplishment is making the distinctions recoverable, not accumulating the longest possible list of animals.

## Workshop and answer discussion

Select three animal charges from documented sources. For each, record species or figure type, attitude, orientation, attributes, tincture, arrangement, and source location. Leave a field explicitly uncertain if the witness cannot settle it. Then choose one and write a brief explanation of which features must survive a simplified drawing.
