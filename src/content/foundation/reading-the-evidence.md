## Make an argument that another person can check

By now, you can describe a shield and recognize some of the documents that record its use. The final workshop asks you to connect those skills in an argument another researcher can examine. Begin with a question, select evidence capable of answering it, and show how the conclusion follows. The size of the achievement or bibliography matters less than whether the reader can retrace that connection. A modest result can be useful when its supporting steps are clear.

There are two routes. The **documentary route** studies an existing, securely located witness. The **design route** constructs an original teaching proposal and researches the framework in which such a design would be used. Both require source criticism. The second does not manufacture a grant, and the first does not require you to claim the right to bear the arms you study.

The introductory knowledge check records comprehension in your browser. The capstone is a separate piece of work. The rubric below is a self-assessment framework; it does not issue academic credit or certify armorial rights. Its value comes from making the standards visible before you begin.

## Choose a question small enough to answer

“What is the history of heraldry?” is much too large for a first project. “How does a particular register entry distinguish a shield, crest, and badge?” is manageable. So is “Which features of a museum object can be securely described from its catalogue and image, and which remain unresolved?”

A useful question identifies an object or corpus, a period, and an operation. Compare, transcribe, identify, reconstruct, or evaluate. Avoid a question that already assumes the desired answer. “Why does this lion prove noble ancestry?” smuggles its conclusion into the question. “What evidence connects this lion-bearing design to a particular bearer?” allows the evidence to decide.

Write the question before collecting a hundred images. Otherwise the availability of attractive illustrations starts defining the research. You may revise the question when the evidence demands it, but record the revision and its reason. A narrower answer supported by real evidence is a successful project, not a disappointment.

## Route A: a documented armorial study

Choose a museum object, manuscript entry, seal, institutional grant or registration, or architectural display with a traceable record. Start with one witness. Record its institution, title, identifier, date or date range, medium, location within the record, stable URL, and access date. Identify whether you inspected the original, a digital image, a transcription, or only the catalogue.

Describe the visible composition without importing the expected blazon. Then compare your description with any source text. Where do they agree? Where does the image supply information absent from the text? Where does the text supply information the image cannot establish? Distinguish artistic variation from possible substantive discrepancy.

For a small ownership problem, examine the [Thomas Cary bookplate](/objects/cary-bookplate/). The name is visible on the engraving; actual use in a particular surviving book requires evidence from that book. The study distinguishes this observation from the catalogue’s maker and date range. Bring it together with another witness in the [comparative research bench](/objects/compare/), then export a notebook with both collection records attached.

If the source attributes the arms to a bearer, preserve its level of certainty. The Met's targe record, for example, gives an alternative family attribution rather than a single settled bearer.[^object] Your project should not become more certain than its source merely because a definitive caption is easier to write.

Write a 1,200–1,800-word study addressing the question. Include a witness inventory and a claim ledger. You may use a linked image where reproduction permission is unavailable. If you make a teaching reconstruction, label it as a reconstruction and state its evidentiary basis.

## Route B: a design with a documented framework

Begin with a fictional bearer or an explicitly educational proposal. Choose a defined setting and consult its relevant institutional or customary sources. State which elements the teaching exercise may model and which formal acts it cannot perform. Do not call the result granted, registered, or legally available without the act and evidence that would support that description.

Prepare a shield design, a blazon, a plain-language explanation, and a visual layer audit. Use a limited palette and a composition that survives reduction. If you propose a crest or badge, describe it separately. Any external ornaments should be justified within the chosen framework rather than added to fill space.

Explain the chosen symbolism as **your design intention**. You can choose a key to represent access, a plant to evoke a place, or an abstract shape to recall a profession. That choice does not make the meaning universal. The explanation should use “I chose” or “the proposal uses” rather than presenting invented intention as ancient custom.

Research comparable designs and describe the scope of the search. A few keyword searches do not prove worldwide uniqueness. The result can honestly be an independently created teaching proposal whose comprehensive clearance remains unresolved. Independent creation and confirmed non-conflict are different claims.

## Build a witness inventory

A **witness** is a source manifestation you actually use. A manuscript image, edited transcription, museum catalogue, and modern reconstruction may concern the same object but are not identical witnesses. Give each a record so that a reader can retrace your work.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Witness ID | Your stable local label, clearly distinct from an archive identifier |
| Source institution or author | Who holds or produced the consulted source |
| Object or document identifier | Actual shelfmark, accession, register location, or publication citation |
| Date | Source date, object date, and later publication date where different |
| Location | Page, folio, entry, image number, or named section actually consulted |
| Access form | Original, scan, photograph, edition, catalogue, or derivative image |
| Language | Language of the text, preserving original terms |
| Rights | Image permission or license, and a separate text-reuse basis |
| Limitations | Missing parts, uncertainty, derivative dependence, or unexamined layers |

Do not invent an archive shelfmark to make the table look complete. “Identifier not established” is a valid research state. It tells you and the next reader exactly what is missing. Likewise, a general homepage is not a substitute for a source location when the argument depends on one entry.

## A dating problem you can actually check

The Met’s [*Seal Matrix*, accession 1981.291](/objects/ghent-style-matrix/) puts two periods into one catalogue date field: nineteenth- or twentieth-century manufacture, in thirteenth-century style.[^matrix] A hurried transcription could keep only the medieval period. The resulting record would be precise, searchable, and wrong.

Give manufacture and style separate fields in your working inventory. The first answers when the physical object was made, according to the catalogue. The second identifies a period used as a stylistic reference. They may eventually be supported by different comparisons or documents. Keeping both makes it possible to explain how the object enters your historical argument.

The photograph presents a second problem. A metal matrix appears beside an orange impression. Their arrangements are reversed, making the relationship between a cut face and a relief image easier to inspect. But that helpful comparison does not give the accompanying impression the same date as the matrix. Nor does the matrix’s bronze medium tell you what the orange material is. Create a separate witness entry, with those fields unresolved.

This is why the historical question belongs at the beginning of a project. If you are investigating later representations of medieval seal imagery, the matrix may be directly relevant. If you are trying to prove that someone used this particular matrix in the thirteenth century, the museum’s dating presents an immediate problem. The same object can be useful for one question and misleading for another.

There is also a temptation to replace uncertainty with a dramatic explanation. A later object in an earlier style might have been made as a reproduction, a revival piece, or for some other purpose. Calling it a deliberate forgery requires evidence of deceptive representation or intent. The date alone does not establish that history. Separate the catalogue’s dating from the story you would like the dating to tell.

Write a revised catalogue note containing manufacture period, stylistic reference, the two photographed witnesses, and one unresolved question. Then use the [seal evidence workshop](/objects/seals/) to test what each sentence claims to know. You can keep the two objects and their source records together in the [comparative notebook](/objects/compare/?left=lowlands-wax-seal&right=ghent-style-matrix).

## Route A in practice: edit a short manuscript inscription

An inscription is a useful first documentary project because it gives you a small object of study without making the research trivial. You can locate a few words, inspect the form in which they survive, compare a modern reading, and explain the decisions needed to turn the witness into a text. The result is an edition at a modest scale. Its quality depends on the traceability of its decisions, rather than on the length of the passage.

The [manuscript transcription desk](/transcription-desk/) began with two dossiers. One reproduces Getty’s full photograph of *Simon de Varie Kneeling in Prayer*, a French illuminated leaf dated 1455 and assigned to Jean Fouquet in the museum catalogue. The other links the Torre do Tombo’s Portuguese presentation of the *Livro do Armeiro-Mor*, 1509, book 19, folio 87. The first French photograph is rights-cleared under the CC0 declaration in the museum’s IIIF manifest. The Portuguese image remains at the archive because this project has not verified its reuse terms. A third dossier now adds a separately located French opening for the comparison exercise below.[^manuscripts]

These are deliberately different encounters. At the Getty desk you can inspect lettering beside the working fields. At the Portuguese desk you open the archive’s PDF separately. In either case, describe what you actually consulted. A student who cannot open the Portuguese image can still study its verified modern caption, but must identify the work as a caption study. An access failure cannot be cured by silently pretending to have seen the manuscript.

### Start with a policy, then write the text

Before transcribing, choose the span. Identify the scroll, line, or inscription within the located leaf. A whole miniature contains more information than one motto, and a manuscript contains more than one miniature. State the scope precisely enough that a reader knows what you left outside the exercise.

Then explain your transcription policy. Will you preserve letter case, line breaks, decorative separators, and joined forms? How will you mark a supplied letter, a doubtful reading, or an unreadable span? There are established editorial conventions for particular disciplines and projects, but an unexplained mixture of them is hard to use. For this introductory assignment, a small, consistently applied and explicitly stated policy is the essential starting point.

Keep three texts separate. The **diplomatic transcription** represents the witness’s written form at the level your policy promises. The **normalized reading** presents the words with declared editorial changes, such as regularized case, supplied spaces, or expanded abbreviations. The **translation** expresses the established reading in English. None should invisibly overwrite the others.

This division makes correction possible. Suppose another reader disagrees with your expansion of a joined form. They can inspect the diplomatic text, challenge the normalization, and see whether the English meaning changes. If all you publish is fluent English, the disagreement becomes harder to locate. It is unclear whether the error arose in the letter reading, the expansion, or the translation.

### Let the apparatus carry the difficult decisions

A **critical apparatus** records the places where the text needs explanation. It is not a collection of impressive footnotes added after the argument is finished. It is the working record of why you made a reading, what alternatives remain, and what evidence might settle them.

Give each entry a locus: lower scroll, third word group, for example. Describe the observed form before naming your preferred resolution. State the proposed reading, its review status, and its basis. If you use another leaf as a comparison, locate that leaf separately. If you rely on the catalogue, say so. Two internet pages repeating one catalogue reading are not two independent inspections of the hand.

The desk’s statuses are practical labels. *Unresolved* leaves the reading open. *Provisional* records a working choice that still needs a defined check. *Checked* records your review step; it does not imply approval by the museum, archive, or a specialist. Name the check and explain its result. A form checked against a better photograph is a different result from a form merely checked against a modern caption.

Record changes of mind. Perhaps the first draft supplied a word because it was familiar, while a later inspection revealed an extra letter. Retain the earlier export and note the revision. A correction is easier to trust when its reason is visible. Erasing the old decision without explanation loses part of the argument’s history.

### Dates belong to particular things

Getty’s folio introduces a dating problem alongside its lettering. The museum reports partial overpainting of the border arms. The manuscript’s catalogue year therefore cannot serve as an automatic date for every visible armorial surface. Your inventory should distinguish the leaf as catalogued, the particular shield or border element you are discussing, and any reported intervention.[^getty-leaf]

This matters when an armorial image becomes a database entry. A neat record with one name, one shield, and one year can silently combine a fifteenth-century manuscript with a later painted form and a modern identification. Give each statement a source and specify the historical event its date describes. A catalogue date is evidence about the catalogue’s dating; an independently dated intervention would require its own witness.

An altered manuscript remains useful. It may answer questions about later representation, ownership, collecting, or appropriation. Those questions need evidence suited to the intervention. Do not throw the leaf away because it changed, and do not remove the change from your description because it complicates a clean illustration.

### A caption does not become a manuscript by proximity

The Portuguese dossier makes source mediation equally visible. The archive’s caption identifies the volume and folio and supplies a modern quoted designation. That is useful institutional evidence. It does not establish that the caption preserves every spelling, abbreviation, accent, or space in the manuscript hand. Compare the two only after inspecting the actual inscription.[^manuscripts]

The distinction between folio and PDF page is a simple control with large consequences. The archival citation is book 19, folio 87; the presentation locates its image on PDF page 23. Record both. The latter is a navigation aid for this file, while the former identifies the archived location. Treating those numbers as interchangeable could send another researcher to an entirely different image.

Write a 600-word note answering one narrow question: “What editorial interventions are needed to move this short inscription from the facsimile into a normalized English reading?” Include the three text layers, two apparatus entries, and a final sentence naming the most useful unresolved check. If you worked only from the caption, explain that limit and ask the corresponding question about the modern caption instead.

### Keep a portable research record

The desk saves separate drafts in your browser for each witness. That convenience does not replace a backup. Export the JSON to restore the fields and apparatus on another device, and export Markdown for a readable report. Both carry the source locator and the distinction between a working draft and institutional approval. A witness link shares the selected dossier without including your notes.

Before loading an imported draft, review its witness and preview. Export the destination witness’s existing draft if you want to preserve it. The desk validates the file format and uses its own source metadata, so an imported file cannot redefine the museum URL or archival location. Validation makes restoration safer; it does not validate the handwriting or the historical argument.

Continue with the [full manuscript workshop](/transcription-desk/reading/) for a paired French and Portuguese assignment and an editorial rubric. The short inscription can become part of your documentary capstone, provided the scope of every claim remains clear.

## Route A extended: what does a second witness add?

A second source can strengthen an argument, redirect it, or merely repeat what the first source already said. Before adding another image to your capstone, write one sentence about its relationship to the first. Is it another photograph of the same surface? Another occurrence within the same manuscript? An independent copy? A modern description based on an edition you already consulted? These relationships support different uses of agreement.

The [manuscript comparison bench](/transcription-desk/compare/) starts with Getty folios 2 and 2v from the *Hours of Simon de Varie*. Its third dossier is *Coat of Arms Held by a Woman and a Greyhound*, Ms. 7, folio 2v, accession 85.ML.27.2v. The museum record identifies that opening; the photograph lets you locate the present scrolls and collar lettering. Its reproduced image has a separate Wikimedia Commons Public Domain Mark / PD-Art record. Keep that provenance distinct from the first image’s Getty CC0 manifest.[^collation]

These are two openings in one book. A comparison can establish how a chosen inscription recurs there, subject to the readings you can actually make. It does not supply an unrelated manuscript copy. This is a worthwhile result. It becomes misleading only if your conclusion claims independence that the source inventory does not establish.

### Give the comparison a defined unit

Select one occurrence on each leaf and describe its location before transcribing it. Pairing “motto” with “motto” is too broad when a leaf presents lettering on more than one scroll and on a collar. The bench’s located entries let you distinguish those occurrences. Record the visible extent, the direction of reading, and any fold or obstruction that interrupts the sequence.

Apply a shared notation policy to both diplomatic readings. Otherwise your comparison may discover your own inconsistent treatment of capitals, line breaks, or joined forms. If the sources demand different treatment, explain the difference rather than forcing them into a policy that hides what matters. A short difficult passage can teach more than a long transcript whose decisions remain invisible.

The exact-text preview highlights differences in the strings you entered. It includes whitespace, punctuation, case, and accents. It neither reads the painted letters nor chooses the historically correct reading. Treat it as a check on your transcription record. A red or green segment gives you a question to investigate; it does not give you a historical answer.

Run the normalized comparison next. If a difference disappears, identify the intervention responsible. Perhaps both readings now use ordinary word spacing or consistent case. That disappearance tells the reader how your edition works. It does not retroactively remove the difference from the objects. The normalized agreement should therefore accompany the diplomatic evidence, rather than replace it.

### Explain agreement as carefully as disagreement

An apparatus entry may record an agreement. State what was compared and at what level. “The normalized wording agrees” is a narrower, more reproducible statement than “the inscriptions are identical.” It leaves room for arrangement, letter forms, omissions, and material history to differ.

Likewise, record when an apparent resolution depends on the second occurrence. If you supplied a doubtful collar letter from a legible scroll, the completed reading is not independent evidence that both occurrences preserve that letter. Preserve the doubtful observation, the supplied resolution, and the reason. This avoids a circular argument in which your reconstruction becomes confirmation of the source used to construct it.

Material questions need their own evidence. Getty’s account of folio 2v reports later repainting of the shield. A recurring inscription elsewhere on the leaf does not date that intervention or establish the earlier shield’s exact form. The catalogue statement, your description of the present photograph, and a proposed reconstruction belong to separate claims with separate support.

### Submit the trail with the argument

Extend your documentary study with three located comparison entries and a 400–600-word discussion of the second witness’s contribution. Include at least one editorial intervention, one agreement whose limits you explain, and one unresolved check. Use the [collation workshop](/transcription-desk/collation/) for the full method and rubric.

Export the comparison as well as the single-witness drafts. The bench retains imported apparatus and source records in its report while keeping its own edited reading copies. If you change a reading at the desk, review that changed draft before loading it into the comparison. A dated export records which version supported the submitted argument. A pair link identifies the sources, but carries none of your analysis.

Your final claim can now name the added evidence precisely: another located occurrence within one manuscript, a demonstrated difference between editorial layers, or a source limitation that changes the scope of the question. A smaller claim with a reproducible trail is stronger than a large claim resting on an unexplained second image.

## Build a claim ledger

A claim ledger links propositions to evidence. It need not be complicated. Its value is that it exposes where a sentence contains more than one assertion or where several sources all depend on the same earlier witness.

| Claim | Supporting witness | What the witness actually establishes | Remaining inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| The catalogue dates the object to a range | Catalogue record | The institution's published dating | Whether the dating should be revised |
| A figure appears in the image | Located photograph | The visible form in that photograph | Species or function if unclear |
| A record associates a design with a bearer | Register entry | The recorded association or act | Any wider entitlement claim |
| A proposed symbol expresses a chosen idea | Designer's statement | Present design intention | Historical analogies, if claimed |

This is where an apparently strong paragraph can fall apart usefully. “The family's ancient arms prove this person's noble ancestry” contains several propositions. Each needs its own evidence. Perhaps the design is documented but the descent is not. Perhaps descent is documented but noble status is not. The ledger identifies the actual gap instead of treating the entire paragraph as either true or false.

For an inherited-armorial question, consult the relevant transmission framework and actual instrument. The Scottish and English examples studied earlier demonstrate that documentary processes and conventions differ.[^framework] A capstone should identify the framework it applies rather than quietly combining convenient parts from several systems.

## Rights and attribution belong in the project

The age of a coat of arms and the rights in a modern image are separate questions. An ancient design can have a newly painted copyrighted realization. A museum may release a particular photograph under an open policy. A contemporary register may permit study while restricting reproduction. Identify the actual image and its stated reuse basis.[^rights]

For an illustration you create, say whether it is an independent teaching drawing, a reconstruction from an object, or an adaptation of another image. Preserve attribution where required. Do not call a traced modern image your original historical reconstruction merely because you changed the colors.

This project can be completed without reproducing protected images. Link to the institutional record, provide a carefully attributed description, and use your own labeled teaching diagrams where appropriate. The research argument should not depend on concealing where its visual evidence came from.

## Write in layers

Organize the study so the reader encounters the evidence before the inference. A practical structure is: question and scope; witness and provenance; description or transcription; comparative analysis; conclusion and limits; references and rights. A design-route project can replace transcription with its specification and framework analysis.

Use the original technical term where it carries information. Explain it in ordinary language when first introduced. Keep long strings of vocabulary out of a sentence if a table or component map would make the relationship clearer. The aim is precision that a reader can follow, not a performance of knowing difficult words.

Your conclusion should answer the question you actually studied. It should also identify what would change the conclusion. A newly located register entry, a conservation report, or a better scan are concrete possibilities. “Everything is uncertain” is rarely a useful conclusion; “the bearer's identity remains uncertain because these two candidates share the visible design” is useful.

## A transparent self-assessment rubric

| Dimension | Points | What earns the points |
|---|---:|---|
| Question and scope | 10 | An answerable question, defined witness or proposal, date and setting |
| Source control | 25 | Traceable records, correct identifiers, access forms, and locations |
| Description and specification | 20 | Accurate anatomy, tinctures, viewpoint, grammar, and relationships |
| Evidence and interpretation | 25 | Claims connected to sources; alternatives and dependencies considered |
| Communication | 10 | Clear explanation, usable structure, terms defined where needed |
| Rights and limitations | 10 | Image status, attribution, and unresolved matters stated accurately |

No score compensates for fabricated evidence. If an identifier, quotation, blazon, or translation was invented, correct or remove it and revisit every claim that depends on it. The self-assessment should make revision easier, not encourage a confident numerical disguise for missing support.

To review your own work, ask another reader to follow one significant claim from the prose to its witness. Can they find the location? Does it say what you said it says? Have you hidden a disputed interpretation inside a factual sentence? This small audit tests the central promise of the project.

## Model answer outline: what an honest result looks like

**Constructed model project:** “How much of a painted shield's specification can be recovered from an institutional catalogue and one photograph?” The introduction defines the witness. The inventory distinguishes catalogue metadata from visible features. The description names the field and main figures while flagging indistinct attributes. The comparison notes what a missing blazon would settle. The conclusion states a partial reconstruction and its limits.

<details>
<summary>Why can a partial reconstruction be a strong answer?</summary>

Because the question concerns what can be recovered from defined evidence. A project that accurately recovers five features and identifies two unresolved ones has answered that question. Inventing the missing two features would make the result look more complete while making the scholarship worse. The next step is a targeted search for the missing description or another witness, with the existing observations retained.

</details>

For a design-route model, submit one coherent shield, a reproducible blazon, a source-based account of the chosen framework, and a clearly attributed design rationale. End by distinguishing the educational proposal from any formal grant or exhaustive uniqueness search. The project succeeds by demonstrating the method, not by declaring a new armiger into existence.

## Continue into research

The foundation course prepares you to ask better questions of actual evidence. Continue with the [world traditions](/traditions/), [source atlas](/source-atlas/), [translation laboratory](/translation-lab/), and [research seminars](/seminars/). Those routes develop historical, multilingual, and methodological depth beyond this introductory sequence.

Keep your witness inventory and claim ledger. They can grow into a comparative study, a documentary edition, or a digital armorial project. If a new source changes your conclusion, record the revision rather than erasing the earlier reasoning. Scholarship improves by making its evidence and corrections visible.
