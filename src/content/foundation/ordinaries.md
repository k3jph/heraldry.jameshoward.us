## Geometry becomes vocabulary

An ordinary is a conventional geometric charge. The useful first set includes the **fess**, **pale**, **bend**, **chevron**, **cross**, and **saltire**. A fess is a horizontal band, a pale a vertical band, and a bend a diagonal band from dexter chief to sinister base. A chevron uses two sloping arms meeting above; a cross combines vertical and horizontal arms; a saltire uses diagonal arms.[^shapes]

These figures are straightforward enough to recognize and versatile enough to build a great many compositions. That is why we begin with them. A student can study orientation, tincture, layering, and proportion without also needing to decide whether an animal has the correct ears. Geometry isolates the structural questions.

It would nevertheless be a mistake to think heraldry is Euclidean geometry with old French labels. A chevron's angle can vary with the available space. A cross may have a treatment that distinguishes it from another cross. An artist's job is to preserve the specified figure while making a coherent composition on the actual support. The vocabulary supplies identity; it does not always supply a complete drawing algorithm.

## Classification is a tool, not a discovery of nature

Lists of “honorable ordinaries” and “subordinaries” vary between writers. Fox-Davies devotes the beginning of his chapter to criticizing rigid classifications and purported exact proportions.[^classification] If two manuals put a particular figure in different categories, that disagreement need not make the figure impossible to identify. It may tell you more about the authors' classificatory projects.

For introductory study, learn to recognize the forms and their names. Learn which terms your chosen source uses. Avoid making the hierarchy itself the object of devotion. A rule that helps organize a textbook should not be promoted into a universal historical law simply because it comes in a tidy table.

This also gives us a useful research question. How did different manuals classify the same figures, and why? A comparative study might examine pedagogical organization, national vocabulary, or changing claims about authority. The disagreement is evidence to preserve, not noise to erase from a supposedly perfect master list.

## Shape, width, and placement

Suppose you are drawing a single fess. It needs to read as a band, leaving the field visible above and below. If it bears three charges, it must leave room for them. If the shield also contains other charges, the available space changes. Proportion responds to the composition. A claim that every ordinary must occupy exactly one-third of the shield creates practical contradictions, which Fox-Davies explicitly discusses.[^proportions]

A **chief** occupies the upper part of the shield as a charge. A **bordure** follows its perimeter. A **canton** occupies a corner, conventionally dexter chief unless otherwise described. These are geometric features whose role must be read from the blazon rather than guessed from decorative framing.

Do not confuse these features with the surrounding artwork. A thin black outline around an SVG shield is usually a rendering choice. A bordure is part of the arms when specified as such. A photographic frame is part of neither. Record what belongs to the design separately from what belongs to its depiction.

## Diminutives and repeated bands

Terms such as **bendlet**, **pallet**, **bar**, and **chevronel** name narrower or related forms. The exact vocabulary and its implications should be checked in the relevant tradition. Multiple narrow horizontal charges are not always equivalent to a field divided horizontally into alternating pieces.[^diminutives]

Try this distinction with a constructed pair. In one shield, place two red bars on white. In another, divide the whole field into a specified number of alternating red and white horizontal pieces. The first describes charges on a background. The second describes the field's partition. A picture can make them look close, especially when band widths are adjusted; the grammatical structure still differs.

If a historical blazon uses a term unfamiliar to you, preserve it in the transcription. Add your normalized reading separately. Do not replace a period term with a modern one merely to make a parser accept the record. A tool that cannot represent an expression should report its limit, not force the evidence into a vocabulary it happens to understand.

## An ordinary can itself carry charges

**Constructed teaching example:** *Argent, on a bend Azure three roses Or.* The white field carries a blue bend; the bend carries three gold roses. The preposition **on** expresses the relationship. If you draw the roses loose on the white field, the charge types and tinctures may all be present, but the design is still wrong.

Now compare a bend **between** charges. The surrounding charges lie in the field on either side rather than on the bend. Their arrangement must be specified or supplied by an applicable convention. The difference between “on” and “between” is not decorative punctuation. It is a structural instruction.

The same reasoning applies to nested compositions. Read from the supporting element toward the supported one. In your notes, write “field → bend → roses.” This brief layer record can expose mistakes that a list of nouns will miss. It is also useful for comparing two blazons: they may contain the same nouns and colors while connecting them differently.

## Modified edges and changed figures

An ordinary may have a distinctive edge treatment such as engrailed, wavy, or embattled. The modifier must attach to the correct figure. If a blazon says a bend is engrailed, do not modify the outer edge of the shield. If a line treatment appears on only part of an image, examine the wording before extending it everywhere.[^edges]

Similarly, a cross can be a full ordinary reaching the edges or a separately specified cross form within the field. Its name, termination, and extent matter. “There is a cross” is a start, not a complete identification. A cropped fragment may not preserve enough of the ends to distinguish a cross form. Say so.

This is a useful place to practice humility in description. A carefully stated partial observation is better than a fully specified mistake. “Visible horizontal arm with a flared termination; other ends missing” preserves evidence for a later reader. “Cross pattée” may be tempting, but it requires enough information to justify the identification.

## Worked problem: what changed?

Take the invented design *Azure, a chevron Argent*. Prepare four versions: one with a wider chevron, one with a lower apex, one with the chevron inverted, and one with an engrailed chevron. Which changes affect artistic proportion, and which require descriptive changes?

The first two may be artistic variations if the figure remains a recognizable chevron and no additional specification controls its placement. The inverted figure changes orientation. The engrailed figure changes the edge treatment. Both normally need corresponding wording. The exercise asks you to justify the distinction, not simply to pick whichever version looks best.

Now try making a shield with a fess and two chevrons. If each were required to consume a rigid one-third of the entire surface, there would be no room left for the field or overlap decisions. The absurdity is useful. It shows why an inherited teaching formula should be tested against actual compositions before being accepted as measurement law.

## Workshop and answer discussion

Using only a field and one ordinary, make six original teaching shields. Name each ordinary and explain its orientation. Then choose one and add three charges **on** it. Redraw the same charge types **around** it, supplying a clear verbal arrangement. Compare the resulting blazons and images.
