## Geometry becomes vocabulary

An ordinary is a conventional geometric charge. The useful first set includes the **fess**, **pale**, **bend**, **chevron**, **cross**, and **saltire**. A fess is a horizontal band, a pale a vertical band, and a bend a diagonal band from dexter chief to sinister base. A chevron uses two sloping arms meeting above; a cross combines vertical and horizontal arms; a saltire uses diagonal arms.[^shapes]

These figures are straightforward enough to recognize and versatile enough to build a great many compositions. That is why we begin with them. A student can study orientation, tincture, layering, and proportion without also needing to decide whether an animal has the correct ears. Geometry isolates the structural questions.

It would nevertheless be a mistake to think heraldry is Euclidean geometry with old French labels. A chevron's angle can vary with the available space. A cross may have a treatment that distinguishes it from another cross. An artist's job is to preserve the specified figure while making a coherent composition on the actual support. The vocabulary supplies identity; it does not always supply a complete drawing algorithm.

## Classification is a tool, not a discovery of nature

Lists of “honorable ordinaries” and “subordinaries” vary between writers. Fox-Davies devotes the beginning of his chapter to criticizing rigid classifications and purported exact proportions.[^classification] If two manuals put a particular figure in different categories, that disagreement need not make the figure impossible to identify. It may tell you more about the authors' classificatory projects.

For introductory study, learn to recognize the forms and their names. Learn which terms your chosen source uses. Avoid making the hierarchy itself the object of devotion. A rule that helps organize a textbook should not be promoted into a universal historical law simply because it comes in a tidy table.

This also gives us a useful research question. How did different manuals classify the same figures, and why? A comparative study might examine pedagogical organization, national vocabulary, or changing claims about authority. The disagreement is evidence to preserve, not noise to erase from a supposedly perfect master list.

## Shape, width, and placement

Suppose you are drawing a single fess. It needs to read as a band, leaving the field visible above and below. If it bears three charges, it must leave room for them. If the shield also contains other charges, the available space changes. Proportion responds to the composition. A claim that every ordinary must occupy exactly one-third of the shield creates practical contradictions, which Fox-Davies explicitly discusses.[^proportions]

A **chief** occupies the upper part of the shield as a charge. A **bordure** follows its perimeter. A **canton** occupies a corner, conventionally dexter chief unless otherwise described. These are geometric features whose role must be read from the blazon rather than guessed from decorative framing.

Do not confuse these features with the surrounding artwork. A thin black outline around an SVG shield is usually a rendering choice. A bordure is part of the arms when specified as such. A photographic frame is part of neither. Record what belongs to the design separately from what belongs to its depiction.

## Diminutives and repeated bands

Terms such as **bendlet**, **pallet**, **bar**, and **chevronel** name narrower or related forms. The exact vocabulary and its implications should be checked in the relevant tradition. Multiple narrow horizontal charges are not always equivalent to a field divided horizontally into alternating pieces.[^diminutives]

Try this distinction with a constructed pair. In one shield, place two red bars on white. In another, divide the whole field into a specified number of alternating red and white horizontal pieces. The first describes charges on a background. The second describes the field's partition. A picture can make them look close, especially when band widths are adjusted; the grammatical structure still differs.

If a historical blazon uses a term unfamiliar to you, preserve it in the transcription. Add your normalized reading separately. Do not replace a period term with a modern one merely to make a parser accept the record. A tool that cannot represent an expression should report its limit, not force the evidence into a vocabulary it happens to understand.

## An ordinary can itself carry charges

**Constructed teaching example:** *Argent, on a bend Azure three roses Or.* The white field carries a blue bend; the bend carries three gold roses. The preposition **on** expresses the relationship. If you draw the roses loose on the white field, the charge types and tinctures may all be present, but the design is still wrong.

Now compare a bend **between** charges. The surrounding charges lie in the field on either side rather than on the bend. Their arrangement must be specified or supplied by an applicable convention. The difference between “on” and “between” is not decorative punctuation. It is a structural instruction.

The same reasoning applies to nested compositions. Read from the supporting element toward the supported one. In your notes, write “field → bend → roses.” This brief layer record can expose mistakes that a list of nouns will miss. It is also useful for comparing two blazons: they may contain the same nouns and colors while connecting them differently.

## Modified edges and changed figures

An ordinary may have a distinctive edge treatment such as engrailed, wavy, or embattled. The modifier must attach to the correct figure. If a blazon says a bend is engrailed, do not modify the outer edge of the shield. If a line treatment appears on only part of an image, examine the wording before extending it everywhere.[^edges]

Similarly, a cross can be a full ordinary reaching the edges or a separately specified cross form within the field. Its name, termination, and extent matter. “There is a cross” is a start, not a complete identification. A cropped fragment may not preserve enough of the ends to distinguish a cross form. Say so.

This is a useful place to practice humility in description. A carefully stated partial observation is better than a fully specified mistake. “Visible horizontal arm with a flared termination; other ends missing” preserves evidence for a later reader. “Cross pattée” may be tempting, but it requires enough information to justify the identification.

## Worked problem: what changed?

Take the invented design *Azure, a chevron Argent*. Prepare four versions: one with a wider chevron, one with a lower apex, one with the chevron inverted, and one with an engrailed chevron. Which changes affect artistic proportion, and which require descriptive changes?

The first two may be artistic variations if the figure remains a recognizable chevron and no additional specification controls its placement. The inverted figure changes orientation. The engrailed figure changes the edge treatment. Both normally need corresponding wording. The exercise asks you to justify the distinction, not simply to pick whichever version looks best.

Now try making a shield with a fess and two chevrons. If each were required to consume a rigid one-third of the entire surface, there would be no room left for the field or overlap decisions. The absurdity is useful. It shows why an inherited teaching formula should be tested against actual compositions before being accepted as measurement law.

## The chief, fess, and pale distribute the available space

A chief occupies the upper portion of the shield; a fess crosses its middle horizontally; a pale crosses it vertically. Their broad geometry is easy to recognize, but a competent composition also has to consider the space each leaves for other charges. Three stars on a chief need enough height to remain readable. A lion below it needs room for head, limbs, and tail. A pale bearing several objects needs enough width for their diagnostic features. These requirements explain why a fixed percentage is an inadequate drawing method.

Begin with the relationships and work toward proportions. If the chief bears charges, establish their count and arrangement, then choose a workable depth. If the field below contains another group, reserve enough space for that group. Revisit both decisions together. The ordinary and accompanying charges are parts of one composition, not independent boxes whose sizes can be selected once and forgotten. Proportion is constrained by what has to fit and what must remain recognizable.

This also distinguishes a chief from a per-fess partition. With a chief, the lower field remains the principal background carrying the other figures. With a per-fess field, upper and lower areas are parts of the divided field. A badly proportioned chief may look like a partition; the wording and layer structure distinguish them. An image alone can be ambiguous, especially when no other charge reveals the construction. Record that uncertainty instead of treating visual width as an infallible grammatical test.

The same word can describe placement without introducing the ordinary. A star in chief is in the upper region; it is not necessarily on a chief. Three figures in pale form a vertical group; they do not necessarily lie on a pale. The noun names a figure, while the positional expression names a relationship. Once that difference is understood, the vocabulary becomes much less mysterious. The shield is not secretly full of invisible bands every time a blazon tells us where something goes.

## Bends and chevrons need a usable axis

A bend follows the diagonal from dexter chief to sinister base; a bend sinister follows the other diagonal. Its centerline provides a useful construction axis, but its actual boundaries have width. Charges placed on it need to remain inside those boundaries. A bend does not become correct merely because a diagonal stripe crosses the shield somewhere. Its direction, extent, tincture, modifications, and relationship to the other figures must satisfy the description.

For a constructed bend bearing three stars, place the group along the diagonal before deciding the final width. The central star may be the largest the available band can comfortably carry; the outer stars need room near the shield's edges. Adjust the group as a composition. Placing three equal stars at equal distances along an infinitely long mathematical line can leave the outer ones clipped by the actual shield. Their intended arrangement should remain clear after the line is fitted to its support.

A chevron uses two sloping arms meeting at an apex. Its angle, height, and thickness respond to the rest of the design. If two charges occupy chief and one base, the chevron has to separate those spaces coherently. If charges lie on the chevron, their placement has to follow its arms rather than float around its apex. A lower apex can create more room above while compressing the space below. There is no single universally correct height independent of the composition.

This is why an apparent centering problem may have several causes. The ordinary may be displaced, its apex may be too high, the figures may be centered in the wrong regions, or the shield's taper may make mathematically centered figures look cramped. Diagnose the relationships one at a time. Moving the whole group until one star looks better can make the others worse. A coherent redraw starts with the support and ordinary, then places each figure within the region or band the words assign to it.

An inverted chevron changes orientation. A chevron with a specified interruption or distinctive curvature changes another meaningful feature. Those alterations normally need descriptive support. A competent artist can vary the exact angle of an unmodified chevron; the artist should not decide that inversion is merely a more fashionable angle. Flexibility occupies the details left open by the specification, not the features the vocabulary explicitly distinguishes.

## Cross and saltire: extent is part of the figure

A full cross combines a vertical and horizontal band reaching the shield's boundaries. A saltire combines diagonal bands. Their intersections produce a central area capable of bearing another charge, and their arms divide the remaining field into spaces. A small cross floating within the field is a different construction. If the ends do not reach the edges, the description may identify it as couped or as a particular cross form. The extent belongs to the reading problem.

Crosses introduce an extensive vocabulary of end treatments and modifications. A cross pattée has widening arms; a cross potent has transverse ends; a cross moline has characteristic divided, curved terminations. A cross crosslet repeats small crosses at its arms. A fitché modification gives a pointed lower termination. These elementary descriptions help identify a family of forms, but an actual source's term and illustration should control the detailed reconstruction.[^cross-vocabulary] A cropped cross with its ends missing cannot reliably be assigned a form whose identity depends on those ends.

Do not confuse the number of arms with the number of crosses. A cross crosslet is one specified charge, not automatically five independent crosses. Nor does every cross-like arrangement of four objects become a cross ordinary. Four keys can be arranged crosswise while remaining four keys. The operation that relates separate objects differs from the figure formed by continuous arms. The difference matters for both blazon and counting a corpus.

A saltire has its own placement problem. In a pointed shield, the lower diagonal arms encounter a different contour from the upper arms. The visual center of their crossing may need adjustment to preserve a balanced continuous figure. The diagonal arms should still read as one saltire. A renderer that builds two unrelated bars with mismatched widths may introduce a kink at the crossing. That is a construction defect in the realization, not a new heraldic form to invent a name for.

## Voiding removes an interior; edging adds a boundary

A **voided** figure has an interior opening that lets the underlying area show through in the manner specified. A **fimbriated** figure carries a narrow edge of another tincture. These operations can both produce outlines, but they do different things. Voiding changes the figure's occupied area. Fimbriation adds a border around it. A thin rendering stroke may imitate either at small size without constituting either formally.

Try a constructed red cross on white. Give it a white interior opening: the remaining red shape surrounds space through which the field appears. Now draw a solid red cross with a narrow gold edge. Its interior remains red, and another tincture separates its outer boundary from the field. Finally, draw a solid red cross with a thin black stroke solely to make the SVG clear. The pictures may share outline-like features, but only the first two involve specified heraldic operations. The stroke belongs to the rendering unless the description establishes otherwise.

The District of Mission's 1992 grant records a specially described mission cross carrying an Oblate cross, with the latter's openings specified in its description.[^mission-cross] This is a useful example of a named form supported by explanatory wording. The source does not merely say cross and expect an artist to choose any familiar silhouette. Parenthetical definitions carry information that should survive transcription and translation.

Roger Miller's 2016 grant supplies another defined case: a counterchanged, voided cross moline on a quarterly wavy field.[^miller-ordinary] Here the artist must manage partition, line treatment, cross form, interior opening, and tincture exchange together. Reading the words as five independent decorative suggestions would not reproduce their relationships. The difficult part is the operation and its scope, which can be understood before any polished drawing is attempted.

## Borders, orles, and tressures are not picture frames

A bordure follows the edge of the armorial field. An orle is inset, leaving field visible between it and the shield's edge. A tressure is a related narrow enclosing figure whose particular form, including repeated or floral treatment where specified, must be read from the source.[^enclosing-vocabulary] These are components of the arms when recorded. A museum mount, photograph border, and browser card outline are external to the armorial design even when they imitate its contour.

The distinction changes how other charges fit. A bordure reduces the central area available to them. An orle leaves an outer strip of field and an enclosed inner region. Charges arranged in orle occupy an enclosing sequence; they do not necessarily sit on a drawn orle. Again, the figure and its arrangement share vocabulary without becoming the same operation. Eight birds in orle are eight birds placed around the field, not eight birds automatically perched on a continuous narrow band.

An enclosing figure can itself be charged, patterned, divided, or given a modified edge. Describe its interior and exterior boundaries carefully. If the wording modifies only one edge, do not apply the treatment to both. A low-resolution reproduction may conceal that distinction. Better images, a complete blazon, and comparative witnesses become particularly valuable where two adjacent narrow lines can merge visually.

Borders also acquire institutional roles in some systems, including particular methods of difference. Their geometry does not assign those roles automatically. A red bordure can be an original feature, an inherited difference, or another documented modification. The cadency chapter supplies the genealogical and institutional questions. Here the task is to describe the border accurately enough for that later analysis. A geometric noun should not carry an unsupported family story inside it.

## Corners, wedges, and side figures

A canton occupies a corner, conventionally dexter chief in elementary English usage unless otherwise specified. A quarter is a larger corner-related figure in the vocabulary. A pile is a wedge, ordinarily issuing from chief unless the source specifies another direction. A gyron is a triangular segment associated with a corner and the central region. Flanches project inward from the sides with curved boundaries.[^other-geometries] Each form has a characteristic relation to the shield's outline, so it cannot be identified from an isolated triangle or curve alone.

Consider a constructed wedge that begins at the upper edge and narrows toward base. Its contour supplies the elementary idea of a pile. A triangular field compartment created by division is another construction. The visual likeness can be close, but the grammar asks whether the triangle is a charge on a background or one region of the field. If its point or base is cut off, read whether the source specifies truncation or whether the image is damaged.

The Canadian record for Christ Church Cathedral, Ottawa, uses a highly qualified reversed pile together with several superimposed crosses and a chief.[^cathedral-layers] It is a useful counterexample to simplistic rules about what a pile is allowed to look like in every tradition. The source's qualifiers provide the instructions. We should neither discard them to fit a beginner template nor pretend that this one grant establishes an unrestricted worldwide default.

Flanches show another limitation of pixel matching. Their arcs can vary with the shield while preserving their relation to its sides. A circular crop may make an ordinary bordure look like a side projection; a damaged edge may remove the point at which a flanch meets the outline. The complete support helps identify the figure. Record the portion visible and the source term rather than treating the crop's silhouette as sufficient proof.

## Small geometric figures have diagnostic interiors

A lozenge, fusil, mascle, and rustre belong to a related visual vocabulary. A lozenge is a diamond-shaped figure; a fusil is typically more elongated; a mascle is a lozenge-shaped opening surrounded by a lozenge-shaped figure; a rustre has a circular opening. A billet is a small rectangular figure. A roundel is circular.[^small-geometries] These distinctions are particularly useful because a small change to an interior can carry the identity of the charge.

The terms should not be turned into modern engineering tolerances. A historical artist's lozenge need not have a universal width-to-height ratio. A worn mascle may lose the evidence of its opening. A solid dark photograph can conceal whether the interior is painted, perforated, or filled with shadow. Examine the medium and source before declaring that a geometric charge has changed category. Shape, depiction, and material structure can produce superficially similar effects.

English roundel terminology often uses names associated with tincture, such as bezant for a gold roundel and plate for a silver one. The name can therefore encode information that another language or source gives explicitly.[^roundel-vocabulary] Preserve the original expression when translating. A literal translation of the word's ordinary meaning may lose the fact that it names a geometric charge. Conversely, describing every circular object as a roundel can erase a coin, wheel, or other specifically depicted object.

A useful reading test is to remove the color while preserving the openings, then remove the openings while preserving the color. Which distinction disappears in each operation? That reveals the information carried by shape and tincture separately. A faithful simplified reproduction needs to retain both when both are specified. The fact that a tiny favicon cannot display a mascle's opening well is a production constraint, not an excuse to quietly call it a lozenge.

## Diminutives depend on description as well as width

A bendlet, pallet, bar, or chevronel is related to a broader form, but a thin image does not independently supply the term. A narrow fess can be an artist's solution to a crowded composition. Multiple bars can have a defined count and spacing. A period description can use a vocabulary that a modern guide organizes differently. Read the actual expression before normalizing it into the diminutive that seems closest to the photograph.

Repeated ordinaries also have relationships to one another. Two bends may be parallel; a larger bend may be accompanied by narrow bands on its sides; several chevrons may be stacked. The whole group needs enough room to remain recognizable, and the source's modifiers need defined targets. Do not let a renderer create a third band merely because a thick outline resembles one. Count figures from the specification and separately count what the image actually depicts.

This is where a source's distinction between **bendy** and multiple bends becomes useful. One describes a repeated field treatment; the other describes figures on a field. The same applies to paly versus pallets and barry versus bars. If the number of pieces or figures is specified, preserve it. If the source leaves the patterned realization open, do not promote the incidental visible count to a fixed armorial requirement.

For an editorial concordance, keep a source-language term, a working English equivalent, an image or structural explanation, and a confidence note. The equivalent should be reversible enough that another reader can locate the original expression. A one-column list of normalized English words makes searching easy but can destroy the distinctions the historical writer used. A reference resource needs both discovery and recovery.

An ordinary can carry a second level of charges. A bend with three escallops is one supporting figure carrying three others. A chief with a book between two stars has an internal arrangement. A fess patterned with checks has its own treatment. Read the ordinary as a local region whose contents need the same attention to count, tincture, direction, and spacing as the principal field.

The Archibald Hinshelwood registration of 15 November 2017 supplies a manageable actual example: a red engrailed bend on gold, carrying three silver escallops, with additional figures in the field.[^hinshelwood] The ordinary's edge treatment and its charges belong to different levels. The tree and stag are accompanying field figures. A drawing putting the escallops beside the bend would retain the objects while losing their supporting relationship.

The record's notes also identify an earlier Scottish recording dated 6 August 1765. The later Canadian registration is therefore not evidence that the design was first invented in 2017. That date distinction will matter in the jurisdictions chapter. Here it reinforces a practical rule for documentary illustrations: use the act date for the act, the older record date for the reported older record, and avoid manufacturing a creation date from whichever date appears largest on the web page.

When the ordinary is narrow, charge orientation may follow its axis according to the description or convention. That does not mean every object should be rotated blindly. A key, sword, star, or animal can have its own specified orientation. Draw an annotated plan before polishing the figures. Identify where each lies and which words control its direction. The ordinary supplies space; the grammar supplies the relationship.

## Superimposed ordinaries require an order of construction

Two ordinaries can cross or one can lie over another. Their intersection may show one uninterrupted figure with the other apparently passing beneath, or a particular combined treatment may be specified. An image with a single dark intersection can conceal which layer is uppermost. Read words such as overall and the surrounding clauses before reconstructing the order.

Use a constructed white field with a blue pale and a red fess overall. Draw the pale first, then the fess crossing it. Reverse the order in a second version. Both pictures contain one pale and one fess, with the same tinctures, but the visible central square changes. The layering is part of the specification. If a contrast analysis considers only the field colors and not the local intersection, it may miss the immediate relationship between the two figures.

The Christ Church Cathedral record demonstrates the problem at greater complexity. Its sequence involves a pile, multiple crosses, a central inescutcheon, and a chief.[^cathedral-layers] To read it, make a supporting-region table and assign each new clause to a figure or figure part. Avoid quoting the entire long blazon merely to signal that it is difficult. The useful work is showing why its prepositions and qualifiers control a reconstructible assembly.

This exercise also exposes the limits of a simple automated renderer. A tool supporting one ordinary cannot represent every valid composition containing several. Report that limit. Flattening the source into whichever ordinary the tool happens to draw makes a software limitation look like a historical simplification. The original wording should remain available for a more capable reconstruction.

## Proportion can be evaluated without pretending it is legislation

A teaching drawing can be judged by several concrete questions. Does the ordinary remain recognizable? Is its orientation correct? Does it reach the required edges or remain appropriately detached? Can its charges be identified at the intended scale? Are the surrounding charges in the correct regions? Does the image preserve the specified gaps and overlaps? These criteria test fidelity and usability without inventing a universal percentage rule.

Compare two constructed chiefs with the same three stars. One is so shallow that the stars touch its boundaries; the other leaves a comfortable margin while allowing the lower field's lion enough room. The second is a more effective realization because both sets of charges remain legible. That is a reasoned drawing judgment. It need not become a claim that every chief must have exactly the same depth as this one.

Likewise, a bend's width should be evaluated perpendicular to its axis rather than by the width of the horizontal slice at one height. A diagonal band can occupy a large horizontal span while remaining relatively narrow in its own direction. Confusing those measurements produces inconsistent comparisons among bends, pales, and fesses. State the measurement method if a research project actually quantifies proportions. Otherwise, describe the practical relationship rather than dress a visual impression as a precise statistic.

At small sizes, simplify artistic detail before sacrificing explicit structure. A less elaborate engrailed edge may remain recognizable while a missing star changes count. A simplified escallop can retain its shell form while an omitted shell changes the design. Which adjustments are appropriate depends on the task and source. A reproduction intended as a documentary image has different obligations from a clearly labeled teaching schematic.

## Research project: an ordinary across a corpus

Choose one ordinary and examine a defined group of dated witnesses. Record direction, width using a stated method if measured, line treatment, supporting charges, accompanying charges, and medium. Keep the source's name for the figure beside your normalized category. The project should ask a specific question, such as whether a charged bend is treated differently from an uncharged one in one manuscript, rather than whether medieval heraldry was symmetrical.

Compare cases that challenge your expectation. A broad uncharged ordinary, a narrow charged one, or an unusual edge treatment can reveal what your first model omitted. Explain the available space and source description before assigning the difference to chronology or national style. If the sample comes from one compiler, it may reflect that compiler's drawing practice. If the images come from restorations, the restoration history may matter as much as the supposed medieval date.

Your final argument should connect a visible pattern to a plausible mechanism and identify the evidence needed to test that mechanism. It can show that proportions respond to charge content in a defined corpus without claiming that every heraldic artist everywhere used the same rule. That is a substantial result because it explains how the drawings work.

## Workshop and answer discussion

Using only a field and one ordinary, make six original teaching shields. Name each ordinary and explain its orientation. Then choose one and add three charges **on** it. Redraw the same charge types **around** it, supplying a clear verbal arrangement. Compare the resulting blazons and images.
