## A small working vocabulary before the first objects

A **witness** is a particular surviving or documented manifestation of the evidence, such as a seal impression, manuscript page, catalogue photograph or later copy. A **witness inventory** identifies each such item by institution, date, locator and relationship to the claim being investigated. Reading a witness **diplomatically** means reproducing its actual letters and abbreviations rather than silently modernizing them; a **normalized** reading supplies editorial changes explicitly. These ideas belong near the beginning of the course, since the Dering Roll and its copies are not interchangeable images of one abstract shield.

The common field terms also need names. A **fess** is a horizontal band, a **pale** a vertical band, a **bend** a diagonal band, and a **chevron** two sloped arms meeting like an inverted V. **Per pale** means the field is divided vertically, unlike placing a pale on an undivided field. A **bordure** is an ordinary around the shield's edge; a **mullet** is a star-shaped charge, and a **roundel** a circular one. **Dexter** and **sinister** refer to the bearer's right and left, which are reversed from the spectator's viewpoint. The Academy teaches their full formal treatment later. Here they let us describe what a document actually contains without using loose substitutes.

## A picture that can survive being redrawn

Imagine that three artists have been asked to draw the same coat of arms. One paints it on parchment. Another carves it above a doorway. A third needs an image small enough to fit on a phone. The lions will not have identical faces. The shield will not have identical proportions. One version may have elaborate shading while another has none. Yet we should still be able to recognize the same arms. This is the first thing to understand about heraldry: identity need not depend on copying an image pixel for pixel.

That does not mean every change is harmless. Replace a lion with an eagle and the composition changes. Replace a gold charge with a silver one and the tincture changes. Reverse a directional figure when its orientation is significant and the result may no longer represent the description. The interesting question is which features carry identity and which belong to an artist's treatment. Learning heraldry means learning to make that distinction before interpreting what the design means.

We will use **arms** for the identifying armorial design and **emblazonment** for a particular depiction. A **blazon** is its formal verbal description. These are working distinctions, not a promise that every historical writer used the words consistently. Fox-Davies's 1909 manual separates describing arms from depicting them, while the National Records of Scotland explains the importance of the verbal description in its register.[^language] Think of the blazon as a specification that admits multiple competent realizations. The painting is one realization, not necessarily the only permissible one.

This is why heraldry is worth studying as a visual language. Its vocabulary includes figures, colors, positions, and relationships. Its grammar helps say what goes where. Its institutions and customs determine who uses a design and what that use claims. None of those layers can be replaced by looking at a pretty shield and deciding that it feels medieval.

## Identification is a problem; origins are a research question

The familiar explanation is that a closed helmet makes recognition difficult and an identifying shield helps. It is a useful way to introduce the practical problem. It is not a complete account of the emergence of hereditary armory. A device used once, a badge used repeatedly, and arms transmitted through generations are different phenomena. A study of origins must ask when the relevant practices became connected, in which places, and in which surviving evidence.

We should also distinguish an old motif from an old armorial system. An ancient eagle does not make every later eagle a continuation of ancient heraldry. Similar pictures can do different social work. Fox-Davies himself criticized arguments that inferred the antiquity of heraldry simply from the antiquity of the animals it depicted.[^origins] His book is evidence of an early twentieth-century argument, not an impartial final verdict on every culture he discusses. Some of its language about non-European peoples is unacceptable and its assumptions require examination.

Instead of trying to identify the first coat of arms in history on our first afternoon, start with a dated witness. Ask what the witness demonstrates. Is a shield depicted? Is a bearer named? Is the design repeated in another context? Is its inheritance documented? Those questions turn an attractive origin story into a research program. They also let us say something useful when the evidence is incomplete.

## Documentary encounter: the Dering Roll

The British Library identifies the Dering Roll as **Add Roll 77720**, dating it to approximately **1270–1280**. Its catalogue describes four parchment membranes with 324 shields arranged in 54 rows of six. It notes names missing or erased at several numbered shields and identifies a concentration of knights from Kent and Sussex.[^dering] Those are observations about a particular artifact, not a census of everyone who bore arms in medieval Europe.

The distinction matters. If you count birds in this roll, your result describes your selected corpus. It does not automatically measure the popularity of birds throughout medieval heraldry. If a name is erased, the remaining shield does not tell you why. If you find the same surname in a modern family tree, the roll does not itself connect that modern person to the named medieval bearer.

This is an excellent first source because its arrangement makes comparison possible. Begin by comparing repeated visual structures rather than guessing personal biographies. Which shields share a field treatment? Which use a repeated charge? Which differ by a small added feature? Record your observations separately from your explanation. You can compare before you can identify. In fact, that is often the safer order.

The catalogue is also not the artifact. Its descriptions and dating are curatorial statements. A digital photograph is another layer. A later printed transcription adds another. When quoting a catalogue, attribute the catalogue; when reporting what you see in a photograph, identify the photographed witness and the relevant location. That small discipline prevents a great many large mistakes.

## Surnames are search terms, not permissions

Suppose you find a shield associated with someone named Martin. You are also named Martin. What have you established? A useful search lead. What have you not established? That the two people are related, that a particular inheritance rule applies, or that you may bear those arms.

The Canadian Heraldic Authority explicitly warns that a coat of arms does not belong to everyone sharing a surname.[^surname] This does not mean families are irrelevant. Family relationships can be central to transmission. It means the relationship must be demonstrated and interpreted within the relevant tradition. The same-name shortcut discards the very evidence that would make an inheritance argument work.

For this course, keep four propositions separate:

| Proposition | What would support it? |
|---|---|
| A person used a particular design | A dated depiction, seal, document, or other attributable witness |
| A document records that design | The document itself, with its location and wording |
| Two people are related | A documented genealogical connection |
| A person is entitled to bear the arms | The applicable rule and evidence satisfying it |

One source can support several propositions, but the propositions do not become interchangeable. A decorative plate proves that someone decorated a plate. Whether its decoration also demonstrates authorized armorial use is another question.

## Meaning needs an author and a context

A lion might have been chosen because its bearer admired strength. It might have been inherited without a newly chosen explanation. It might make a pun. It might repeat the language of a wider political community. The picture alone does not let us choose between those possibilities.

A useful distinction is between **documented intention**, **historical association**, and **your interpretation**. A grant commentary may explain why a particular bearer selected an owl. A historical study may show that an eagle had imperial associations in a defined setting. You may find a creature visually aggressive. All three statements can be interesting, but each requires a different kind of support. Do not promote the third into the first simply because it sounds plausible.

When you write an interpretation, use verbs that show the evidence. “The grant commentary explains” identifies an attributed intention. “This motif also appears in” reports comparison. “I suggest” announces an argument. A sentence that merely says “the bird means freedom” hides who assigned the meaning, where, and when. It sounds certain because it has removed the information needed to test it.

## Worked problem: same design, different evidence

**Constructed teaching example:** a blue shield bears three gold stars. A manuscript gives a similar image beside a name. A modern website reproduces it with five-pointed stars. A family owns a silver seal showing three stars without color. None of these fictional witnesses refers to a real grantee.

First, describe each witness independently. The manuscript supplies painted tinctures if the colors are legible. The seal supplies shapes and an arrangement, but its uncolored impression does not establish blue or gold. The website may supply a transcription, but until its source is identified it is a dependent claim. The family possession establishes present custody, not an uninterrupted chain of inheritance.

Second, state your provisional reconciliation. Perhaps all three depict one design. Perhaps two similar designs have been confused. The star's number of points may matter, depending on the source description and convention. A responsible answer identifies what would resolve the uncertainty: a blazon, a better image, provenance, or a document connecting the bearer to the object. “More research needed” becomes useful when you say which research.

## What has to remain the same?

Start with a shield bearing a blue field and one gold bend. Make the shield taller. Give it a rounded base. Paint the gold with metallic leaf in one version and flat yellow in another. Provided the applicable description and conventions permit those treatments, the design remains recognizable because the important relationships survive: one diagonal band, the same direction, the same tinctures, and the same relation between band and field. The outline, texture, and exact width belong to the realization. Change the band to the opposite diagonal and you have changed a feature the vocabulary distinguishes. Add a second band and you have changed the number. The eye may still group all these pictures together, but resemblance is a broader category than heraldic identity.

This gives us an operable model. Think of the arms as a set of constraints, and of an emblazonment as an image satisfying them. Some constraints are explicit: three charges, a red field, a specified attitude. Others depend on the language and convention: the ordinary meaning of an unmodified term, an expected arrangement, or the way a particular figure is represented. Artistic choices occupy what those constraints leave open. The task is therefore to discover the constraints before deciding how much variation is acceptable. A computer cannot solve that problem by comparing pixels, and an artist cannot solve it by announcing that the result has the same general vibe.

The model also explains why a blazon does not make an original image irrelevant. A grant's painting may settle what an unusual newly introduced charge looks like. A historical depiction may reveal a convention that later terminology obscures. A particular institution may prescribe a standard version for its correspondence even though other heraldic realizations remain possible. The verbal and visual witnesses can constrain each other. We need to identify the authority and purpose of each, rather than assume that words always defeat pictures or pictures always defeat words.

Sweden's Riksarkivet offers a useful contemporary comparison. Its guidance distinguishes the stable blazon from artistic realizations and places versions of the greater state arms by Brita Grep and Henrik Dahlström beside one another. The dates attached to those drawings are 1917 and 2014.[^swedish-language] The institutional example makes variation visible across time. It does not license us to change an explicit charge, but it does show why the existence of a newer official-looking drawing cannot, by itself, prove the adoption of new arms.

For a historian, there is a further distinction between continuity of design and continuity of bearer. The same institution may change its name while retaining its arms. A successor organization may use an older emblem under a particular decision. An unrelated person may copy it. All three can produce similar images, but their institutional histories differ. Identity of composition is one question; identity of bearer and authority to use the composition are two more. Keep them connected without collapsing them.

## Recognition depends on what an audience knows

A shield can be distinctive without being immediately identifiable to everyone who sees it. Recognizing a pattern and knowing its bearer require different kinds of knowledge. You may recognize three gold crowns on blue as familiar while being unable to name the institution whose particular use you are examining. A medieval observer might know the local families represented in a roll but have little reason to recognize a distant civic coat. Heraldry supplies a structured signal; it does not install a universal lookup table in the viewer's head.

That observation changes how we explain an identifying system. A signal works through an audience, a context, and a set of alternatives. If two designs are nearly identical, the relevant difference may be obvious on a large painted banner and invisible on a worn seal. If an audience already knows the bearer, a simplified badge may be sufficient. If it does not, an accompanying inscription may do more identifying work than the shield. Successful communication involves the whole display, including placement, repetition, names, and circumstances.

Consider a constructed town procession. An official carries a shield-shaped badge showing the town's arms; several attendants carry flags; a document names the office and occasion. A spectator can connect the badge with the town because the procession supplies context. A museum photograph centuries later removes that context. The researcher has to recover it from the record. We should not confuse our difficulty identifying an isolated object with proof that the object failed to identify its wearer in its original setting.

The Met's *Messenger Badge and Shield*, accession 1984.26a, b, makes this relationship concrete. Its record dates the badge to 1400–1450 and the associated shield to about 1800, with the culture qualified as possibly Italian. The museum explains the use of armorial badges by messengers working for lords or civic authorities.[^messenger] A person wearing an employer's arms is communicating a relationship to an employer. The image need not mean that the messenger personally owns or transmits those arms. That difference is fundamental to understanding livery and institutional representation.

The two dates also prevent a seductive mistake. A photograph containing two objects is not evidence that they were manufactured together. Their present association belongs to a later object history unless other evidence establishes an earlier one. The badge, the larger shield, the design, the person who wore the badge, and the collection assemblage each have a history. A single caption can name the group while its component dates tell us to keep those histories separate.

## An origin is a bundle of changes

There is no difficulty finding ancient pictures of animals. The historical problem is discovering when particular pictures began to function as armorial identity within a durable social practice. That requires several observations: use by an attributable bearer, repetition, differentiation from other bearers, transmission or institutional continuity, and a recognizable method of display. These need not appear in the same document or on the same day. Asking for the first coat of arms assumes a sharp boundary that the evidence may not supply.

We can make the problem more precise by distinguishing three proposed origins. The origin of a motif concerns when a lion, eagle, cross, or geometric figure appeared in art. The origin of a particular coat concerns when that composition became associated with a bearer. The origin of an armorial system concerns how repeated identifying designs, inheritance, language, institutions, and display conventions became connected. Evidence for the first may contribute almost nothing to the third. Finding an eagle on an ancient object cannot supply the missing social mechanism merely because later arms also contain eagles.

Historical origin arguments often change their definition halfway through. They begin with a narrow definition involving hereditary shield designs, then establish antiquity using any repeated personal symbol. The evidence has not necessarily become worse; the proposition has changed. A careful essay states its definition, tests borderline cases, and explains which features its witnesses actually establish. If an early seal shows an identifying design but inheritance remains unknown, that is a meaningful partial result. It should be described as such rather than inflated into the birth certificate of heraldry.

Nor should a practical explanation become an exclusive cause. Recognition in battle is a plausible function of distinctive display. Seals, ceremonies, patronage, kinship, and competition for status offer other mechanisms through which armory could spread and stabilize. To assign relative importance, we need dated evidence from those settings. A modern classroom demonstration of helmeted anonymity illustrates a problem; it is not direct testimony about every medieval person's reason for choosing arms.

The same method improves accounts of expansion beyond aristocratic warfare. Ask when a civic authority, religious institution, or other body used a recognizable design, what the design did, and how continuity was recorded. Do not assume that the adoption of a shield-shaped emblem made every institution behave like a hereditary knight. Institutions can transmit identity through office and corporate succession. Their mechanisms of continuity need their own explanation.

## Two Dering records are not interchangeable

An especially instructive trap appears when searching the Dering title. The British Library's record for Add Roll 77720 concerns the medieval roll introduced above. Another catalogue record, Add MS 38537, also uses the name Dering Roll but describes a fifteenth-century witness and discusses a related tricked copy in Harley MS 6137. The latter record also describes altered arms and a substituted Dering name at numbered entry 61.[^dering-copy] These are separate shelfmarks and separate descriptions. Searching by a famous title without carrying the identifier into the notes can quietly join their properties into an object that never existed.

We can avoid that error with a witness table. Give every physical document its own row. Record the shelfmark, material, catalogue date, described contents, later interventions, and connections to other witnesses. Put a printed edition in another row, even if it reproduces one of the manuscripts. Record its publication date separately from the manuscript's. A modern digital image belongs in the access column for the witness it depicts; it is not another medieval copy simply because it has its own URL.

Then ask a specific question of entry 61. What design and name are reported as earlier? What substituted design and name are reported as later? Which witness and which catalogue statement support the answer? The important consequence is that a name beside arms can itself be an intervention. Association in the surviving document may accurately report the present state of the surface while failing to establish an original association. A researcher who overlooks alteration can produce a tidy genealogy from a very untidy source.

Attributing the account also matters. A catalogue's report of erasure and substitution is evidence about the curator's analysis. To make an independent material claim, we would need to inspect the relevant surface, conservation documentation, and comparative witnesses. The same distinction applies to the attribution of responsibility. Evidence of alteration does not automatically identify the hand that performed it. Preserve whatever uncertainty the particular source gives rather than making the story more dramatic by selecting a culprit.

This is not a reason to distrust everything. It is a reason to make confidence local. The shelfmark is secure. The catalogue reports an intervention. The original association and the responsibility for alteration may require additional work. Separating those claims lets the source remain useful while preventing one uncertain step from contaminating the entire argument.

## The Zürich roll: a comparative witness with its own problems

The *Zürcher Wappenrolle*, Swiss National Museum AG 2760, is catalogued through e-codices as a parchment roll from the Lake Constance region, dated about 1330–1345. Its summary describes shields accompanied by crests and a geographically defined selection of bearers.[^zurich] Here is a useful comparison with an English roll: the external component is part of the repeated visual unit. A project that crops every shield and discards the crest would remove evidence that the compiler repeatedly chose to include.

That observation should lead to a question rather than a universal declaration about German-speaking heraldry. What does the crest contribute to differentiation in this particular corpus? Does it repeat shield motifs, introduce other figures, or vary among similar shields? Answer by locating particular entries and recording them. A regional label tells us where to begin; it does not do the comparison for us.

The catalogue supplies an additional exercise in translation criticism. The German summary refers to a missing fifth part; the English summary refers to a missing fourth part. Both connect knowledge of missing arms to a late eighteenth-century copy. The discrepancy is visible in the two summaries.[^zurich] It should remain visible in a research note until the physical description and copying history resolve it. Fluent English does not make the English ordinal authoritative, and proximity to the source does not make the German wording immune to error.

Notice the layers of time involved. A medieval design can survive in a later copy of a now-missing portion. That copy is direct evidence of what the later copyist depicted, while its value as evidence of the medieval original depends on the copying relationship. The date of the design, the date of the lost original portion, the date of the surviving copy, and the date of a modern catalogue are different fields. Losing those distinctions makes a late copy look like a medieval surface or makes a medieval composition look like an eighteenth-century invention.

For this chapter, the catalogue is a starting witness, not a completed edition of the roll. The exercise asks you to move from the institutional locator to a defined image and entry, then compare the crest and shield. Record what you actually inspect. If you use the late copy, cite it as that copy. A source trail is most valuable when it makes the transition between witnesses easy to follow.

## Social identification is not a biological claim

A coat can identify an individual, a family line, an office, or a collective body according to the setting. None of those functions makes the design a genetic marker. Similar arms can arise through relationship, borrowing, official augmentation, political affiliation, deliberate imitation, or coincidence. The picture may help identify a hypothesis, but it cannot choose the mechanism on its own.

This becomes clear when we compare three constructed uses of one tree. Person A bears a tree inherited under a documented grant. Municipality B places a tree in its arms to represent a local landscape. Organization C uses A's tree in a badge under a recorded association. Their designs share a figure. Their histories share different kinds of connection, and perhaps none at all between A and B. A search engine grouping all tree shields performs a useful visual retrieval task. It has not established one extended family.

A useful inference therefore has two parts. Describe the similarity precisely, then identify the relationship that would explain it. A shared rare combination might make a genealogical connection worth investigating. A common charge on a common field supplies much weaker discrimination. Even a rare combination is a lead, because copying and mistaken attribution remain possible. The strength of a resemblance depends on the comparison set and the mechanisms available, not on how excited the researcher becomes when two images look alike.

We should also distinguish historical entitlement from present reception. Someone can identify with a design through a family story even when the documentary chain is absent. That story can be studied as memory or identity without being certified as inheritance. The most respectful response is to identify what the account establishes and what another claim would require. Scholarship does not improve by treating affection as proof, but it need not treat affection as meaningless either.

## Symbols do not come with one dictionary of meanings

Suppose a contemporary commentary says that a book represents education. That is an attributed explanation for a particular emblem. It does not establish that every book charge in every period had the same intended meaning. A closed book might be inherited; a specific book might refer to a text; an open book could carry an inscription essential to identifying it. The generic shape provides a category, while the record supplies the local proposition.

The same problem affects canting arms, where a design makes a visual or linguistic allusion to a name. A possible pun requires the relevant historical form of the name and the language in which the connection works. An English translation may destroy the sound relationship. A modern spelling may create a resemblance absent from the older name. A good argument therefore preserves the original name and charge term before explaining the proposed allusion. It also distinguishes a documented explanation from a retrospective clever guess.

For a constructed example, imagine a bearer called Bell whose shield has a bell. The coincidence makes canting plausible. It does not date the choice or identify its maker. If a grant commentary explicitly records the name allusion, the interpretation becomes much stronger. If the arms predate the surname's documented form, the chronology may complicate it. A dictionary match begins an inquiry; it should not end it.

Meaning also changes through reuse. An inherited figure may acquire an explanation that the original chooser never intended. That later explanation is still historical evidence if we can date and attribute it. We can say that a twentieth-century institution interpreted a medieval motif in a new way without claiming the twentieth-century interpretation caused the medieval choice. The causal direction matters. Later readers can give a symbol another life, but they cannot travel backward and select it for its first bearer.

## Looking at a design as a system

Three levels make a useful working distinction. At the visual level, we describe what is depicted and how its parts relate. At the documentary level, we identify what words, images, acts, or records establish the design and bearer. At the institutional level, we ask what the use claims and who recognizes or regulates it. A problem may be solved at one level and unresolved at another. We can accurately describe a shield whose bearer remains unknown, or establish a grant whose surviving photograph is too small to reveal every detail.

This helps explain why a commercially supplied picture can be attractive and historically unhelpful at the same time. Its linework may be competent; its title may refer to a real surname; its specific connection to the purchaser may be unsupported. Those judgments are independent. A useful critique identifies the missing link rather than dismissing the art or repeating a slogan about family crests. Ask for the bearer, the record, the date, and the relationship claimed. The answer may reveal a genuine historical design without establishing a modern entitlement.

The system also includes the means of reproduction. A stamp reverses its matrix in the impression. A screen displays emitted light. Cloth folds. Stone weathers. A banner presents a moving rectangle rather than a rigid shield. These are mechanisms that affect what the observer can recover. They are not mere defects in otherwise perfect illustrations. Later chapters will show how to preserve armorial relationships while taking the physical and digital support seriously.

The result is a discipline with several kinds of expertise. Drawing requires visual judgment. Reading a blazon requires technical language. Identifying a bearer requires documentary work. Evaluating transmission requires institutional history and genealogy. A capable student learns where one competence stops and another begins, then uses them together. Knowing the Latin word for left does not provide a pedigree, although it may stop you from drawing the pedigree's arms backward.

## A first comparative research project

Choose two dated armorial corpora and define a narrow comparison: the presence of crests, the use of names beside shields, or the treatment of repeated geometric designs. Before counting, state what constitutes an entry and what counts as observable. A missing or cropped crest is not automatically an intentionally omitted crest. An erased name is not equivalent to a blank space that never carried one. Those distinctions determine the denominator of your result.

Produce a small catalogue with witness identifiers and image locations, then write an argument explaining one pattern and one counterexample. Keep observations separate from institutional interpretation. If the corpora differ in date, geography, purpose, or preservation, explain how those differences constrain the comparison. The aim is not to announce that one country preferred birds after inspecting twenty convenient images. It is to show that you can turn a visual question into a defined, reproducible inquiry.

End by identifying one missing source that would materially change the argument. That might be a complete image of a damaged segment, the copying history of a later witness, or a record identifying an otherwise anonymous bearer. Your conclusion should already be useful before that source is found. A sound first project establishes a limited pattern, explains its basis, and shows exactly where the next project begins.

## Workshop and answer discussion

Choose one documented armorial object from an institutional catalogue. Write three paragraphs: what is visible, what the catalogue says, and what remains unknown. Include the institution, title, identifier, date or date range, URL, and date consulted. Do not infer a bearer merely from the presence of a surname somewhere on the page.

<details>
<summary>What would a strong submission do?</summary>

A strong submission distinguishes observation from attribution. It preserves a date range rather than converting it to a precise year. It identifies whether an image was examined and gives its location. Its unresolved question is answerable: “Does the surviving inscription identify the bearer?” is better than “What does it all mean?” A weaker submission invents symbolism, substitutes a modern drawing for the object without saying so, or treats a catalogue attribution as personal entitlement.

</details>

The point of this first unit is not to memorize a list of exotic words. It is to learn how a recognizable design, a particular image, a bearer, and a documentary claim fit together. Once you can keep those four things in view, the vocabulary becomes much easier to use intelligently.
