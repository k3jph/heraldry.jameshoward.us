## Begin with a place, a date, and a kind of claim

“Is this coat of arms legal?” is not yet a well-formed research question. Which place? Which date? Which person or institution? Which use? Are we asking about a new grant, inherited arms, registration, reproduction of an image, or a claim to a particular external ornament? Each question can lead to different evidence.

Heraldic practice combines institutions, customary conventions, documentary acts, and particular rights. A design can be conventionally heraldic without having been granted by a public authority. A document can register an existing design rather than create it. A person can own a painting without being the armorial bearer it depicts. The first task is to identify what kind of proposition you need to establish.

This unit compares several frameworks. It teaches how to read the evidence for authority. It does not adjudicate an individual's claim or substitute a general survey for a particular instrument. A strong comparison states exactly what each source supports and where its authority applies.

## Five documentary verbs

**Grant**, **registration**, **confirmation**, **matriculation**, and **assumption** describe different kinds of events. Their precise legal implications depend on the system. Do not normalize them all into “approved” merely because that makes a spreadsheet easier to read.

| Working category | Research question |
|---|---|
| Grant | What instrument creates or confers the arms, and under whose authority? |
| Registration | What existing arms or use is recorded, and what does recording do? |
| Confirmation | What prior status or design is recognized, and why is confirmation needed? |
| Matriculation | What branch or bearer is entered, with what documented difference? |
| Assumption | Who adopted the design, when, and under which custom or legal setting? |

These questions are more useful than a global ranking in which every grant is “real” and every assumed coat is “fake.” That ranking imports one institution's framework into contexts where it may not apply. It also hides differences among documents that sound similarly official.

For a source record, preserve the original act type and wording. Add your working category separately. If you are unsure whether an instrument confirms, registers, or grants, explain the uncertainty rather than silently choosing the most impressive term.

## England and Wales: an institutional account

The College of Arms describes armorial authority as deriving from the Crown and explains the role of the Kings of Arms and the Court of Chivalry. Its account of descent and marshalling is specific to its framework.[^college] An institutional account is indispensable for learning what that institution says; a historical argument may also need the underlying commissions, instruments, cases, and dated practice.

Do not treat every page carrying the word “heraldry” as equally authoritative on that framework. A commercial surname catalogue, a heraldic society's teaching guide, a grant, and a court record are different source genres. They can answer different questions. The source's relationship to the claim matters more than whether its typography looks old-fashioned.

Equally, do not convert the College's guidance into a rule for Scotland, Canada, France, or the United States. Comparing systems requires preserving their differences. A source can be authoritative within its scope without being authoritative everywhere.

## Scotland: record the act and the pedigree

The Court of the Lord Lyon's guidance distinguishes grants and matriculations. Its register consultation guidance identifies the Public Register and explains how historical entries can be searched.[^lyon] For a researcher, the useful object is the particular entry, not simply the fact that a register exists.

A research note should state whether it uses an index, an image of an entry, an extract, or an institutional summary. An index helps locate evidence. It is not the same as reading the full record. If the entry narrates a pedigree, identify the relevant text and distinguish its recorded assertions from any additional genealogy you have supplied.

The record's date is important. A later entry can document a later act while referring to earlier family use. Do not collapse those dates into one. “Arms recorded in this entry on this date” and “arms allegedly used by an ancestor on an earlier date” require different citations.

## Canada: registration and confirmation are visible distinctions

The Canadian Heraldic Authority's registration guidance distinguishes recording existing officially granted emblems from confirmation after a recipient's name change. It notes that changes to the emblems can require a new grant.[^registration] These distinctions explain why a register entry's act type matters.

Consider a university whose name changes while its arms remain the same. A confirmation can address continuity of bearer or name without implying the shield was newly invented on the confirmation date. A careless catalogue that stores only one date and calls every record a grant would lose that history.

For comparison, ask what is continuous and what changes: legal person, name, design, entitlement, record, or artwork. Those are separate variables. A single document may clarify several of them, but the researcher should still identify each proposition explicitly.

## Flanders: read the original administrative text

The Flemish implementation decree of **17 July 2000**, under the decree of **3 February 1998**, provides a concrete non-English administrative source. Its articles distinguish an application, preparation of a *wapenbrief*, registration, and the official register. The consolidated text displays dates for its provisions.[^flanders]

This is an ideal comparative reading exercise. Retain *wapenbrief* in the transcription and explain your working English equivalent. Identify the roles of the council, applicant, artist, and government without merging them into a single “herald.” Record which version of the article you consulted. An administrative fee printed in a historical provision should not be presented as a current quotation without checking the current guidance.

A translation is not complete merely because the Dutch has become fluent English. The institutional relationships must survive. Who recommends? Who approves? Who records? What document is issued? These verbs are the machinery of authority. Losing them turns a legal-administrative source into a vague story about receiving a nice picture.

## France: an armorial can be an administrative corpus

The Bibliothèque nationale de France describes the *Armorial général de France* as arising from an edict of **1696** and distinguishes textual and painted manuscript series.[^france] This makes the corpus an excellent warning against treating every armorial entry as the same kind of legal event.

A painted entry can establish what that witness depicts. A textual record can establish what it records. A modern index can connect names and locations. Whether a particular entry demonstrates earlier use, noble status, or a distinct legal entitlement requires further evidence. The existence of an enormous official-looking armorial does not make every inference drawn from it official.

In comparative work, ask how a record came to be created and what population it sought to record. Registers shaped by administration can answer questions about administration very well. They may answer genealogical or social questions only after their enrollment conditions and limits are understood.

## United States: recommendations have an identified author

The American Heraldry Society's guidelines present themselves as recommended practice and expressly disclaim authority to mandate compliance. They describe the assumption of original personal arms in the United States and oppose taking another bearer's arms merely through a shared surname.[^american] These are the society's stated recommendations and account of practice, not a government grant procedure.

That distinction should remain visible in citations. “The Society recommends” is an accurate way to discuss its guidance. “American law requires this cadency mark” would need a different source. Similarly, a private register should be identified as private. Its record can be useful evidence of a documented adoption or publication without being mislabeled a sovereign grant.

When researching a specific American use, define the emblem and issue precisely. An institution's protected seal, a reproduced artwork, and a voluntarily assumed personal coat do not raise identical questions. The absence of a personal heraldic grant procedure does not answer every question about every public emblem or image.

## Workshop: build a comparison that can be checked

Compare three systems using the same six fields: territorial and institutional scope, act types, issuing or recording body, documentary requirements, transmission framework, and unresolved questions. Cite an original-language institutional or normative source for each. Do not fill an empty field with a rule borrowed from a neighboring country.

<summary>What makes the comparison strong?</summary>

It preserves differences in terminology and authority. It dates its sources, distinguishes current guidance from historical provisions, and does not pretend that a brief table is a complete statement of law. Every cell either has evidence or is explicitly unresolved. The conclusion explains one substantive difference and how that difference changes the documents a researcher must seek.

For a graduate extension, follow one institutional summary to a cited underlying instrument. Compare what the summary says with the actual wording. Identify whether the summary omits a qualification, a procedural step, or a historical limitation. That is how a survey becomes source criticism.
