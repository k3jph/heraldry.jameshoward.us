## Begin with a place, a date, and a kind of claim

“Is this coat of arms legal?” is not yet a well-formed research question. Which place? Which date? Which person or institution? Which use? Are we asking about a new grant, inherited arms, registration, reproduction of an image, or a claim to a particular external ornament? Each question can lead to different evidence.

Heraldic practice combines institutions, customary conventions, documentary acts, and particular rights. A design can be conventionally heraldic without having been granted by a public authority. A document can register an existing design rather than create it. A person can own a painting without being the armorial bearer it depicts. The first task is to identify what kind of proposition you need to establish.

This unit compares several frameworks. It teaches how to read the evidence for authority. It does not adjudicate an individual's claim or substitute a general survey for a particular instrument. A strong comparison states exactly what each source supports and where its authority applies.

## Five documentary verbs

**Grant**, **registration**, **confirmation**, **matriculation**, and **assumption** describe different kinds of events. Their precise legal implications depend on the system. Do not normalize them all into “approved” merely because that makes a spreadsheet easier to read.

| Working category | Research question |
|---|---|
| Grant | What instrument creates or confers the arms, and under whose authority? |
| Registration | What existing arms or use is recorded, and what does recording do? |
| Confirmation | What prior status or design is recognized, and why is confirmation needed? |
| Matriculation | What branch or bearer is entered, with what documented difference? |
| Assumption | Who adopted the design, when, and under which custom or legal setting? |

These questions are more useful than a global ranking in which every grant is “real” and every assumed coat is “fake.” That ranking imports one institution's framework into contexts where it may not apply. It also hides differences among documents that sound similarly official.

For a source record, preserve the original act type and wording. Add your working category separately. If you are unsure whether an instrument confirms, registers, or grants, explain the uncertainty rather than silently choosing the most impressive term.

## England and Wales: an institutional account

The College of Arms describes armorial authority as deriving from the Crown and explains the role of the Kings of Arms and the Court of Chivalry. Its account of descent and marshalling is specific to its framework.[^college] An institutional account is indispensable for learning what that institution says; a historical argument may also need the underlying commissions, instruments, cases, and dated practice.

Do not treat every page carrying the word “heraldry” as equally authoritative on that framework. A commercial surname catalogue, a heraldic society's teaching guide, a grant, and a court record are different source genres. They can answer different questions. The source's relationship to the claim matters more than whether its typography looks old-fashioned.

Equally, do not convert the College's guidance into a rule for Scotland, Canada, France, or the United States. Comparing systems requires preserving their differences. A source can be authoritative within its scope without being authoritative everywhere.

## Scotland: record the act and the pedigree

The Court of the Lord Lyon's guidance distinguishes grants and matriculations. Its register consultation guidance identifies the Public Register and explains how historical entries can be searched.[^lyon] For a researcher, the useful object is the particular entry, not simply the fact that a register exists.

A research note should state whether it uses an index, an image of an entry, an extract, or an institutional summary. An index helps locate evidence. It is not the same as reading the full record. If the entry narrates a pedigree, identify the relevant text and distinguish its recorded assertions from any additional genealogy you have supplied.

The record's date is important. A later entry can document a later act while referring to earlier family use. Do not collapse those dates into one. “Arms recorded in this entry on this date” and “arms allegedly used by an ancestor on an earlier date” require different citations.

## Canada: registration and confirmation are visible distinctions

The Canadian Heraldic Authority's registration guidance distinguishes recording existing officially granted emblems from confirmation after a recipient's name change. It notes that changes to the emblems can require a new grant.[^registration] These distinctions explain why a register entry's act type matters.

Consider a university whose name changes while its arms remain the same. A confirmation can address continuity of bearer or name without implying the shield was newly invented on the confirmation date. A careless catalogue that stores only one date and calls every record a grant would lose that history.

For comparison, ask what is continuous and what changes: legal person, name, design, entitlement, record, or artwork. Those are separate variables. A single document may clarify several of them, but the researcher should still identify each proposition explicitly.

## Flanders: read the original administrative text

The Flemish implementation decree of **17 July 2000**, under the decree of **3 February 1998**, provides a concrete non-English administrative source. Its articles distinguish an application, preparation of a *wapenbrief*, registration, and the official register. The consolidated text displays dates for its provisions.[^flanders]

This is an ideal comparative reading exercise. Retain *wapenbrief* in the transcription and explain your working English equivalent. Identify the roles of the council, applicant, artist, and government without merging them into a single “herald.” Record which version of the article you consulted. An administrative fee printed in a historical provision should not be presented as a current quotation without checking the current guidance.

A translation is not complete merely because the Dutch has become fluent English. The institutional relationships must survive. Who recommends? Who approves? Who records? What document is issued? These verbs are the machinery of authority. Losing them turns a legal-administrative source into a vague story about receiving a nice picture.

## France: an armorial can be an administrative corpus

The Bibliothèque nationale de France describes the *Armorial général de France* as arising from an edict of **1696** and distinguishes textual and painted manuscript series.[^france] This makes the corpus an excellent warning against treating every armorial entry as the same kind of legal event.

A painted entry can establish what that witness depicts. A textual record can establish what it records. A modern index can connect names and locations. Whether a particular entry demonstrates earlier use, noble status, or a distinct legal entitlement requires further evidence. The existence of an enormous official-looking armorial does not make every inference drawn from it official.

In comparative work, ask how a record came to be created and what population it sought to record. Registers shaped by administration can answer questions about administration very well. They may answer genealogical or social questions only after their enrollment conditions and limits are understood.

## United States: recommendations have an identified author

The American Heraldry Society's guidelines present themselves as recommended practice and expressly disclaim authority to mandate compliance. They describe the assumption of original personal arms in the United States and oppose taking another bearer's arms merely through a shared surname.[^american] These are the society's stated recommendations and account of practice, not a government grant procedure.

That distinction should remain visible in citations. “The Society recommends” is an accurate way to discuss its guidance. “American law requires this cadency mark” would need a different source. Similarly, a private register should be identified as private. Its record can be useful evidence of a documented adoption or publication without being mislabeled a sovereign grant.

When researching a specific American use, define the emblem and issue precisely. An institution's protected seal, a reproduced artwork, and a voluntarily assumed personal coat do not raise identical questions. The absence of a personal heraldic grant procedure does not answer every question about every public emblem or image.

## Authority is a relationship, not an adjective

Calling an image “official” leaves the central question unanswered. Official for whom, through which act, and for which purpose? A municipal council can adopt a visual identity. A specialist body can advise on the composition. Another officer can issue the document that gives the emblem its recognized status. An archive can preserve that document without having made the decision. All four bodies may appear on official websites, but they do not exercise the same function.

Begin by making an actor map. Identify the applicant or adopting body, the adviser, the decision-maker, the recorder, and any body that hears a challenge. Then connect them with verbs drawn from the actual source. An arrow marked “recommends” should not be converted to one marked “grants” because both end at an attractive shield. This is a small discipline with large consequences: it prevents the research from awarding powers to the wrong institution.

Next identify the object of the act. Is it a personal coat, corporate coat, flag, badge, public seal, protected name, or specific image? An instrument concerning one can leave the others untouched. A municipality's approved arms and a designer's copyrighted rendering can coexist as different objects of rights. Owning the canvas, commissioning the design, bearing the arms, and controlling an institutional seal are relationships that may overlap, but they must be demonstrated separately.

Finally identify the population and territory to which the act applies. “Private persons,” “public bodies,” “residents,” and “descendants” are not interchangeable eligibility categories. Nor does a national label guarantee one procedure for every region or every class of bearer. The most useful comparison is therefore a set of scoped mechanisms. A map colored with countries can introduce the subject; it cannot finish the argument.

## What kinds of source can establish the mechanism?

A statute can define a power or restriction. A regulation can specify how that power is administered. A warrant or letters patent can perform a particular act. A register can preserve its record. A judgment can resolve an issue raised in a particular proceeding. A procedural guide can explain how the institution currently expects applicants to proceed. A society's recommendation can document that society's preferred practice. These sources have different jobs even when they use much of the same vocabulary.

Read them in relation to the proposition. To establish that a body granted specified arms on a date, seek the instrument or identified register entry. To establish that the body had power to act, seek the relevant constitutional or statutory foundation. To establish what a court decided, distinguish the decision from the parties' submissions. To establish what applicants are presently told, use the current institutional guidance and record when you consulted it. A source need not answer every question to be excellent evidence for one.

The reverse problem is a source being asked to do work outside its competence. A museum catalogue may provide a careful attribution of an armorial object without determining a living person's entitlement. A genealogical database may suggest a line to investigate without proving every generation. A private register may establish a documented publication date without creating a public-law grant. Its usefulness depends on the question, not on whether we can promote it to a more impressive genre.

Keep the original terminology when translating. If a source distinguishes an *avis* from a decision, or a registration from a confirmation, the English should preserve the distinction. Supply a working equivalent and explain the function. Translation is partly an exercise in institutional anatomy: the limbs should still connect to the same body after we have changed the labels.

## England and Wales: separate descent from display

The College's account provides a useful correction to a common beginner's model. Arms granted to a man pass equally to his legitimate children in its stated framework; the page does not describe the eldest son as the only child who receives arms. It also describes cadency marks as something children may use. Inheritance and the choice to distinguish a particular display therefore require separate treatment.[^english-descent]

Marshalling introduces another set of conditions. The page's English category of heraldic heiress concerns the transmission of paternal arms as a quartering under specified family circumstances. It is not a general synonym for a wealthy daughter or a rule that every ancestor contributes a quarter. Use the actual category, then test the particular lineage. A pedigree provides the relationships to examine; it does not automatically supply the heraldic consequence.

The College expressly frames its page as a limited account rather than a comprehensive statement of all law. That qualification is part of the source. A current educational summary can guide us toward the relevant mechanism, but a historical dispute may depend on an earlier instrument, a particular grant's destination, or a judgment. The careful researcher moves from the general account to the specific evidence instead of using the account as an infinitely expandable answer.

This matters when comparing gendered display conventions. A source can distinguish the shape on which a woman displays arms, the inheritance of the shield, the use of a crest, and a marital arrangement. Those are separate questions, even if a manual puts them in the same chapter. Preserve the source's date and scope, identify which question it answers, and avoid presenting its arrangements as the inevitable geometry of all heraldry.

## Scotland: an administrative act and a judicial act

The Lyon Court's own description distinguishes the grant of new arms as an administrative act from matriculation as a judicial act and describes different routes for review or appeal. This is more than a terminological curiosity. It shows why “the herald approved it” can conceal the character of the proceeding and the documents through which a result can be examined.[^lyon-functions]

For a matriculation study, identify the earlier recorded arms, the applicant's relationship to the relevant bearer, the terms governing transmission, and the difference entered for the applicant. An illustration of similar arms is not a substitute for that chain. The institution's procedural guidance tells us what sorts of evidence are relevant, while the particular entry tells us what was actually recorded.

The Public Register also changes as a historical source. National Records of Scotland notes that its first volume, covering 1672–1804, generally contains brief records without the fuller genealogies and extensive illumination a reader might expect from later examples. A database designed around lavish modern entries can therefore misrepresent the early volume by treating its absent fields as defects. The right question is what the record was intended to preserve at that time.[^scottish-register]

The published **Irving of Bonshaw** decision, dated 25 August 2014, supplies a useful check on overly mechanical rules. Paragraph 14 discusses periodic matriculation practice without treating the absence of such matriculation as automatically extinguishing an heir's right to undifferenced arms. That is a particular court's reasoning in a particular proceeding. It should correct an absolute claim that non-recording necessarily destroys the right; it should not become an invented universal exemption from every other requirement.[^bonshaw-law]

Read a judgment with its internal distinctions intact. The petitioner's claim, counsel's argument, evidence summarized by the court, findings, and final order belong in different columns. If the research quotes a litigant's proposition as “the court held,” it has changed the author of the statement. Heraldic litigation is already complicated enough without assigning everyone the judge's chair.

## Canada: creation, continuity, and record access

The Canadian Heraldic Authority's establishment in 1988 is a dated institutional event. Its public register subsequently provides records of grants, registrations, and confirmations, with descriptions and attributed symbolism. That infrastructure makes it possible to compare acts rather than infer them from the appearance of the artwork.[^canadian-foundation]

The Universities Canada entry illustrates continuity particularly well. The 2015 confirmation refers to an earlier 2004 record under a previous organizational name. A catalogue should preserve both dates and both event types. The confirmation date is not automatically the date on which the shield's design first existed, and the earlier name is not necessarily a second unrelated armorial bearer. Explain the documented relationship instead of asking a single “date” field to do all the work.[^canadian-continuity]

The Authority also describes recording First Nations emblems within its work. That should lead to careful questions about the nature of each record and the community's own authority, rather than an assumption that every recorded emblem has been converted into a European coat of arms. Read the particular instrument and terminology. Inclusion in an institutional collection can create a documentary relationship without making every represented tradition interchangeable.

Operational availability is a further variable. On **10 October 2026**, the grant guidance consulted for this chapter stated that new applications were not being accepted because of operational requirements. An office's powers, its existing register, and its present capacity to receive an application are distinct facts. The notice may change; the dated statement belongs to this edition's account rather than to the timeless definition of Canadian heraldry.[^canadian-availability]

## Ireland: institutional succession and a statutory function

The National Library of Ireland places the Chief Herald's office within its own institutional history, identifies the earlier Ulster office, and directs readers to section 13 of the National Cultural Institutions Act 1997 for the statutory functions. Its account also identifies the Genealogical Office manuscripts and the Register of Arms as research resources. These are several connected histories: an office, its powers, its archive, and its records of individual acts.[^ireland]

Institutional succession does not mean every earlier act can be described in the vocabulary of the present office. An older document may name a different official and operate in a different political setting. Preserve those names and dates. Then explain how the archive reached its current custodian. The custodian's present name helps us find the document; the document's historical issuer helps us interpret it.

This distinction is especially useful for a digitized manuscript. The modern library supplies the image, catalogue identifier, and access service. The historical official produced or recorded the act. A later index may add identifications. A citation can name all of these roles without pretending the modern library issued a sixteenth-century grant. The chain of custody is evidence of preservation, not a time machine for administration.

The office's page consulted on **10 October 2026** also stated that new applications for grants or confirmations were paused until further notice because of resourcing capability. Here again, the archive remains useful while a new application service is unavailable. A practical guide should point readers to the dated notice rather than promise a service merely because an older manual describes it.

## Flanders: old arms are a defined evidentiary category

The underlying Flemish decree of **3 February 1998** distinguishes old arms supported by at least a century of public use from new arms that do not meet that evidentiary definition. Its categories are administrative definitions within the decree's scope, not a global judgment about whether a design looks medieval. An old-looking shield adopted yesterday does not acquire a century of public use from its Gothic lettering.[^flemish-definition]

For an application based on historical use, the key task is therefore documenting that use. A series of dated objects, records, or publications can support a chronology. A single undated family drawing cannot establish the required span simply because someone reports that it is ancient. The decree makes an evidentiary threshold visible, and the implementation text supplies the procedural setting in which evidence is evaluated.

Read the eligibility provisions and their amendments too. The consolidated text displays versions and includes a distinct treatment of private persons and institutions, with public bodies addressed through other frameworks. “Flemish heraldry” is too broad a category to identify the procedure without first identifying the applicant. Territory, legal status, and type of emblem help determine which text to consult.

The *wapenbrief* then belongs within a chain of steps, not merely as an ornate final picture. Track application, advice or preparation, decision, document, and registration according to the identified provisions. The painter's authorship is another relationship again. A beautiful document can be both an artwork and a record of an institutional act; its beauty does not tell us which officer performed which step.

## The Netherlands: public bodies form a specific remit

The Hoge Raad van Adel's account of government arms presents a history of changing procedure. It identifies an early nineteenth-century system for confirming existing public arms, a later return to royal decisions, and subsequent provisions concerning public bodies. Its contemporary account should be read alongside that chronology rather than projected backward without qualification.[^netherlands]

The published regulatory overview also describes a 2013 policy limiting new grants by royal decree to public bodies, while noting that private institutions had received grants previously. This gives us a precise example of why a historical grant and present eligibility can diverge. A private institution's older royal grant is evidence of the act at that time. It does not establish that an identical applicant can use the same route today.

Municipal change introduces another problem. A new legal body can share a name with an older one, incorporate its territory, or inherit parts of its public functions. Those similarities may matter to a decision about arms, but they are not themselves the decision. Seek the relevant decree and any advice about continuity or replacement. Otherwise a modern catalogue can accidentally grant the old arms to a new municipality by spelling its name the same way.

For comparison, keep the public-body procedure separate from questions about personal or family arms. A source explaining government grants cannot be enlarged into a complete account of every private armorial claim in the Netherlands. The boundary of the source is useful information. It tells us which questions require a different body of evidence rather than inviting us to fill them from a neighboring country's practice.

## Sweden: a state emblem and a protected use

Sweden offers two different statutory questions. The 1982 law describes the national arms and their forms. The **1970 law protecting arms and certain other official designations** addresses specified uses, including commercial use of protected emblems and registered municipal arms. A researcher asking what the arms are needs the former; one asking whether a particular use requires permission may need the latter.[^swedish-framework]

The protection law also reaches confusingly similar signs. That shows why a copy with a slightly different line or feather does not necessarily avoid the problem merely by being a new drawing. The protected relationship may concern the official emblem or resemblance, rather than a particular image file. Equally, the law's defined categories and uses must remain visible. It is not evidence that every privately assumed shield in Sweden requires the same permission.

Riksarkivet's guidance adds an administrative and artistic layer. Its examples distinguish a stable armorial description from different renderings, and its public-symbol guidance explains the role of consultation in composing official arms. Those materials tell us how the institution advises users and designers. Read them alongside the statutory text to distinguish advice, approval, registration, and protected use.[^swedish-advice]

This comparison is also a reminder that the right to reproduce a photograph and the right to present an emblem as one's own are different. An open image license can address the image's reuse while a public-emblem restriction addresses the context of display. Neither proposition is resolved simply by finding a download button. Identify the object and activity to which each permission relates.

## Finland: an archive records changing municipal procedure

The National Archives of Finland's **Heraldica** collection guide explains the historical handling of municipal arms, including a 1949 legal framework and a change in 1995 that ended the earlier ministry-approval procedure. It also distinguishes groups of arms and seals within the collection. This is an archive explaining the records generated by changing administration, not a current application form.[^finland]

That chronology changes how we interpret a dated municipal record. A mid-twentieth-century confirmation belongs to a different procedural setting from a later local adoption. Comparing the two requires knowing which office acted and which records its action created. Otherwise the absence of a ministry confirmation in a later file can be mistaken for a defect when the procedure itself had changed.

The collection's digitized arms and seals provide a strong corpus for investigating those changes. Start with a limited period and class of record. Count confirmations, drawings, seal impressions, or identifiable municipalities separately. The archive's total number of images does not automatically equal the number of distinct coats, and its historical coverage does not make it a complete register of every private armorial use.

## Czech municipal heraldry: a recommendation and a grant

The Czech Chamber of Deputies' **REKOS** resource describes the parliamentary process for municipal symbols and supplies a searchable register. Its account distinguishes expert consideration and recommendation from the chair's grant. That actor distinction should survive translation and summary. A committee's favorable opinion is one step in the process, not simply another name for the final act.[^czech-process]

The published heraldic principles are particularly useful because they articulate design expectations for new municipal arms. They address distinctiveness, reconstructibility from a verbal description, the treatment of historical seals, and restrictions on external ornaments in new designs while recognizing documented historical exceptions. These are scoped principles for the identified procedure. They should not be mistaken for rules governing every personal coat ever used in the Czech lands.[^czech-principles]

A historical seal can supply a basis for a new composition without needing to be copied as a miniature landscape. The guide's preference for heraldic stylization explains why a building or saint's attribute may be adapted into a clearer figure. But adaptation is a proposed design act, not a claim that the older seal already contained the new arms. Record both witnesses and the institutional decision linking them.

The requirement for consent where relevant living families' or legal persons' arms are involved introduces another relationship. A historical connection to a locality does not by itself resolve permission to reuse an existing coat. A design dossier should preserve the connection, the proposed armorial borrowing, and the documented consent as different pieces of evidence. The final image cannot tell us that all three were obtained.

## South Africa: registration is a statutory subject

South Africa's government publishes the **Heraldry Act 18 of 1962**, whose original text establishes a bureau and council and addresses armorial and related emblems. The National Archives' institutional account describes registration and legal protection as central functions. The government listing also identifies subsequent amendments. Those layers should be kept together: original enactment, amendment history, current institutional guidance, and the particular record under study.[^south-africa]

The original statute's definitions distinguish a coat of arms from a badge and recognize external accessories without requiring every coat to display them. Reading definitions first prevents us from assuming that a shield-only record falls outside the system because it lacks a helmet. It also prevents a badge from becoming a coat merely because both are included in the institution's work.

For a contemporary procedural or enforcement question, the unamended 1962 scan is insufficient by itself. Follow the amendment history and consult the relevant current instrument and guidance. That is an ordinary historical discipline, not a reason to ignore the original. The original explains the framework's creation; later provisions explain how it changed. A study should state which version supports each proposition.

The comparison with a British grant should then concern actual mechanisms: who acts, what is registered or conferred, what document is issued, and what protection follows under the identified provisions. Avoid choosing an English verb solely because it sounds familiar. An institutional framework deserves to be understood on its own documentary terms before we decide which comparisons are useful.

## Germany: public protection is not a complete private law survey

Section **124 of the German law on administrative offences** addresses unauthorized use of specified federal or Land arms, the federal eagle, and official flags, including confusingly similar signs. It supplies a clear primary source for a defined public-emblem restriction.[^germany-public]

It does not, by itself, explain every claim concerning a private family's arms. A survey that jumps from this provision to a universal personal-armorial rule has changed its subject. Private claims may require different legal sources, relevant judgments, evidence of adoption or transmission, and the precise issue in dispute. The fact that a public coat is protected does not tell us that a particular private applicant needs a sovereign grant.

Similarly, section 12 of the Civil Code concerns protection of names. Its text is not a self-contained heraldic manual. If an argument says that a court applies a named legal principle to arms, identify the judgment and its reasoning rather than treating the Code's name provision as though it literally lists all armorial consequences. This is a useful stopping rule: a familiar citation should lead us to evidence, not replace the evidence we have not found.[^germany-names]

Historical and private armorial registers remain valuable sources for their own recorded entries. Describe the publisher or registering body and the act actually performed. A private publication can establish a design's documented appearance and attribution. Whether that appearance proves a disputed exclusive right is a further proposition. The register's heraldic expertise and its legal effect should be assessed separately.

## Italy: titles, names, and arms are different questions

The Italian Constitution's **fourteenth transitional and final provision** states that noble titles are not recognized, treats specified earlier territorial predicates as part of a name, and addresses the suppression of the Consulta araldica through law. Its wording provides a much more precise starting point than the claim that “Italy abolished heraldry.”[^italy]

The provision concerns named constitutional subjects. It does not make every historical coat disappear from an archive or building, and it should not be enlarged into a complete answer about every modern civic emblem, personal display, or artwork. Ask which proposition follows from the text and which requires another source. A constitutional treatment of noble titles is not identical to an attribution of a Renaissance painted shield.

The distinction also helps when encountering old files from a former heraldic institution. Their historical evidentiary value does not depend on that institution retaining its earlier powers today. An instrument can document a past act; an archive can preserve it; current law can treat a related title differently. Those propositions can all be true without giving the historical file a present power it no longer has.

## France: distinguish the witnesses within the armorial

The BnF guide's manuscript ranges are useful practical anchors: the textual and painted series have different shelfmarks and functions. A researcher should identify the actual volume, folio or page, entry, and witness type rather than cite “d'Hozier” as if it were one indivisible book. A later printed extraction or index has its own date and editorial choices.[^france]

This matters for comparison. A name in an index may direct us to an entry; it does not show us the image's colors or the textual description's spelling. An image may display a design; it does not independently explain the enrollment conditions. An administrative corpus can be studied as evidence of representation, participation, and record-making, but those arguments need the procedure that generated the records.

Noble status is a separate proposition again. A claim that an entry proves nobility must be supported by evidence about that entry and the governing conditions, not by the fact that the collection appears under a modern genealogical guide's “noblesse” heading. Catalogue organization helps readers navigate. It does not transform every person within a large administrative corpus into a legally equivalent category.

## Beyond a country-by-country list

These comparisons do not exhaust the world's armorial practices. The [national traditions studies](/traditions/) provide routes into further regional histories, including shared armorial affiliations, non-European systems of identity, and colonial or postcolonial transformations. A global resource grows by expanding such evidence, not by filling every country cell with a borrowed English rule.

The same territory can contain personal, corporate, municipal, military, royal, and religious systems whose authorities differ. A church can regulate an official display within its own institutional setting while a state protects its national emblem and a private society recommends personal practice. The relevant jurisdiction is sometimes an institution or class of act as much as a place on a map.

Comparison should therefore end with a mechanism we can explain. One system's registration may record pre-existing arms; another act may create or confirm a design; another source may merely recommend a convention. The verbs tell us what additional evidence to seek and what conclusions remain unavailable. That is a stronger result than declaring a winner in a competition for the most official-looking parchment.

## Five problems in authority

### 1. The artist signed the grant

**Constructed problem:** a decorated document names an issuing official, records an armorial act, and bears an artist's signature. A caption calls the artist the granting authority. Correct it.

<details><summary>Answer discussion</summary>

Distinguish the act's issuer from the artwork's maker. Preserve both credits and identify the evidence for each. The artist's important contribution does not establish a power to confer arms, and identifying the issuer does not erase the artist's authorship.

</details>

### 2. A private register is called a royal grant

**Constructed problem:** a society records an assumed coat and issues a certificate. No royal act is identified. What can the certificate establish?

<details><summary>Answer discussion</summary>

It can document what the society recorded, when, and for whom, subject to the actual wording. It cannot be relabeled a sovereign grant without the relevant evidence. Examine the society's rules and the record's asserted basis; assess usefulness and legal effect as separate questions.

</details>

### 3. The institution has paused applications

Compare the Irish and Canadian notices cited above. Which facts about the offices remain supported while new applications are paused?

<details><summary>Answer discussion</summary>

Their histories, published records, and described functions remain research subjects. Present application availability is a dated operational fact that can change. A pause does not erase earlier grants, and a historic power does not establish that an application will be accepted today.

</details>

### 4. The first volume lacks a pedigree

A researcher expects a Scottish early-register entry to contain the genealogy found in a later example and treats its absence as proof of invalidity. How should the collection guide change the analysis?

<details><summary>Answer discussion</summary>

The guide identifies a different record format for the early volume. Evaluate the entry against its historical function and seek additional genealogy where needed. The absence of a field that the source did not normally contain is not the same as a failed requirement in a later procedure.

</details>

### 5. One law is made to answer two questions

Compare Sweden's national-arms description with its official-emblem protection law. Explain why a correct drawing and an authorized use require different inquiries.

<details><summary>Answer discussion</summary>

The description defines the emblem and permitted forms. The protection law concerns specified contexts of use and permission. A drawing can be armorially accurate while its use raises another question; a permission to use an emblem does not prove that a drawing reproduces it correctly.

</details>

## Research project: follow one power to one act

Select a dated armorial act and identify its issuer, beneficiary, emblem, documentary genre, and stated basis of authority. Follow that basis to a relevant underlying instrument. Then compare the individual record with the institution's general guidance. Explain which propositions are established by each source and where the guidance simplifies a more complex mechanism.

Prepare a version history as well. Record the instrument's date, later amendments if relevant, the record's date, and the date on which you consulted current guidance. A chronology with four dates may look less convenient than a single year, but it will tell the reader what actually happened. Finish with a scoped conclusion: this body performed this act for this bearer under this identified framework. That is the level at which authority becomes researchable.

## Workshop: build a comparison that can be checked

Compare three systems using the same six fields: territorial and institutional scope, act types, issuing or recording body, documentary requirements, transmission framework, and unresolved questions. Cite an original-language institutional or normative source for each. Do not fill an empty field with a rule borrowed from a neighboring country.

<details>
<summary>What makes the comparison strong?</summary>

It preserves differences in terminology and authority. It dates its sources, distinguishes current guidance from historical provisions, and does not pretend that a brief table is a complete statement of law. Every cell either has evidence or is explicitly unresolved. The conclusion explains one substantive difference and how that difference changes the documents a researcher must seek.
