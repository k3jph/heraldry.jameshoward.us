## Related does not necessarily mean identical

Cadency concerns the differentiation of related armorial bearings. A difference may indicate a branch, a person, or a relationship under a particular convention. It is not simply a decorative sticker added to make a familiar shield look new. The first question is which system and period you are studying.[^history]

Think of a family tree and an armorial tree as different records that can be connected. The genealogical tree states relationships between people. The armorial record states designs, grants, registrations, inheritances, or uses. A relationship in the first does not automatically determine the precise form in the second. The connection requires the applicable transmission rules and evidence of what happened.

This is particularly important because people move, institutions change, and specific grants can contain particular provisions. A modern residence, a surname's geographic origin, and a source's jurisdiction may be three different things. Identify the documentary context before choosing a cadency chart.

## The familiar English sequence

The College of Arms describes a conventional English sequence: label for the eldest son during his father's lifetime, then crescent, mullet, martlet, annulet, fleur-de-lis, rose, cross moline, and double quatrefoil for subsequent sons.[^english] Memorizing the sequence is useful for recognizing a possible convention. It is not a complete method for reconstructing a pedigree from a shield.

To use that sequence in a historical argument, we first have to establish that the figure functions as a difference. A martlet placed among the principal charges gives us no reason, by itself, to assign birth order. Even a recognizable difference needs a dated context: the convention may have been applied unevenly, and the bearer’s circumstances or a particular instrument may affect its use. The chart helps formulate a question about the picture. The source has to supply the connection to the person.

If a catalogue describes an ordinary coat containing a crescent, preserve that description. Do not silently add “second son” to the bearer field. Instead, record a possible cadency interpretation separately, with the grounds for it and the evidence still needed. A possibility becomes a claim only when you supply the missing support.

## Scottish matriculation is a different process

The Court of the Lord Lyon distinguishes a new **grant** from a **matriculation** identifying an applicant's place in an armigerous family. Its guidance calls for genealogical proof and entry in the Public Register.[^scotland] This is an institutional process, not merely a choice among clip-art differences.

The practical implication for a learner is that recognizing related arms is not equivalent to determining the design a descendant should bear. The recorded matriculation matters. A sibling or cousin's design may be informative about family relationships but is not necessarily the applicant's own record. Do not transplant an English birth-order sequence into a Scottish case and assume the result has official standing.

For historical study, compare dated entries rather than beginning with an idealized diagram. A difference visible in a later record may not have appeared in an earlier witness. The chronological relationship among the witnesses matters. When a source describes what should happen, distinguish that normative statement from evidence of what did happen in a particular family.

## Canada offers another comparison

Canadian guidance describes an heir to the original arms and differenced **cadet shields** for other children; the default heir is the eldest child unless otherwise specified.[^canada] That is already enough to show why an exclusively male English chart cannot be treated as a universal family algorithm.

The point of comparison is not to decide which tradition is the “real” heraldry. It is to identify the variable: who inherits, how other descendants are distinguished, and what documentation establishes the outcome. Comparative work becomes productive when it asks the same questions of different systems while allowing the answers to differ.

A grant can also be more informative than a general FAQ for an individual case. General guidance explains a framework. The particular instrument establishes what was granted and to whom, within that framework. Always check whether the source you're reading is a general explanation or the actual record at issue.

## Difference is broader than a birth-order mark

Related coats can differ through changed tinctures, bordures, added figures, modified figures, or other devices. The formal interpretation depends on the tradition and source. An **augmentation** associated with honor is not automatically a cadency device; an alteration caused by error is neither. Similarity alone cannot choose between these explanations.[^difference]

This gives us a useful method. First, describe the actual differences. Second, determine whether the bearers are documented as related. Third, inspect records that explain the modifications. Only then assign a role such as cadency, augmentation, or independent adoption. The order matters because otherwise the expected family story starts selecting which observations you notice.

For a digital armorial, use separate fields for observed difference and interpreted function. “Red bordure present” is an observation. “Marks a cadet branch” is an interpretation. The latter may change after new evidence appears; the former should remain traceable to the image.

## Worked problem: three shields and an incomplete tree

**Constructed teaching scenario:** shield A has a blue field with a gold lion. Shield B adds a red bordure. Shield C adds a silver crescent. A manuscript places the designs beside three similar names. A later genealogy calls the people cousins but supplies no references.

You can state the compositional differences. You can report that the manuscript associates each design with a name. You can attribute the later genealogy's assertion. You cannot yet conclude that B is the prescribed coat for a cadet line or that C identifies a second son. The names may represent unrelated people, spellings may have been confused, or the genealogy may be wrong.

What would improve the argument? A documented relationship, a jurisdiction and period, a register entry or grant identifying the change, and comparison with other dated uses. If the source contains a pedigree narrative, transcribe the relevant wording rather than citing “family tradition” as though it were a single stable document.

## Genealogy needs links, not leaps

An inheritance argument has to bridge generations. A familiar surname at each end is not the bridge. For every claimed parent-child link, identify the evidence and its limitations. Contemporary records, derivative compilations, and unsupported online trees are not equivalent witnesses.

This does not require pretending that all records are perfect. Names vary, dates conflict, and records can be missing. It requires explaining how you resolve conflicts and how confident you are in the link. A conclusion should be no stronger than the weakest necessary step that remains unresolved.

Keep the genealogical proof separate from the heraldic conclusion. A well-established descent may still not satisfy the particular transmission rule. Conversely, a recorded grant to a present bearer may settle that bearer's design without establishing every claim in an ancient family narrative. Ask the document the question it can answer.

## Inheritance and differentiation are two operations

A system has to answer at least two questions: who can succeed to arms, and how related bearers are distinguished. Those questions are connected but not identical. A person may inherit an armorial right under a rule that permits several relatives to bear a related design. Another system may identify one successor to the undifferenced coat and record separate versions for others. A picture of a difference cannot independently tell us which transmission framework created it.

Think of the genealogy as a graph of people and the armorial history as a graph of acts and designs. A parent-child link connects people. A grant links a bearer to a specification. An inheritance rule connects a particular genealogical relationship to a possible armorial outcome. A matriculation or recorded difference identifies a result in a defined system. Keeping the graphs separate makes it possible to inspect every connection rather than make descent and design look like one automatic family tree.

In a fictional example, a parent has three children. The genealogical fact does not by itself say that the eldest receives one design, the second a crescent, and the third a star. That outcome depends on the relevant convention and instrument. A new grant may expressly include children. A specific record may give personalized differences. Another tradition may not use the expected birth-order marks. The family relationship establishes a premise; the armorial framework supplies the operation applied to it.

This is why a reference chart should begin with its scope. Name the jurisdiction, period, source, and kind of bearer. A chart of elementary English marks can be useful for recognizing a possible convention. It should never become a universal diagram of how all families transmit all arms. The difference between a convenient learning aid and a legal-historical conclusion is the evidence connecting the aid to the case.

## English descent is not simply an eldest-son algorithm

The College of Arms' account says that a man's arms pass equally to his legitimate children irrespective of birth order, within its described framework of male-line transmission. It then says cadency marks may distinguish brothers.[^english] Those propositions should be read together. The chart does not mean that only the eldest son inherits arms or that a younger son's shield is merely borrowed decoration. The marks concern differentiation within the described family practice.

The qualification legitimate belongs to the institutional source's wording and historical legal categories. Preserve it when explaining that source rather than silently rewriting it as a worldwide or timeless rule. For an actual case, the instrument, relevant period, and applicable law matter. A general web summary expressly limited in scope cannot decide every question involving adoption, legitimacy, name changes, or a special destination.

The familiar sequence nevertheless deserves to be understood. A label marks the eldest son during the father's lifetime in the stated convention; crescent, mullet, martlet, annulet, fleur-de-lis, rose, cross moline, and double quatrefoil follow for later sons. The first position is explicitly time-dependent. Reading a label without a date loses part of the convention. A mark's presence at one moment does not necessarily determine its treatment after the father's death or in a later branch.

Do not infer compulsory uniform use from the existence of a conventional sequence. The source's may matters. A historical corpus can show uneven use, inherited differences, artistic omission, or specific provisions. To establish what happened, compare dated witnesses. To establish what a rule prescribed, cite the relevant normative source. A family reconstruction becomes stronger when those two kinds of evidence are compared rather than collapsed.

## A label has shape, position, and duration

A label is conventionally a horizontal figure with pendant points. Its point count, tincture, position, and any charges on it can be meaningful. A three-point label and another point count are not interchangeable because both look like hanging ribbons. A damaged impression may preserve the horizontal strip while losing a pendant. Count what survives and use the source description separately for reconstruction.

Duration is another feature. A label assigned during a parent's lifetime can indicate a temporary differentiation in a particular convention. A label forming part of an inherited or specially granted design may have another status. The same geometric figure can therefore be temporary in one record and permanent in another. Its silhouette does not supply the temporal clause. Read the instrument and compare uses over time.

The Roger Miller grant of 20 May 2016 makes the temporal relation explicit. Its son's recorded version uses a silver three-point label during the father's lifetime and identifies him as the future inheritor. The daughter's version uses a counterchanged bordure.[^miller-cadency] This is evidence of named designs for named children, not a puzzle requiring us to infer birth order from the artwork. The text gives the relationship and the intended succession.

Use that record to separate four fields: person, relationship, current specified design, and future transmission statement. The future statement should not be converted into a completed historical event. The date of the grant records the act; it does not establish that the inheritance has already occurred. A register can describe a conditional future while documenting a secure present specification.

## Canadian cadet shields can make personal references

Canadian general guidance describes differenced shields for children and an eldest-child default for succession unless otherwise specified.[^canada] Actual records show why the particular design matters. A difference need not be selected solely from an abstract birth-order chart. It can make a personal reference while retaining a recognizable relation to the original arms.

Michel Maria Joseph Shore's record names a daughter whose version adds a blue heart and expressly identifies her future inheritance of the recipient's arms. Its commentary connects the heart with her work and with eldest-daughter differentiation.[^shore] The meaning is attributed to the record. A heart found elsewhere is not enough to establish the same profession, relationship, or succession. Shape supplies the possible comparison; the text supplies this specific connection.

The Sheldon Edward Boles record provides a useful counterexample to a single-symbol decoding scheme. It records personalized replacements of a central figure, including a mortar and pestle for a son identified as future inheritor, and a heart for a daughter.[^boles] Here the difference is a substitution within the composition rather than a small separate mark simply dropped over the field. The underlying design stays related while a principal element changes.

Those cases show why a record-based approach is more informative than a universal dictionary of cadency signs. A personalized difference can identify a child and a profession together. A recognizable conventional heart can participate in a source-specific scheme. The grant's actual provisions establish the result. If the diagram is uncertain, follow the named component description rather than inventing the difference that a generic chart would predict.

## Scottish matriculation connects a branch to a record

The Scottish process distinguishes an original grant from matriculation based on a prior grant and documented relationship. The Lyon Court describes the pedigree evidence and destination of the original grant as relevant to the applicant's claim.[^scotland] This makes the original instrument and the connection to it central objects of research. A surname or a remembered clan association cannot replace those objects.

An **undifferenced** coat and a **differenced** version describe an armorial relationship, not a judgment about whose family story is more valuable. The original or senior design may be retained by the relevant successor; another bearer can have a recorded difference. Establish that relationship from the entries and applicable framework. Adding a border yourself does not recreate the judicial or administrative act that a recorded matriculation represents.

Historical Scottish practice often uses borders and modifications in systems of difference, as Fox-Davies's dated account illustrates.[^scottish-differences] The useful lesson is to inspect a set of related entries rather than treat the illustration as an automatic border generator. Tincture, line, additional charges, and existing composition can matter. A difference scheme also has to avoid collision with another recorded design. The actual result is not always recoverable from birth order and one remembered color.

The National Records of Scotland explains that the first register volume, spanning 1672–1804, has brief entries, limited genealogical detail, and few illuminations.[^register-detail] This is a practical warning about evidence availability. An early entry may establish a blazon without providing the complete pedigree a modern learner expects. The absence of a painted shield is not absence of arms; the brevity of the record is not proof that no genealogy existed elsewhere. Follow the relevant documentary trail.

## A real judgment separates guidance from extinction of rights

The Lyon Court's interlocutor in the petition of Robert Alec Snow Irving of Bonshaw, dated 25 August 2014, discusses succession, evidence, and the distinction between court practice and the loss of rights. Paragraph 14 explains that the guidance about periodic matriculation does not by itself extinguish an inheritor's right to undifferenced arms through absence of a recent matriculation.[^bonshaw] This is a much better teaching source than an unattributed claim that arms expire after a fixed number of generations.

Read the judgment in layers. Its opening order records what the court decided. Its findings and reasons distinguish submissions from the court's own analysis. The evidence inventory identifies material placed before it. A proposition stated by counsel is not automatically a judicial finding; a quotation from an earlier case is not a new fact about this petitioner. The document's structure tells us who is speaking and what authority the statement carries.

For the present chapter, the useful proposition is narrow: procedural guidance and extinction of an inherited right are not interchangeable. Do not use the case to declare that every possible Scottish succession dispute is settled or that recording never matters. The court's reasoning concerns a defined petition and evidence. A sound comparative note identifies the paragraph and explains the distinction without converting a single judgment into a universal online succession calculator.

The case also demonstrates how property, name, family representation, and arms can interact without being one thing. A land transaction, a testamentary provision, a genealogical link, and a matriculation can each supply different premises. The court evaluates their relation. A purchaser's possession of a named estate cannot simply be treated as a purchase of every historic armorial dignity associated with it. The actual instrument and governing reasoning are what need study.

## A border is an observation before it is an interpretation

Suppose two shields share the same field and main charge, but one adds an engrailed border. The safe first account describes the difference. A cadency interpretation then asks whether the bearers are related, whether the system uses that modification in a defined way, and whether the record assigns it that function. The border can also be an original constituent feature or another alteration. Similarity alone cannot choose the explanation.

The same method applies to a changed tincture, substituted object, added canton, or modified animal. Record exactly what changes and what remains. If the description says an animal's tongue changes color but the photograph is too small to show it, the record and observation have different precision. Do not announce that the images are identical simply because the visible difference is below the image's resolution.

Now introduce chronology. Coat B first appears after coat A in the surviving records, but a later first surviving witness does not necessarily establish a later invention. There may be missing earlier records. A contemporary grant defining B as a difference would strengthen the direction of the relationship. A late catalogue saying the designs are related supplies another kind of evidence. Date the statement as well as the depicted use.

For a corpus, distinguish observed similarity from documented derivation. A cluster of related-looking shields can be a useful research result without a complete genealogical explanation. Label it a visual cluster and identify the missing evidence. A database that calls every cluster a family will manufacture relationships faster than any researcher can verify them.

## Augmentation, alteration, and error need different histories

An augmentation is a documented addition or modification associated with honor in its particular framework. Cadency differentiates related bearings. An artistic or copying error can also alter a design without creating either. These categories may produce comparable visual effects—an added figure, changed tincture, or new feature—but their causes differ.[^difference] Establishing the cause requires the relevant act or a source that explains it.

Consider three fictional shields that add a small gold star. In the first, a grant records it as an honor. In the second, a family record assigns it as a child's difference. In the third, a modern illustrator accidentally copies a decorative star from the page margin into the shield. The observed addition is the same kind of shape. The documentary histories are not. A visual classifier cannot decide the category without another layer of evidence.

The distinction also controls reproduction. If a bearer received an augmentation at a defined date, an earlier dated depiction may correctly lack it. A later commemorative depiction may show it retrospectively. A teaching redraw should specify which historical state it represents. Automatically replacing every earlier image with the fullest later coat would erase the sequence that explains the change.

An error is still historical evidence of the erroneous depiction. Preserve it in a witness description rather than silently correcting the photograph's blazon. A normalized reconstruction can show the intended design with an editorial explanation. The two layers let readers study both armorial identity and the history of its reproduction.

## Genealogical proof has an architecture

A descent claim depends on links between identifiable people. Each link needs evidence of identity and relationship. Two records with the same name may concern different individuals. A birth record may identify parents but not connect the child securely to the adult named in a later grant. A marriage, residence, occupation, probate record, or other source can help bridge that identity question. The exact needs follow the records, not a fixed formula of one certificate per generation.

In a constructed family, two cousins share a common given name and live near one another. A later pedigree chooses one as the ancestor but provides no citation. Before discussing arms, establish which person is connected to the next generation. A flawless heraldic interpretation built on the wrong cousin remains wrong. The visible shield cannot rescue the missing identity link merely because it agrees with the family story.

Negative evidence needs a defined search. “No older brother found” is weaker than a documented examination of relevant birth, baptism, marriage, death, and probate sources with their coverage limits. A missing record could reflect loss, migration, indexing, or an actual absence. If a transmission condition depends on the absence of another line, identify what evidence supports that condition and what uncertainty remains. Do not treat a search box returning zero results as a genealogical event.

The conclusion should be conditional where a necessary premise remains unresolved. “If these two records concern the same person, the documented descent reaches the grantee” accurately locates the uncertainty. It is more useful than either confident entitlement or a vague statement that the family is complicated. A reader can now see which discovery would strengthen or overturn the argument.

## Names change; armorial relationships need another source

A surname can change through marriage, adoption, an institutional act, deliberate choice, or changing documentary practice. A shared surname does not prove descent; a changed surname does not automatically disprove it. The genealogy needs records connecting identities, and the transmission analysis needs the relevant instrument's conditions. Name continuity and armorial continuity should be tested separately.

The Bonshaw judgment is a defined example involving name and armorial succession within its findings.[^bonshaw] The lesson is to follow the court's evidence and reasoning rather than detach one statement and use it for every modern name change. The source connects particular acts and persons. A general analogy can suggest what to investigate but cannot decide another case.

For an institutional bearer, the problem shifts again. A university's name change can lead to confirmation of its emblems without a new family generation. A merger can create a different legal person. Those events are not cadency merely because related designs continue. The public-life chapter examines corporate continuity. Here the distinction prevents a kinship vocabulary from being applied automatically to institutions.

When cataloguing names, retain the source form, normalized identifier, and evidence connecting variants. Do not overwrite a historical spelling because a modern form is easier to search. The ability to return from the normalized record to the original is essential when two apparently identical people later turn out to be distinct.

## Similar arms can express affiliation without proving common ancestry

Heraldry includes armorial groupings and institutional affiliations whose relationships are not reducible to a biological tree. A shared motif can express service, office, political connection, or a tradition of related design. The Scottish institutional guide itself notes similarity among shields of unrelated same-surname bearers in its described setting.[^scottish-usage] That statement should make a researcher cautious about deriving a pedigree from visual resemblance alone.

The broader Polish–Lithuanian comparison is developed in the [regional study](/traditions/poland-lithuania/), which distinguishes a named **herb** from individual bearers and documentary genealogy. Use it as a path into that literature, not as a shortcut that lets any reader adopt a coat from a surname index. A source-specific grouping is a historical relationship to investigate. The word clan does not mean exactly the same thing in every tradition using the translation.

For a digital project, provide distinct link types: descent, documented grant, matriculation, stated affiliation, shared named arms, and visual resemblance. These are not merely technical labels. They preserve the historical propositions the dataset asserts. If every edge is called family, an uncertain resemblance can acquire the appearance of a proven genealogy when displayed in a tree.

A good explanatory paragraph can therefore say that two arms are compositionally related, that the source identifies a particular affiliation, and that common ancestry remains unestablished. Those statements are compatible. The result is useful because it identifies exactly which relation is known.

## Reading a cadency chart critically

Before using a chart, identify its author, date, institution, source basis, and intended use. A historical manual might be describing a convention, recommending a scheme, criticizing actual practice, or reproducing a theoretical table. The heading alone will not decide which. Read the surrounding prose. A neat diagram can be more portable than its qualifications, which is precisely why isolated copies cause trouble.

Test the chart against a small set of dated records. Does it predict the observed designs? Where it does not, does the instrument explain a personalized difference or another exception? Is the visual discrepancy caused by a crop or restoration? Does the chart address the same kind of bearer? A failure can reveal the chart's scope rather than invalidate the entire practice.

An exceptional record should also remain exceptional unless a broader pattern supports generalization. One grant's heart, substituted mortar, or border does not establish a mandatory national sequence. Conversely, a general convention does not erase the particular grant's provision. A serious reference presents both levels and explains how they relate.

This is a productive research stance because it turns the difficult case into evidence. The student learns to compare prescription, implementation, and individual act. That method applies to legal history far beyond heraldry, but heraldry gives us unusually visible results to examine.

## Research project: build a transmission dossier

Choose a documented original coat and one recorded related version. Assemble the instruments or institutional entries, the genealogical relationship stated or independently supported, the design difference, and the dates of acts and depictions. Keep the observed difference separate from the source's assigned function. State whether the study concerns actual use, prescribed succession, or a proposed entitlement.

Produce two diagrams: a people-and-relationships graph and an acts-and-designs graph. Link them only where evidence permits. Each significant connection should have a source and a precise proposition. If the original grant has not been inspected, say that the study relies on its institutional summary. If a pedigree comes from the armorial instrument rather than independent civil records, identify that origin.

Conclude by explaining the mechanism that created the related design and one remaining uncertainty. The project can demonstrate cadency in a defined case without claiming to certify every modern descendant. Its value is the transparent connection between people, rules, records, and visible differences.

## Workshop and answer discussion

Compare two documented related coats in a defined jurisdiction. Write a visual difference inventory before consulting any family explanation. Then compare your inventory with the register or scholarly account. State whether the source itself assigns the difference a familial function.

It should identify both witnesses and their dates, preserve the exact changes, and cite the evidence for the relationship. It should distinguish a convention from demonstrated use and a general rule from a particular record. It should not assign birth order solely from an ordinary charge that happens to appear in a cadency chart. An unresolved conclusion can earn full credit if the missing evidence is correctly identified.
