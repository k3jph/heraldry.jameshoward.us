# The Heraldry Project — editorial mandate

**Adopted 2026-10-08. Status: ACTIVE, OPEN-ENDED.**

## Governing ambition

Build the most complete, accurate, accessible, multilingual, research-grade digital education in heraldry that we can responsibly produce. The work must reach **genuine undergraduate, graduate, and independent-research depth**. It must not stop at English practice, family-crest misconceptions, and an introductory tincture rule.

**The standing direction is to keep expanding until the scholarly target is met, and to keep updating when new evidence appears.** The field cannot literally be exhausted: the target is sustained intellectual coverage, not an arbitrary word count. The project is **never to be described as complete** merely because a homepage, twelve lessons, a visualizer, or a language index exists.

## Nonnegotiable standards

1. **Global, comparative, and polyglot by default.** Cover British and Irish traditions without allowing them to define the rest of the world. Build substantive courses for French, German-speaking, Low Countries, Iberian, Italian, Nordic, Baltic, Polish–Lithuanian, Central and Eastern European, Balkan, colonial/postcolonial, African, North and South American heraldry, and the institutions where each has its own rules. Comparative emblem systems such as Japanese `家紋` (kamon) receive serious study without falsely collapsing them into European armory.
2. **Use originals.** Prioritize primary archival catalogs, statutory texts, national heraldic bodies, registers, original seals, armorial manuscripts, formal grants and historical scholarship in the source language. Secondary syntheses and English translations do not replace primary documentation.
3. **Translate responsibly.** Preserve original title, original term, language, edition, page/folio/section, permanent URL and date consulted. English notes must distinguish **original editorial synthesis**, **summary translated from the source**, **short attributed quotations**, and **full licensed/public-domain translations**. Do not publish extensive translations of copyrighted contemporary material without permission; publish original English syntheses with links instead.
4. **Do not flatten disagreement.** Describe variation within jurisdictions, across eras, between noble and civic arms, and between descriptive art conventions and enforceable law. Treat claims of descent, title, registration and legal entitlement individually.
5. **Historically significant illustrations.** The current original SVG examples are teaching aids. Build a separate collection of authentic documented arms, with dates, provenance, folio references and appropriate image licences. Never silently conflate these collections.
6. **Teach students to do original research.** Require translation comparison, source criticism, paleography, blazon transcription, heraldic art analysis, archival work, documentary genealogy and capstones that could grow into publishable scholarship.
7. **Evidence for each factual claim.** Prefer at least one traceable primary citation per major proposition, and more where the matter is disputed. Identify unresolved claims and translation uncertainty.
8. **Respect source rights and people.** Track reproduction rights separately from the historic age of the depicted arms; protect personal data in contemporary registers. Use no unsourced heraldic art from commercial sites.
9. **Public access first.** Keep the site fast, usable on mobile, accessible to keyboard and assistive technologies, freely usable and published on GitHub Pages.
10. **Editorial voice.** Clear, learned, explanatory, playful when appropriate, and never patronizing. Complexities should be made understandable rather than suppressed.

## University-level acceptance standard

For each national or regional teaching cluster:

- Introduction with historical periodization and primary bibliography.
- Institutional and legal history with dated original evidence.
- Vocabulary concordance retaining native terms and careful English equivalents.
- Analysis of **actual dated objects** rather than exclusively hypothetical arms.
- Several worked primary-source readings or translations with source provenance.
- Formal composition, language and local artistic differences.
- Exercises combining visual reading, original research and written argument.
- Evaluated undergraduate comprehension and graduate-level source criticism.
- Documented open questions, bibliographic gaps, and live review status.

For the graduate track, students should be able to **formulate a research question, assemble a multilingual corpus, distinguish manuscript witnesses and editions, make an original documented translation, and defend a historical/legal interpretation**.

## Scope architecture

- **Foundation**: revise and expand existing 12 units into genuinely substantial, footnoted lessons.
- **Comparative traditions**: regional monographs, available now through the new Traditions area, progressively expanded.
- **Research sourcebook**: original-language catalog and carefully distinguished English notes.
- **Research seminar**: source criticism, paleography, cataloguing, comparative law, digital humanities, material history, and publishable capstones.
- **Primary armorial museum**: historically real artifacts with rights-cleared imagery and metadata.
- **Tools**: open SVG studio, trustworthy blazon grammar, visual reading and source comparison, multilingual search and historical period timelines.

## Publication quality gates

No fabricated quotations, archive shelfmarks, blazons or translations. Do not present machine-produced interpretation as a reviewed expert translation. Each published entry states source language, authority, provenance, and confidence. Research notes can be flagged as provisional while still useful.

The original 12-unit introduction remains online but **must be labelled an introductory foundation**, not an accredited degree or a finished university curriculum. Automated technical tests are necessary, not sufficient, for scholarly publication.

This mandate is a persistent **project goal**, not a claim that software can operate indefinitely without an active work session or explicitly scheduled task. Continue work on future sessions from this charter and queue; update their states as evidence accumulates.
